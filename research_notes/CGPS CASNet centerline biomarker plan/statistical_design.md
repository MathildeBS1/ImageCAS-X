# Statistical Design for CGPS Centerline/Tortuosity Biomarker Association Study

## Key Question 1: Regression modeling for a continuous imaging biomarker with multiple vessels per patient

### Takeaway
The standard approach for a continuous per-vessel outcome measured repeatedly within patients is a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) with fixed effects for the covariates of interest (age, sex, vessel identity/type) and at minimum a random intercept per patient; a naive per-vessel OLS ignores the correlation between vessels from the same patient, which understates standard errors and inflates Type I error.

### Cited Findings
- Linear mixed models are the standard tool "whenever your data have grouping, clustering, or repeated measures that violate the assumption of independence required by a standard linear model," extending ordinary linear models by adding random effects alongside fixed effects, where fixed effects describe population-level relationships and random effects absorb the grouping structure — [Coursera: What Are Linear Mixed Effects Models?](https://www.coursera.org/articles/linear-mixed-effects-models)
- Mixed models can accommodate multiple nested clustering levels (e.g., repeated observations within patients within a higher-level unit), and can handle unbalanced designs (unequal numbers of vessels per patient, missing vessels) without discarding subjects, unlike repeated-measures ANOVA — [Trials: mixed model for repeated measures simulation study](https://trialsjournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13063-020-4114-9); [bookdown: Linear mixed models for more than two measurements](https://bookdown.org/pingapang9/linear_models_bookdown/premidpost.html)
- In an applied vascular-geometry example (endovascular navigation study), a mixed-effects linear regression was used specifically to relate vascular geometric features to outcomes while including random effects to account for variability *between vascular trees*, with fixed effects estimated at the population level — directly analogous to a per-vessel-within-patient design — [Vascular Geometry Characterization for AI-Based Endovascular Navigation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.09130)
- Mixed models for repeated measurements are described as the standard modern alternative to repeated-measures ANOVA/MANOVA specifically because they properly model the within-subject correlation structure rather than assuming independence — [Mixed models for repetitive measurements: the basics (Fertility and Sterility)](https://www.fertstert.org/article/S0015-0282(24)00237-1/pdf)

### Inferences
- General model form for this design (one row per vessel per patient):

  y_ij = β0 + β1·age_i + β2·sex_i + β3·vesselType_ij + β4·(age_i × sex_i) [optional] + u_i + ε_ij

  where i indexes patient, j indexes vessel within patient, y_ij is the tortuosity/geometry outcome for vessel j of patient i, β's are fixed effects, u_i ~ N(0, τ²) is the patient-level random intercept capturing unobserved patient-level factors shared by all of a patient's vessels (overall vascular "tortuosity tendency," global calcification burden, image quality of that scan, etc.), and ε_ij ~ N(0, σ²) is the vessel-level residual, with u_i and ε_ij independent. If vessel type has a materially different age/sex effect (e.g. RCA tortuosity behaves differently from LAD), a random slope for vessel type per patient, or simply fitting vessel type as a fixed effect with a patient random intercept, is the standard extension; a full random-slope model (u_i for both intercept and vessel-type effect, with their covariance) is justified only if there are enough vessels per patient to estimate it (typically ≥3–4 vessels/patient here: LAD, LCx, RCA, +/- diagonal/OM branches).
- Why naive per-vessel OLS is wrong, in plain terms: OLS treats every row (vessel) as an independent draw, but vessels from the same patient share the same age, same sex, same scanner, same overall disease burden and same image quality — so their residuals are correlated, not independent. This correlation means the *effective* number of independent data points is smaller than the number of vessel-rows. OLS's standard errors are computed as if the sample size equals the number of rows, so they are too small; this pushes p-values down and inflates the false-positive (Type I error) rate for the age/sex association test. The mixed model's random intercept explicitly absorbs the shared within-patient component, so the fixed-effect standard errors for age/sex are correctly widened to reflect the true number of independent patients driving that estimate — this is the general rationale given across the clustered/repeated-measures LMM sources above, not a claim specific to any one paper.
- The patient-level random intercept variance τ² and residual variance σ² also directly give the intraclass correlation (ICC = τ²/(τ²+σ²)), which is worth reporting in the thesis as a diagnostic: a near-zero ICC would suggest clustering barely matters here, while a substantial ICC (loosely, >0.1–0.2) confirms the naive-OLS concern is real for this dataset.

### Gaps
- None of the sources found gave a coronary-tortuosity-specific worked LMM equation with vessel as the repeated unit; the general LMM rationale is standard biostatistics and directly transfers, but a coronary-tortuosity-specific published LMM was not located in this pass.

## Key Question 2: Handling sex, age (possibly nonlinear), and standard covariate adjustments (BSA, dominance)

### Takeaway
Sex enters as a binary/categorical fixed effect (dummy-coded), age as a continuous fixed effect that should be checked for nonlinearity (e.g. via a quadratic term or spline) rather than assumed linear, and the coronary-geometry literature commonly adjusts absolute size measures for body surface area (BSA) and for coronary dominance pattern, since both materially affect raw vessel caliber/length independent of the physiologic effect being tested.

### Cited Findings
- When coronary artery diameters are indexed to BSA, the male/female difference in size becomes statistically non-significant, whereas the unadjusted (absolute) diameter difference is significant (women's epicardial diameter is about 9% smaller than men's before BSA adjustment) — [Examining the Influence of Gender, Age, and Dominance on the Caliber of Normal Coronary Arteries in the South Indian Population, PMC10811496](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10811496/); consistent with the classic normative reference — [Lumen Diameter of Normal Human Coronary Arteries (Circulation, 1992)](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.cir.86.1.232)
- Coronary dominance pattern (right- vs left- vs co-dominant) significantly affects proximal RCA and LCx diameter (but not LAD diameter), and affects the diametric ratio of coronary arteries to BSA in children — [Change of coronary artery indices according to coronary dominance pattern in early childhood, PMC6584238](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6584238/)
- Branch-artery caliber is not affected by anatomic dominance directly, but increases with branch length expressed as a fraction of the origin-to-apex distance — a normalization-by-relative-position approach reported in the same normative coronary-diameter literature — [Lumen Diameter of Normal Human Coronary Arteries](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.cir.86.1.232)
- In the large-scale (38,691 angiogram, 22,334 patient) machine-learning RCA tortuosity study, after adjustment for other risk factors, age was *not* independently associated with tortuosity, but sex, hypertension, diabetes, and presence/severity of coronary artery disease were — indicating that in a properly adjusted multivariable model, the age effect on tortuosity specifically can disappear once other correlated risk factors are included, while the sex effect persists — [A Machine Learning Driven Approach to Quantifying Coronary Artery Tortuosity, PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/) (finding drawn from search-result summary; the PMC full text was not directly retrievable due to a bot-check page blocking fetch, so treat the exact adjustment set and effect direction as needing re-verification against the primary text before citing a specific number in the thesis)
- Separately, sex differences in tortuosity prevalence are reported as large and simple (unadjusted) in another cohort: 45.2% of women vs 19.7% of men had tortuosity, with tortuosity increasing with age in women from around age 45 onward — [Gender differences in the prevalence of coronary artery tortuosity and its association with coronary artery disease, PubMed 28616559](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28616559/) (this is a single-study prevalence/percentage finding, not a regression coefficient; full text was not accessible in this pass to confirm the statistical test used, so treat the specific percentages as a reported finding to be verified, not a settled meta-analytic figure)

### Inferences
- Extended fixed-effects specification building on Q1's model:

  y_ij = β0 + β1·age_i + β2·age_i² + β3·sex_i + β4·vesselType_ij + β5·BSA_i + β6·dominance_i + u_i + ε_ij

  - Sex: single dummy variable (e.g. female = 1); if the sex effect is suspected to depend on age (the literature above suggests women's tortuosity rises specifically from ~45 onward), an age×sex interaction term (β·age_i·sex_i) is the standard way to test that rather than stratifying, since stratifying loses power and discards the shared residual/variance information.
  - Age: do not assume linearity by default. Standard practice is to first fit a flexible term (quadratic, or a restricted cubic spline with 3-4 knots) and test whether it improves fit (e.g. likelihood-ratio test vs the linear-only model) before deciding to keep the model linear-in-age; this is generic regression modeling practice, not tortuosity-specific, but directly applicable.
  - BSA: needed only if the outcome is an absolute-scale geometric quantity like vessel length or diameter, where body size confounds patient size; a unitless tortuosity ratio (arc/chord) is less obviously BSA-dependent, but total vessel length and the denominator of some indices are, so BSA (computed from height/weight via a standard formula, e.g. Du Bois) is a reasonable covariate to include or to check by sensitivity analysis.
  - Dominance: because dominance changes which vessel (RCA vs LCx) is expected to be long/tortuous by anatomy alone, and CGPS will have per-vessel labels, dominance should be included at minimum as an effect on the RCA/LCx-specific geometry, either as a covariate or as a stratified/interaction term with vessel type.

### Gaps
- No source specifically validated BSA-adjustment or dominance-adjustment for a *tortuosity index* (a ratio, which is already partly scale-invariant) as opposed to absolute diameter/length; the BSA/dominance evidence found is for absolute coronary caliber, and its direct applicability to a dimensionless tortuosity ratio should be treated as an inference, not a directly cited fact, and is worth a brief sensitivity check (with vs without BSA) in the thesis rather than an assumed necessity.
- The exact multivariable adjustment set and coefficients in the 38,691-patient ML tortuosity study (PMC13308244) could not be directly verified from primary text in this pass (bot-check blocked full-text fetch); if this paper is to be cited with specific numbers in the thesis, it should be re-fetched or read via a proxy/institutional access and verified (candidate for the `paper-review` skill).

## Key Question 3: Sanity-checking a segmentation/geometry pipeline OOD with no ground truth

### Takeaway
The literature converges on three complementary strategies for QC without ground truth: (a) proxy/plausibility QC — either simple bound-checking or learned quality-prediction methods such as Reverse Classification Accuracy, validated against a small manually-reviewed reference set — (b) inference-time uncertainty via test-time augmentation (TTA) variance, usable without retraining and reported as at least as informative as MC-dropout for aleatoric-type uncertainty, and (c) explicit awareness that domain shift (scanner, protocol, contrast/reconstruction, population) is the dominant reported failure mode for CT segmentation models moved to a new site, with image-quality proxies (contrast enhancement, edge sharpness) correlating with the resulting accuracy drop.

### Cited Findings
**(a) Proxy/plausibility QC**
- Reverse Classification Accuracy (RCA) is a documented method for per-case segmentation quality estimation with no ground truth at deployment: it trains a one-off classifier from the single test image + its own predicted segmentation as pseudo-ground-truth, then has that classifier re-segment a small (order-of-100 image) manually-verified reference set; if the classifier can segment at least one reference image well, the original predicted segmentation is inferred to be good. Applied to UK Biobank cardiac MRI, RCA achieved 99% accuracy distinguishing good vs poor segmentations (DSC threshold 0.7-1.0) with 97.1% true-positive rate for poor cases in a 400-case experiment, 95% accuracy / 75.2% TPR in a larger 4,805-image UKBB run, and predicted-vs-real quality metrics correlated at r ∈ [0.95, 0.99] — [Automated quality control in image segmentation: application to the UK Biobank cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging study, PMC6416857](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6416857/)
- More broadly, "once segmentation methods are deployed in clinical practice, routine quantitative evaluation cannot be carried out; evaluating per-case quality is crucial to identify cases where segmentation has failed," and simple plausibility checkers comparing metrics (e.g. mean gradient, size bounds) against pre-set thresholds are used as first-line automated flags before any manual review — [Downstream-Aware Automated QC of Images and AI-Generated Segmentations, Springer JDI](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02156-y)

**(b) Uncertainty quantification usable without retraining**
- TTA is explicitly framed in the literature as a "free lunch" post-hoc technique: "given a test image, multiple augmented copies are generated, passed through the trained model, and the resulting predictions are aggregated," and it is "model-agnostic and can be applied to pretrained systems" without retraining — directly applicable to a frozen CAS-Net deployed on CGPS — [Uncertainty Quantification in Medical Image Segmentation: A Comprehensive Survey, PMC13514988](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13514988/)
- TTA-based aleatoric uncertainty (variance of predictions across augmented/transformed copies of the same input) was reported to give better uncertainty estimation than test-time-dropout-based (MC dropout) model uncertainty alone in one comparative study — [Aleatoric uncertainty estimation with test-time augmentation for medical image segmentation with CNNs (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925231219301961) (full PDF could not be parsed as text in this pass — binary/compressed content — so treat this as the abstract-level claim only; recommend a direct read via `paper-review` before citing specific numbers)
- A caveat worth flagging: a more recent paper argues TTA can actually *hurt* medical image classification performance in some settings ("I Can't Believe TTA Is Not Better"), so TTA-derived uncertainty should be validated on CGPS itself (e.g. does TTA variance actually track visually-assessed failure cases) rather than assumed reliable by default — [I Can't Believe TTA Is Not Better: When Test-Time Augmentation Hurts Medical Image Classification, arXiv 2604.09697](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09697v1)
- Standard UQ method families for pretrained-model deployment (no retraining) also include deep ensembles (if multiple independently-trained CAS-Net checkpoints exist) and Bayesian/MC-dropout methods (only if the architecture has dropout layers), with the trade-off that ensembles/Bayesian methods better capture *epistemic* uncertainty (model's lack of knowledge on OOD data) at higher compute cost, while TTA is cheaper but more aleatoric-flavored (sensitive to input perturbation, not necessarily to true OOD-ness) — [PMC13514988 survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13514988/)

**(c) Known pitfalls of cross-cohort CNN segmentation deployment**
- In a coronary-CT-specific generalization study, a model trained on one cohort (ASOCA) dropped from ~0.90 to ~0.80 Dice when applied to a different cohort (GeoCAD), with the largest gap in low-calcification cases (0.86→0.79); artery contrast enhancement correlated positively with Dice (r=0.408, p<0.001) and edge sharpness correlated positively with Dice (r=0.239, p=0.046), and the authors attributed the shift to scanner/protocol differences (retrospective vs prospective ECG-gating) — [Optimising Generalisable Deep Learning Models for CT Coronary Segmentation: A Multifactorial Evaluation, PMC13230296](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13230296/)
- Systematic OOD testing is reported to be rare in cardiac deep learning generally: "systematic out-of-distribution testing, adversarial validation and explicit outlier-detection frameworks were seldom reported," and most pipelines are trained/evaluated on curated data with limited representation of motion artefact, low contrast, implants, or rare pathology — meaning a thesis that *does* attempt OOD QC on CGPS is addressing a documented, acknowledged gap in the field, not reinventing a solved problem — [Deep learning for cardiac MRI: performance evidence and barriers to clinical integration, PMC13007597](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13007597/); congenital-heart-disease-specific confirmation of the same generalization gap — [Beyond In-Distribution Metrics: A Systematic Out-of-Distribution Evaluation of Congenital Heart Disease Segmentation, arXiv 2609.17068](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17068)
- A distinct but related failure mode is "silent failure" — the model produces a confident-looking but wrong segmentation with no obvious visual red flag — addressed in the OOD detection literature via distance-based methods comparing test-time feature representations to the training distribution — [Distance-based detection of out-of-distribution silent failures for Covid-19 lung lesion segmentation, arXiv 2208.03217](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.03217)

### Inferences
- A concrete, minimal QC protocol assembled from the above for the CGPS deployment: (1) compute simple plausibility bounds per case (per-vessel length range, expected vessel count per patient, expected topology e.g. no disconnected fragments above a small size) and flag outliers automatically, in the spirit of the plausibility-checker approach; (2) run TTA (the mirroring/flip TTA the CAS-Net inference pipeline already performs at 4x over X,Y per the walkthrough) and record the prediction variance per voxel/per-vessel as an uncertainty proxy, correlating it post-hoc with a small clinician-reviewed spot-check sample rather than assuming it is meaningful a priori (per the TTA-can-hurt caveat); (3) quantify the image-domain shift between ImageCAS-X training data and CGPS directly and quantitatively (e.g. compare intensity histograms in the vessel ROI, slice thickness, reconstruction kernel, contrast timing/HU in the aorta) since contrast enhancement and edge sharpness are the two image-quality proxies specifically shown to correlate with coronary-CT segmentation accuracy drop in the ASOCA→GeoCAD study; (4) draw a random clinician-reviewed subsample (the exact size is a design choice, not something the sources specify for this task — see Gaps) stratified by the automated QC flags from steps 1-3, so the manual review budget is spent disproportionately on cases the automated signals already suspect, mirroring the RCA-style validate-a-cheap-proxy-against-a-manual-reference-set approach.
- Because the sources show that domain shift severity is well predicted by simple image statistics (contrast, sharpness) rather than requiring a full learned pipeline, a lightweight first pass, distribution comparison of HU/contrast/slice-thickness between ImageCAS-X and CGPS, is a defensible and citable QC step even before any per-case learned quality score is built.

### Gaps
- No source gave a validated, coronary-vessel-specific plausibility-bound protocol (e.g. "flag if RCA length <X mm or >Y mm"); such bounds would need to be derived from the ImageCAS-X training cohort's own distribution as a reference range, which is a design decision internal to this thesis rather than a literature-sourced number.
- RCA (Reverse Classification Accuracy) as validated in PMC6416857 requires a small manually-labeled reference set from the *target* domain to calibrate against; whether CGPS has (or the thesis budget allows creating) even a handful of manually-segmented cases for this purpose was not addressed by the sources and should be treated as an open design question, not resolved here.
- The TTA-uncertainty aleatoric paper's numeric comparison against MC-dropout could not be verified from primary text (PDF was binary/unparseable in this fetch); recommend re-reading via `paper-review` before quoting specific numbers.

## Key Question 4: Sample size / power for detecting age or sex effects on tortuosity

### Takeaway
No off-the-shelf closed-form power formula for a coronary-tortuosity LMM was found in the literature; the standard modern practice is simulation-based power analysis (e.g. via the `simr` R package) built on a pilot/plausible effect size and the estimated variance components (patient-level vs vessel-level vs residual), and one directly relevant reported effect size is a large unadjusted sex-prevalence difference (45.2% vs 19.7%) from a single tortuosity study, while a much larger machine-learning study found no independent age effect once other risk factors were adjusted for.

### Cited Findings
- General statistical statement: "despite the popularity of the general linear mixed model for data analysis, power and sample size methods and software are not generally available for commonly used test statistics and reference distributions," so in practice, "statisticians often resort to simulations… or rough approximations" — [Power and Sample Size for Fixed-Effects Inference in Reversible Linear Mixed Models, PMC7009022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7009022/)
- One analytic route that does exist for clustered/repeated designs: recognizing the LMM Wald test's non-central distribution as equivalent to the (well-studied) multivariate Hotelling-Lawley test, so multivariate power/sample-size results carry over to the mixed-model case — [same source, PMC7009022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7009022/)
- Practical, code-supported alternative: simulation-based power analysis, fitting a model to pilot/plausible data and simulating draws under a range of assumed effect sizes and sample sizes to estimate empirical power — [Estimating power in (generalized) linear mixed models: an open introduction and tutorial in R, PMC8613146](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8613146/); worked example with the `simr` package — [Power Analysis with simr](https://humburg.github.io/Power-Analysis/simr_power_analysis.html)
- A concrete effect-size decomposition formula given in an applied mixed-model power tutorial: d = (difference between condition means) / sqrt(sum of all variance components), where the variance components are partitioned across participant-intercept, item-intercept, participant-slope, item-slope and residual variance; the tutorial's authors also recommend, in their (psycholinguistic reaction-time) domain, "a minimum of 1,600 observations" flexibly distributed across participants and items to achieve adequate power for small effects (d≈0.4 or smaller) — [Power Analysis and Effect Size in Mixed Effects Models: A Tutorial, Journal of Cognition](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.10). This numeric recommendation is domain-specific (psycholinguistics) and should not be transplanted as a target number for the tortuosity study; it is included here only to illustrate the general variance-partitioning logic, which does transfer.
- Reported coronary-tortuosity effect-size-adjacent findings from Key Question 2 that bear directly on power planning: a large, simple sex-prevalence gap (45.2% women vs 19.7% men with tortuosity) in one cohort — [PubMed 28616559](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28616559/) — versus, in a much larger (22,334-patient) multivariable-adjusted analysis, no independent age effect after adjustment, with sex, hypertension and diabetes remaining associated — [PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/). The contrast between these two is itself informative for power planning: the smaller/simpler analysis reports a large raw sex effect, while the much larger adjusted analysis found age's effect explained away by confounders — suggesting that (a) a raw/unadjusted sex effect on coronary tortuosity, if real, may be large enough to detect with a comparatively modest CGPS sample, but (b) an independent age effect (after adjusting for correlated risk factors) may require a substantially larger sample than a naive power calculation using only the raw age-tortuosity correlation would suggest.

### Inferences
- General power-analysis sketch for this design (two-group or continuous-covariate test in a random-intercept LMM), following the structure implied by the sources: total variance = τ² (between-patient) + σ² (within-patient/vessel-level). For a simple two-group (sex) comparison of a per-vessel mean, the effective design effect from clustering is roughly 1 + (m-1)·ICC, where m is the average number of vessels per patient and ICC = τ²/(τ²+σ²); required sample size scales up from the naive independent-observations formula by this design effect. In practice, given the analytic-formula scarcity noted above, the recommended path for the thesis is: (1) fit the LMM on a pilot slice or on the training cohort's own analogous measurement (if available) to get realistic τ² and σ² estimates, (2) plug those into a simulation-based tool (`simr` in R, or `statsmodels`/`lme4`-equivalent simulation in Python) to get an empirical power curve as a function of CGPS's actual planned N, rather than relying on a textbook closed-form formula that the literature says doesn't really exist for this design.
- Because the CGPS cohort size is presumably fixed (not a variable the thesis controls), the practically useful framing is not "how many patients do I need" but "what is the minimum detectable effect (MDE) at 80% power given the CGPS N actually available" — this should be computed and reported (via simulation) rather than a generic sample-size target, and stated explicitly as a limitation if the achievable N only powers detection of a large effect (e.g. only powered to detect an effect on the scale of the 45.2%-vs-19.7% type finding, not powered for the smaller/adjusted age effect).

### Gaps
- No coronary-tortuosity-specific published power analysis or a directly reusable numeric effect size in "Cohen's d" or standardized-mean-difference terms for age or sex was found; the 45.2%/19.7% figure is a prevalence contrast (categorical tortuosity present/absent), not directly convertible to an effect size for a continuous tortuosity index without further assumptions, and should be treated as illustrative context rather than a plug-in number.
- The exact variance components (τ², σ², ICC) for any coronary geometric measure repeated across vessels within patients were not found in the literature search; these will need to be estimated from CGPS pilot data or from the ImageCAS-X test cohort itself before a simulation-based power analysis can be run.

## Key Question 5: Effect of centerline smoothing/resolution on curvature-based tortuosity reproducibility

### Takeaway
The literature does document this concern directly: centerline point spacing/resolution has a much larger effect on tortuosity/bend-count results than smoothing choice does, with one comparative study finding detected bend counts varying from 3 to 33 depending on resolution alone, and curvature-based statistics reported to stabilize once inter-point spacing is under about 0.1 mm — supporting a "use a fine, consistent resampling resolution and don't over-smooth" recommendation, though the two largest illustrative numbers below traced to search-result summaries whose primary text could not be directly verified in this pass (both blocked by a bot-check page) and should be re-read directly before being quoted as thesis numbers.

### Cited Findings
- In a comparative study on automated landmarking of bends in vascular structures, "centerline smoothing had the least impact on results, while centerline resolution showed to completely over- and underestimate the number of bends, varying from 3 to 33" — [Automated landmarking of bends in vascular structures: a comparative study with application to the internal carotid artery, PMC8626959](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8626959/) (finding drawn from the search-engine's summary of this paper; direct full-text fetch was blocked by a bot-check page in two attempts, so the exact experimental conditions behind "3 to 33" should be confirmed by reading the paper directly, e.g. via the `paper-review` skill, before citing the specific numbers in the thesis)
- Separately, average curvature (AC) is reported to increase as the distance between centerline control points decreases, and to become stable once that distance is under approximately 0.1 mm, with the effect described as significant specifically for measurements of severe tortuosity — source: search-engine summary citing a hybrid vessel-morphology tortuosity classification study (title/URL for the specific claim was not individually isolated in the search output; treat as a reported finding requiring re-verification, likely from [A Hybrid Framework for Blood Vessel Morphology Classification, arXiv 2607.14195](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14195) or [Bridging local and global tortuosity of retinal vessels, PMC12331121](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12331121/), both returned by the same search and both about resolution-dependence of curvature metrics — this attribution is uncertain and flagged as a gap below)
- More generally, "obtained discrete spatial coordinates inevitably contain noise, and a standard Gaussian smoothing filter is applied... to reduce microscopic coordinate jitters and limit noise amplification before extraction of geometric invariants" is standard described practice, and best-fit polynomial smoothing (minimizing error between the polynomial and the original centerline) is one documented alternative — same search-engine summary, sources as above
- Downstream reproducibility concern: "applying algorithms to the same cohort revealed variability that makes comparison of results between previous studies questionable," motivating calls for objective, reproducible, and presumably resolution-standardized tortuosity quantification — same search summary, likely from the retinal-tortuosity reproducibility literature (exact source not individually isolated; flagged as a gap)

### Inferences
- The directly load-bearing, best-supported takeaway for the thesis is the resolution point, not the smoothing point: curvature-based descriptors are much more sensitive to how densely/finely the centerline is resampled before curvature is computed than to how heavily it is smoothed, so the thesis's methods section should report and fix a specific, consistent centerline resampling resolution across all CGPS cases (and ideally match it to whatever resolution was used, if any, for validation on ImageCAS-X ground truth centerlines) as the primary reproducibility control, with smoothing parameters treated as a secondary, lower-impact choice.
- Given CAS-Net inference on CGPS runs at a fixed 0.5mm isotropic geometry (per the ImageCAS-X pipeline's resampling step, if the same resampling is applied at inference for CGPS), the "under ~0.1mm control-point spacing for stable curvature" figure, if verified, would argue for resampling the extracted centerline to a finer point spacing than the native voxel spacing before computing curvature statistics, rather than using the raw centerline vertices as extracted from the voxel grid.

### Gaps
- The two most quantitatively specific claims above (bend count "3 to 33" and curvature stabilizing "under 0.1mm") both came through search-engine summaries rather than a verified primary-text read; PMC8626959 was blocked by a bot-check page on two direct fetch attempts, and the 0.1mm claim's exact source paper among the several returned by that search was not conclusively isolated. Both should be treated as leads to verify (via `paper-review` or a differently-routed fetch) rather than citable numbers, before they appear in the thesis with a specific figure attached.
- No source discussed the specific effect of centerline resolution/smoothing choices on a *tortuosity index (arc/chord ratio)* as distinct from curvature-based descriptors (SOAM, ICM) or bend-count metrics; arc/chord ratio is less sensitive to point-density in principle (it depends mainly on total arc length and endpoint positions, not local curvature), but this distinction was not explicitly confirmed in any source found and is inferred from the definitions of these metrics (Key Question 2 findings) rather than directly cited.
