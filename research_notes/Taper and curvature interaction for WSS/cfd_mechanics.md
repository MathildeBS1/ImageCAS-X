# Taper and curvature interaction for wall shear stress (WSS) in coronary arteries: CFD, idealized and analytical evidence

Scope note: sources were found with web search (about 20 tool calls). Several key full texts (Vorobtsova 2016 post-print, De Nisco 2019 atheroprotective helical flow) returned HTTP 403, so for those only the abstract-level claims are reported. Classic curved-pipe theory papers (Dean 1927/28, Berger et al. 1983) were not retrieved; the statements that rely on them are marked as inferences or gaps.

## Q1. Idealized curved tubes: how does adding taper change the inner-wall low-WSS region and Dean vortices? Any parametric studies varying both?

### Takeaway
I found no parametric CFD study that varies convergent/divergent taper and curvature together on a grid. The best idealized evidence (Johnston et al. 2009, S-shaped tapered coronary models) shows that the two effects largely **superpose**. Curvature sets *where* around the circumference and along the bends the local low-WSS points lie (inner wall of each bend). Taper without flow loss adds a monotone axial gradient: WSS rises as the radius falls distally. As a result the global WSS minimum moves upstream to the wide, straight proximal inlet. Taper changes the magnitude and the ranking of low-WSS sites, not the curvature-determined locations.

### Cited Findings
- Johnston et al. (MODSIM 2009) simulated pulsatile flow in rigid S-shaped coronary models (in-plane "s2d" and out-of-plane "s3d"), tapering evenly to an outlet radius of 78% of the inlet (inlet R = 2 mm, outlet 1.56 mm), with bend radius of curvature Rc = 3.5 cm and total length 16 cm. Inlet parameters: Re = 226, Womersley α = 2.6, Dean number Dn = 54, curvature ratio δ = R/Rc = 0.057 — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)
- In every model, during forward flow there is "high" WSS on the outside of each bend and "low" WSS on the inside. The pattern reverses during back flow, so high WSS moves to the inside — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)
- The positions of the points of consistently low WSS (low throughout the cycle) depended only on the planar geometry. They were not affected by the inlet waveform or by whether the second bend was in-plane or out-of-plane — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)
- Taper changes the magnitude: WSS increases as the radius decreases down the artery. The point of global minimum WSS therefore moves from a bend (C′) in uniform-radius models to the straight proximal inlet (X) in tapered models. The authors conclude that the proximal low-WSS region in realistic arteries is "due, in part, to the overall taper in the artery and not only to the local curvature variation", and that realistic coronary models need both taper and out-of-plane bends — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)
- At X (low WSS from taper), OSI was only moderate, whereas at the curvature-induced low-WSS points (C′, E), low WSS coincided with high OSI. Taper-driven low WSS and curvature-driven low WSS therefore differ in oscillatory character — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)
- In curved tubes, WSS around the outer half of the circumference can reach about twice the straight-tube value and falls toward the straight-tube level near the inner bend. A change in curvature (straight-to-bend transition) produces a localised shift in the WSS distribution — [Bernad et al., Proc. Romanian Academy 2014](https://acad.ro/sectii2002/proceedings/doc2014-1/07-Bernad.pdf)
- Vorobtsova et al. (2016) built idealized coronary models in which each vessel has segments of progressively increasing curvature and the diameter tapers uniformly along the length, together with patient-specific models. Perfusion pressure fell with increasing tortuosity. In patient-specific vessels, more tortuous vessels had higher WSS, which the authors attributed to helical flow (strong tortuosity–helicity correlation) — [Vorobtsova et al., Ann Biomed Eng 2016, via repository listing](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2770474); [post-print](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2770474/3/2016%20Vorobtsova%20Chiastra%20-%20Effects%20of%20vessel%20tortuosity%20on%20coronary%20hemodynamics%20POST-PRINT.pdf) (full text not accessible, 403)
- In idealized LM bifurcations (10 models), larger curvature moderately *increased* the area of low TAESS and *decreased* the areas of high OSI and RRT, and produced higher helicity intensity — [Shen et al. review, arXiv 2507.17109, Appendix Table I summarising idealized LM study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17109)
- In idealized coronary bifurcations, a smaller curvature radius was associated with higher helicity intensity in all cases — [Chiastra et al. 2017, J Biomech, repository PDF](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/handle/11583/2677968/158335/SSS_IdealizedCoronary_Chiastra_0119.pdf)
- Non-coronary rigid-wall idealized artery: as the taper angle increased (0.5°, 1°, 1.5°, convergent), both WSS and pressure drop increased, as did peak wall shear — [Univ. Baghdad J. Eng. study](https://joe.uobaghdad.edu.iq/index.php/main/article/view/2462)
- In curved pipes, raising curvature alone strengthens the Dean vortices and the cross-stream stresses. Relaminarisation needed a concurrent change in the cross-section, which shows that cross-sectional shape and curvature interact in setting the secondary flow (turbulent, non-arterial regime) — [arXiv 2511.17073](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.17073)

### Inferences
- Convergent taper with conserved flow raises the mean axial velocity (U ∝ Q/r²) and therefore Re ∝ U·r ∝ Q/r. Re rises distally while δ = r/Rc *falls* for a fixed Rc. The Dean number Dn = Re·√δ ∝ (Q/r)·√(r/Rc) = Q/(√r·√Rc), so a converging tube at fixed Q and Rc has a slowly *increasing* Dn distally. Its axial WSS baseline (∝ Q/r³) rises much faster. The circumferential inner/outer WSS asymmetry therefore sits on top of a rising baseline: low inner-wall WSS in a distal bend is "less low" in absolute terms than in an identical bend proximally. This explains Johnston's finding that the global minimum moves proximally. It is derived from the standard definitions (Dn = Re·√(R/Rc), as used by Johnston), not from a single source.
- Divergent taper (widening) does the opposite: it decelerates the core flow and creates an adverse pressure gradient that favours separation on the inner wall of a bend. Low WSS and curvature-driven low WSS would then *compound* on the inner wall. No coronary-specific CFD of a curved divergent segment was found to confirm this (see Gaps).
- Working model for the thesis: on the log-WSS scale, taper (via Q/r³) and curvature (via the circumferential Dean asymmetry) act approximately **multiplicatively**, that is additively on log WSS. Taper modulates the baseline level, and curvature redistributes WSS around the circumference at roughly fixed relative amplitude for moderate Dn. This is an inference from Johnston 2009 and the scaling above, not a tested result.

### Gaps
- No idealized parametric CFD study was found that crosses convergent vs divergent taper with curvature ratio (or Dn) and reports the inner-wall low-WSS area or Dean-vortex strength.
- Dean (1927/28) and Berger, Talbot & Yao (1983, Annu Rev Fluid Mech) were not retrieved. Analytical results for flow in curved pipes with slowly varying cross-section were not found.
- The Vorobtsova 2016 full text (with its taper angles and numbers) could not be accessed (403).

## Q2. Patient-specific coronary CFD: how much WSS variance do curvature, torsion, taper/diameter and their interaction explain? Which dominates?

### Takeaway
No patient-specific study was found that reports variance explained (R²) of WSS jointly by curvature, torsion and taper. The evidence is a set of univariate correlations in small samples (n = 3–39 vessels or trees, one study with 127 LM bifurcations), and the findings are inconsistent. Curvature and torsion act mainly *through* helical flow intensity, which correlates with higher TAWSS. Diameter is the most consistent single correlate of TAWSS (negative). Several groups report that curvature's effect depends on local diameter or calibre, which is an interaction, but none quantify it.

### Cited Findings
- 39 patient-specific left coronary trees from CTCA (ASOCA dataset; 20 non-stenosed, 19 stenosed). In stenosis-free segments, the absolute helicity intensity h₂ correlated positively with average TAESS in all segments (p < 0.05). In trees with stenosis elsewhere, h₂ correlated strongly and negatively with low-TAESS% in stenosis-free segments (LAD ρ = −0.86; LCx ρ = −0.89; Diagonal ρ = −0.80; Marginal ρ = −0.94). In fully non-stenosed trees, h₂ did *not* correlate with low-TAESS%, high-TAESS%, OSI% or RRT% — [Shen et al., arXiv 2502.06161 / R Soc Open Sci](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06161)
- Same study: among the geometric factors (average absolute curvature, diameter, torsion), the only significant correlation with TAESS in stenosis-free segments was diameter of the Diagonal, negatively with average TAESS (ρ = −0.85, p = 0.0046). Curvature and torsion did not correlate significantly with TAESS. LCx diameter (p = 0.041) and torsion (p = 0.024) were larger in stenosed than in non-stenosed trees — [Shen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.06161)
- 30 swine coronary arteries (RCA, LAD, LCx): vascular torsion dictates helical-flow features (r = 0.64, p < 0.001), and helicity intensity is inversely associated with the percentage of surface area under unfavourable (low/oscillatory) WSS — [De Nisco et al., Ann Biomed Eng 2019, summarised in search result and repository](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2726967)
- Patient-specific tortuous vessels had higher WSS, driven by helical flow, together with lower perfusion pressure (Vorobtsova et al. 2016) — [repository listing](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2770474)
- Conflicting literature on tortuosity: one study found tortuosity linked to low-shear area only in LMCA and LAD, another found LMCA WSS decreased with tortuosity. Different tortuosity metrics may explain the inconsistency, and patient-specific geometry dampened the effect — [Sci Rep 2022, s41598-022-04796-w](https://dx.doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-04796-w)
- 127 patient-specific LM bifurcations from CTCA, transient non-Newtonian CFD: Finet's ratio, diameters and curvatures correlated with low TAESS, high OSI and high RRT; haemodynamics were highly sensitive to Finet's ratio. "The combined influence of these factors aligns more closely with the distribution of haemodynamics than when considering each factor individually" (Gharleghi et al., Sci Rep 2025) — [review arXiv 2507.17109](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17109)
- 39 patient-specific left coronary trees (Zhang et al., R Soc Open Sci 2024): curvature was a crucial factor for disease onset; obstructive disease was correlated with high OSI, and curvature and OSI may predict plaque onset — [review arXiv 2507.17109, Table I](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17109)
- The review states that "curvature effect depends on the local vessel calibre/diameter variation", that bifurcation angle has minimal effect in isolation, and that the effects of anatomical features are interdependent. It also reports that curvature studies are inconsistent because of differing metrics, and that mean absolute curvature was recommended for its strong correlation with adverse TAESS — [Shen et al. review 2025, arXiv 2507.17109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17109)
- The same review notes that diameter "and its variation are important shape factors that have yet to be studied in detail" — [arXiv 2507.17109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17109)
- In four human RCAs, the WSS pattern related to lumen diameter, curvature and their combinations, with a strong correlation between WSS and reciprocal radius (Johnston & Johnston) — [summary via search, Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/7702e510-ce09-510b-ac29-4187a7595860)
- For in vivo dynamic RCA geometry (not WSS), no single curvature/torsion parameter dominated wall thickness; combinations gave R² = 0.17–0.44 — [PubMed 14500289](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14500289)

### Inferences
- Across studies, diameter or radius (the r³ term) appears to be the most robust single predictor of *time-averaged* WSS magnitude. Curvature and torsion matter more for the *direction* and multidirectionality of WSS (helicity, OSI, transWSS) and for where low WSS falls circumferentially, and less for segment-averaged TAWSS.
- Segment-averaged geometry hides the circumferential inner/outer redistribution. This may be why curvature often fails to correlate with segment-mean TAWSS (Shen 2025) while it clearly shapes local maps (Johnston 2009, idealized LM studies).

### Gaps
- No study was found reporting partial R² or interaction terms (curvature × taper) for WSS in a multivariable model.
- Sample sizes are small (3–39 trees; 127 LM bifurcations limited to the LM region). Plaque-free baseline cohorts that relate geometry → WSS → *new* plaque are rare.

## Q3. Physical scaling: Poiseuille WSS ∝ Q/r³, side-branch flow loss (Murray), and whether deviation from flow-matched taper is the relevant quantity

### Takeaway
For Poiseuille flow, τ_w = 4μQ/(πr³). Along a real coronary artery, flow is lost to side branches, so Q falls together with r. If the trunk followed Murray's law (Q ∝ r³), WSS would be constant along the vessel and taper alone would carry no WSS signal. Measured coronary flow–diameter exponents are about 2.3–2.4, not 3, so Q falls *more slowly* than r³ and WSS rises gently distally even in "normal" taper. Physically, the WSS-relevant quantity is the mismatch between the observed taper and the taper expected from branch flow loss (an "excess taper" or "under-taper"), not taper itself. Taper is also used in practice to *infer* side-branch flow loss.

### Cited Findings
- Murray's law (exponent 3) implies constant WSS throughout the vasculature, which experimental measurements do not support. Kassab and colleagues showed theoretically and experimentally that the epicardial coronary flow–diameter exponent is 7/3 (2.33) — [Kassab & Finet, EuroIntervention 2015](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/doi/10.4244/EIJV11SVA3)
- Huo & Kassab (2009) derived four structure–function scaling laws (diameter–length, volume–length, flow–diameter, volume–diameter) from minimum energy, with a diameter–length exponent of 3/7 — [Huo & Kassab, Biophys J 2009, PMC2716468](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2716468)
- A meta-analysis of 1,070 coronary trees gave a pooled exponent of 2.39, close to 7/3 — [Taylor et al., Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol 2024, PMC11380967](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11380967/)
- Patient-specific study, 6 coronary trees (van der Giessen et al., J Biomech 2011): compared with an empirical flow–diameter fit (exponent 2.27, from Doriot et al. 2000 in angiographically normal arteries), Murray's law underestimated side-branch flow. It gave about 8% higher average WSS in the main branch (range −5% to +27%) and about 13% smaller low-WSS regions near bifurcations (range −12% to +68%) — [van der Giessen et al. 2011](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/the-influence-of-boundary-conditions-on-wall-shear-stress-distrib/)
- An optimised power law gave flow and microvascular-resistance exponents of 2.15 and 2.38, not 3.0 — [Frontiers Physiol 2022, 871912](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.871912/full)
- Recent methods use Murray's law to infer side-branch diameter, and therefore flow loss, from the degree of taper of the reconstructed main vessel (porous-wall or flow-leak models) — [Saxton et al., Sheffield repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/33452/1/Saxton-EvaluationOfModels%28VoR%29.pdf)
- Doriot's fit gave a strong inlet flow–diameter correlation (R² = 0.87) and is widely used for flow-split boundary conditions — [Shen et al. review, arXiv 2507.17109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17109)
- In a single-subject RCA MRI model, side branches below MRI resolution were excluded, so flow is overestimated distally. Removing taper (constant mean velocity) shifted the largest TAWSS and transWSS to the proximal vessel, whereas OSI and the cross-flow index changed less — [Kandangwa et al., Front Bioeng Biotechnol 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2022.962687/full)
- In idealized tapered models without side branches (no flow loss), WSS rises distally as r falls — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)

### Inferences
- Let Q(s) ∝ r(s)^k with k ≈ 2.3–2.4 (empirical). Then τ_w ∝ Q/r³ ∝ r^(k−3) ≈ r^(−0.6 to −0.7). A segment that tapers from 4 to 3 mm in diameter at "normal" flow loss would see roughly a 20% rise in Poiseuille WSS (assuming k = 2.33: (3/4)^(−0.67) ≈ 1.21). The same taper with *no* branch flow loss (k = 0, as in branch-free CFD models) gives (3/4)^(−3) ≈ 2.4×. Simulated "taper effects" in branch-free idealized models therefore overstate the in vivo effect by roughly an order of magnitude in exponent. These numbers are derived arithmetic, not a published result.
- This suggests a candidate geometric predictor: **taper residual** = observed log-diameter slope minus the slope expected from downstream branch flow (side-branch diameters via a k ≈ 7/3 law). A positive residual (tapering faster than flow is lost) predicts rising WSS. A negative residual, where the vessel stays wide while flow leaves, as just distal to a large side branch, predicts locally low WSS. This links taper to the LM→LAD/LCx diameter ratios: a daughter that is wide relative to its flow share should have low WSS. This is a physics-based hypothesis; no study testing it against plaque outcomes was found.
- Raw taper, without accounting for side-branch loss, mixes the "flow-matched" part (no WSS change) with the mismatch part. It is therefore an attenuated, noisy proxy for the WSS-relevant quantity.

### Gaps
- No study was found that explicitly defines "deviation from flow-matched taper" and relates it to WSS or plaque.
- The Doriot et al. (2000) in vivo coronary WSS measurements were not retrieved directly.

## Q4. Pulsatility and cardiac motion (dynamic curvature): TAWSS vs OSI vs transWSS/TSVI

### Takeaway
Time-averaged WSS is robust to cardiac motion and to time-varying curvature; the multidirectional and oscillatory metrics (OSI, transWSS, cross-flow index) are not. Pulsatility also reverses the inner/outer WSS pattern during back flow in curved segments. Static geometry from a single CCTA phase is therefore adequate for TAWSS-type hypotheses but weak for OSI/transWSS-type ones.

### Cited Findings
- Single healthy volunteer RCA, 4-D MRI at 14 phases, moving-wall CFD. Spearman rank correlation between static-pulsatile and dynamic-pulsatile maps: TAWSS 0.93, OSI 0.75, cross-flow index 0.58, transWSS 0.19. With zero inflow, wall motion alone produced a mean TAWSS of 28% of the dynamic + pulsatile value. FSI was judged inappropriate because coronary deformation is driven mainly by cardiac motion — [Kandangwa et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2022.962687/full)
- Same study: the static model has a dominant vortex that persists through the cycle. In the dynamic model two vortices of comparable strength appear and vary over the cycle. The authors cite analytical work showing that time-varying curvature can reverse the direction of Dean vortices. Dean-like secondary flow, which largely determines transWSS, is particularly sensitive to curvature changes from bending — [Kandangwa et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2022.962687/full)
- The effect of cardiac motion on TAWSS was insignificant compared with its effect on temporal WSS variation and OSI — [CinC 2022, paper 319](https://cinc.org/archives/2022/pdf/CinC2022-319.pdf)
- FSI with bending: inclusion of bending changed OSI by +208% (LAD), +2600% (RCA) and 0% (LCx), and highly significantly changed TAWSS. The effect is vessel-specific — [UCL Discovery 10160093 / PMC10409843](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409843)
- LAD–D1 bifurcation curved over a sphere of time-varying radius (Prosi et al., J Biomech 2004): during systole, when curvature and its variation are greatest, the flow waveform dominates the effect on flow patterns and WSS. Dynamic curvature reduces the shear along the outer wall of the bifurcation — [TU Graz summary](https://www.numerik.math.tugraz.at/biomech/cfd/selected_studies/dynamic.html)
- In pulsatile tapered S-shaped models, the inner-low/outer-high pattern holds during forward flow and reverses during back flow. Locally low WSS coincided with high OSI at curvature-driven sites but not at the taper-driven proximal minimum — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)
- Swine coronaries: counter-rotating bi-helical flow develops naturally, and helicity is negatively associated with disturbed-shear descriptors — [De Nisco et al. 2019 repository](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2726967)

### Inferences
- Coronary flow is diastole-dominant. Dynamic curvature peaks in systole, when flow is low, which partly decouples curvature dynamics from the high-flow phase. This is consistent with Prosi 2004, and with TAWSS being little affected while oscillatory metrics are.
- For a CCTA-only geometry study, curvature-based predictors are most defensible for a TAWSS/low-WSS mechanism. A hypothesis based on OSI or transWSS (or TSVI) would need dynamic geometry, which a single-phase CCTA cannot provide.

### Gaps
- No study was found that quantifies TSVI (topological shear variation index) against curvature and taper together in native coronaries. The TSVI evidence found concerns healed SCAD segments (high curvature/torsion with disturbed WSS) — [Morbiducci-group SCAD study, polito repository](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2981209)
- Theodorakakos et al. (2008) on dynamic curvature was not retrieved.

## Q5. Length scales: at what spatial scale do curvature and taper effects on WSS operate?

### Takeaway
Curvature effects are local and directional. They act over a bend: a few diameters to centimetres, with the radius of curvature of the order of cm (δ ≈ 0.05), and they redistribute WSS around the circumference (inner vs outer wall). Taper and flow-loss effects are cumulative and axial: they set a baseline that changes over the length of a segment or vessel (several cm), modulated at each side branch. Analyses that average WSS or geometry over whole segments keep the taper/diameter signal but largely cancel the circumferential curvature signal.

### Cited Findings
- Idealized coronary models: Rc = 3.5 cm, inlet R = 2 mm (δ = 0.057), straight inlet 3 cm, total length 16 cm. Taper effects (20% radius reduction over 16 cm) shifted the global WSS minimum to the proximal 3 cm, whereas curvature effects localised to the inner wall of each bend — [Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)
- A curvature transition (straight to bend) produces a localised shift in the WSS distribution, so transition length matters — [Bernad et al. 2014](https://acad.ro/sectii2002/proceedings/doc2014-1/07-Bernad.pdf)
- Curvature influences downstream flow, especially with distance from a bifurcation, causing skew, spin and asymmetry of secondary-flow vortices (Shen et al., J Biomech 2021) — [review arXiv 2507.17109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17109)
- 90% of vulnerable plaques in ACS patients formed within 42 mm of the LAD ostium and 40 mm of the LCx ostium, which supports proximal-segment scales — [review arXiv 2507.17109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17109)
- Curvature metrics differ (tortuosity index, mean absolute curvature, squared curvature, curvature derivative), and mean absolute curvature was recommended for its strong correlation with adverse TAESS. Single-bend 2D representations neglect the spatial continuity of curvature — [review arXiv 2507.17109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17109)
- In 39 trees, segment-level average curvature and torsion did not correlate with segment TAESS, whereas diameter did (Diagonal) — [Shen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.06161)
- Helical flow structures propagate downstream, and torsion governs helical features (r = 0.64, swine) — [De Nisco et al. 2019](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2726967)

### Inferences
- Secondary-flow development and decay in a bend occurs over an entrance length of the order of a few diameters (≈ mm to 1–2 cm in coronaries). Segment-level (≈ 1.5–5 cm) curvature summaries are therefore coarse but not irrelevant, and peak or mean-absolute curvature is likely more informative than end-to-end tortuosity.
- For a per-segment "where" model, taper or diameter mismatch is naturally a segment-scale quantity. Curvature acts at sub-segment, circumferential scale, so a segment-level binary outcome (any new plaque in the segment) will capture curvature effects only if they shift *whether* plaque appears in the segment, not just its circumferential location. That points to a likely attenuation of curvature effects at segment level.

### Gaps
- No study was found that formally compares the explanatory power of geometry at different spatial aggregation scales (mm-level vs segment-level) for WSS.
- No coronary-specific measurement of Dean-vortex development length vs Dn was found.
