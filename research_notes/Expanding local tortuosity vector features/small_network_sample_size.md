# Sample-size requirements and overfitting risk scaling with input channel count for small neural networks vs. logistic regression

## Is there an EPV-style heuristic for small neural networks, and what do double descent / overparameterization findings imply for a "few hundred samples, few thousand parameters" model?

### Takeaway
Classical logistic regression has a well-quantified heuristic (events-per-variable, EPV ≥ 10, with more recent work pushing this to ≥ 20 in many settings) grounded in decades of simulation work. No equivalent, validated numeric heuristic exists for neural networks: the few "samples-per-parameter" rules of thumb in circulation (e.g., 10x or even 50x oversampling) are informal, pre-date modern deep learning, and are explicitly contradicted by the double-descent literature, which shows that once a network is large enough to interpolate the training set, test error can decrease again even with parameter counts far exceeding sample size. This means "few hundred samples, few thousand parameters" cannot be judged safe or risky by a parameter-counting rule alone; the actual determinant is the *effective* complexity the optimizer explores (via architecture, regularization, and implicit bias of SGD), which is not simply read off from the raw parameter count.

### Cited Findings
- The events-per-variable (EPV) rule for logistic/Cox regression requires ≥10 outcome events per candidate predictor; this traces to Peduzzi et al.'s 1996 simulation study, and "events" means minority-class outcomes, not total observations — [No rationale for 1 variable per 10 events criterion for binary logistic regression analysis, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12874-016-0267-3); [CASRAI EPV guide](https://casrai.org/guides/events-per-variable-and-the-minimum-sample-size-for-regression)
- van Smeden et al. and related simulation work show the safe EPV threshold is not fixed at 10: "EPV = 10 produced negligible bias" for 3–5 predictors, but "EPV = 20 was required to achieve the same level of bias in the 7-, 10-, and 12-predictor models," and for external validation performance "EPV ≥ 25 was a better choice" than EPV = 10 — [Adequate sample size for developing prediction models is not simply related to events per variable, PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/); [Sample size for binary logistic prediction models: Beyond EPV criteria, Statistical Methods in Medical Research 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0962280218784726)
- Frank Harrell now recommends EPV ≥ 20 as a more conservative default than the historical EPV ≥ 10 — [CASRAI EPV guide](https://casrai.org/guides/events-per-variable-and-the-minimum-sample-size-for-regression)
- A general-purpose informal rule for neural network parameter counts appears in ML practitioner writing as "a ten-fold overdetermined system" (10 samples per parameter), with "more conservative bounds for neural networks, such as d ≤ m/50" (50 samples per parameter) also cited, but the source frames these explicitly as informal heuristics, not derived from a validated theory — [Machine Learning Rules of Thumb, Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/machine-learning-rules-of-thumb-b50232b4b2f8/)
- A separate informal rule ties hidden-layer size to data volume: keep hidden units below Nh = Ns / (α·(Ni+No)) with α a scaling factor of 2–10, explicitly intended "to prevent overfitting" by limiting free parameters relative to training degrees of freedom — [Machine Learning Rules of Thumb, Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/machine-learning-rules-of-thumb-b50232b4b2f8/)
- Double descent (Belkin et al. 2018 and follow-on work): classical U-shaped bias-variance test-error curves hold only up to the "interpolation threshold" (where parameters ≈ samples); beyond that threshold, in the overparameterized regime, test error can decrease again even as parameter count keeps growing, and models with "far more parameters than data points often achieve excellent generalization" — [DataCamp, What is Double Descent](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/what-is-double-descent-in-machine-learning); [Lil'Log, Are Deep Neural Networks Dramatically Overfitted?](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2019-03-14-overfit/)
- Raw parameter count is a poor proxy for effective capacity: on CIFAR-10, a fully-connected network with 650k+ parameters was found to have only ~9k "intrinsic dimension," and a convolutional network with 62k parameters had an even lower intrinsic dimension of ~2.9k, meaning the network's actual degrees of freedom used to fit data are far smaller than the raw parameter count suggests — [Lil'Log, Are Deep Neural Networks Dramatically Overfitted?](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2019-03-14-overfit/)
- The same source reports evidence that "explicit regularization ... is neither necessary or sufficient for reducing generalization error" in some overparameterized regimes, i.e. SGD's implicit regularization (bias toward simpler/lower-norm interpolating solutions) can substitute for dropout/weight decay under some conditions — [Lil'Log, Are Deep Neural Networks Dramatically Overfitted?](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2019-03-14-overfit/)
- A practitioner-facing clinical ML guide states plainly that "a deep neural network will often require thousands of patients to converge, while a logistic regression model may achieve stable results with only a few hundred patients," and that "no general effect of machine learning models or deep learning models was observed with small sample sizes (below estimation of a magnitude of thousands)" — i.e. below roughly a thousand samples, deep models did not show a consistent advantage or a consistently safe behavior — [search-result synthesis of clinical ML practical guide material](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.15069) (note: full text of this arXiv PDF could not be parsed to verify exact wording/context; treat as a secondary-source paraphrase, see Gaps)
- A digital-mental-health ML benchmarking study found the sample-size convergence point (where adding more data stopped changing performance) differed by model family: "simpler models" (naive Bayes, logistic regression, SVM) converged at a median N ≈ 750, tree-based models (random forest, AdaBoost) converged at N ≈ 1,000, and neural networks required N ≈ 1,500 to converge — the highest of the families tested — [Estimation of minimal data sets sizes for machine learning predictions in digital mental health interventions, PMC11655521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11655521/)
- The same study recommends "a minimal data set size of N = 500 ... to mitigate overfitting" in general, and "N = 1000 as a minimal data set size when comparing simple to more complex feature groups," and separately shows that at N ≤ 300 the train/test performance gap was severe (average AUC gap up to 0.12) and that at N = 100, model-selection-by-cross-validation could yield "up to 70% of the results being >0.61 AUC despite a useless model" — a direct demonstration of small-sample overfitting/optimism in exactly the "few hundred patients" regime relevant here — [PMC11655521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11655521/)

### Inferences
- The literature converges on a qualitative, not numeric, answer: neural networks do not have a validated EPV-equivalent. The closest quantitative anchors (N≈750–1500 convergence points from PMC11655521, "thousands" for DNN convergence from the clinical ML guide) suggest that a "hundreds of patients" cohort sits below where most reported neural-network convergence studies stopped seeing overfitting/instability, i.e. is in the regime these authors flag as risky for NN-family models specifically, even though LR-family models were reported to stabilize in that same range.
- Double descent is not a license to ignore sample size: it describes behavior specifically at the interpolation threshold and beyond, reached via either very large models (as in the cited image-classification examples with tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of parameters on thousands of training images) or explicit label/optimization interpolation. A "few thousand parameter" small network trained on "a few hundred" clinical samples may or may not be past its own interpolation threshold; without measuring the model's *effective* (intrinsic) dimension or empirically checking the train/test curve for a second descent, it should not be assumed safe purely because parameters > samples.

### Gaps
- No study was found that provides a validated numeric "samples-per-parameter" or "samples-per-weight" threshold for small clinical neural networks analogous to EPV ≥ 10/20 for logistic regression. All numbers found (10x, 50x oversampling; N≈750/1000/1500 convergence points) come from either informal ML-practitioner writing or a single digital-mental-health benchmarking study, not a field-wide consensus.
- The arXiv clinical ML guide (2006.15069) could not be fully parsed (PDF text extraction failed); the "thousands vs few hundred patients" and "no general DL advantage below thousands" claims attributed to it above came through a search-engine synthesis layer, not verified against the primary text directly. Treat with more caution than the PMC-sourced findings.
- No source was found that directly computes where the interpolation threshold sits for a specific small 1D-CNN/GRU/MLP architecture size against a "few hundred subject" clinical cohort, so it is not possible to state whether this specific project's small network would already be in the double-descent-favorable overparameterized regime or still in the classical risky "just past the peak" zone.

## Does going from 1-channel to 4-channel sequence input meaningfully increase parameter count and overfitting risk in small 1D-CNN/RNN/transformer/MLP architectures, and are there architectures/techniques that decouple channel count from overfitting risk?

### Takeaway
For the architecture families in scope, the parameter-count impact of going from 1 to 4 input channels ranges from negligible (RNN/GRU/LSTM, transformer with a linear input embedding, MLP with a bottleneck) to small-but-nonzero and controllable (standard 1D-CNN first layer), because in all of these architectures the input-channel dimension only multiplies the *first* layer's weights, not the whole network; specific techniques (depthwise/grouped convolutions, weight sharing across channels, channel dropout, bottleneck/embedding layers) exist precisely to keep a wider input from proportionally inflating the parameter count or the overfitting risk it carries. No literature was found running the exact experiment (same small architecture, same small clinical cohort, varying only channel count 1 vs 4) to give a directly measured generalization-gap number, so the quantitative part of this question remains an inference from architecture mechanics rather than a cited empirical result.

### Cited Findings
- In a standard 1D-CNN, the first convolutional layer's parameter count scales linearly with input channels (kernel_size × in_channels × out_channels + bias), while every subsequent layer's parameter count is unaffected by the original input channel count — this is a direct architectural fact about convolution, not itself a citation-bearing "finding," but is the basis for the "only first layer touched" claim; see channel-based regularization treatments in the flagged sources below for the same in_channels dependence being treated as the thing to regularize the number of channels can affect.
- Depthwise convolution applies "a spatial convolution ... independently over each channel of the input tensor," and depthwise-separable convolution "decouples a convolutional layer into depthwise and pointwise layers"; this factorization was designed specifically so that scaling the number of channels does not multiply the convolution's parameter count the way a standard/dense convolution would — [Convolutional neural network, Wikipedia summary via search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolutional_neural_network)
- Weighted Channel Dropout (WCD) and spatial dropout both "operate on channels ... rating and selectively dropping them" (WCD) or "drop entire channels instead of single values" (spatial dropout), and are reported to consistently improve generalization / validation performance specifically as a regularizer against a model relying too heavily on any one channel — [Weighted Channel Dropout for Regularization of Deep CNN](https://liner.com/review/weighted-channel-dropout-for-regularization-deep-convolutional-neural-network)
- Parameter sharing (tied weights) is one of the three foundational properties of convolutional architectures, alongside sparse interactions, and multi-sensor/multichannel 1D-CNN designs have been built specifically "to unify and share weights in multi-sensor CNN by using 1D convolution in the same temporal window" — a direct architectural mechanism for keeping per-channel parameter growth sublinear — [Deep Learning for Sensor-based Activity Recognition: A Survey, arXiv:1707.03502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03502)
- With very limited data, "the estimation of model parameters may be greatly affected by noise and random errors," and lightweight, shared/grouped-weight convolutional designs are proposed as an explicit countermeasure to achieve "excellent generalization performance" in the few-shot multivariate time-series regime — [A shared multi-scale lightweight convolution generative network for few-shot multivariate time series forecasting, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1568494624011943)
- In the ECG domain, adding channels/leads is reported to have gone both ways depending on how it's used: optimal-lead-subset selection ("eliminating redundancy") was shown to outperform using the complete 12-lead ECG on an external test set, framed as a way to "regularize models for improving generalizability" — i.e., more raw channels increased overfitting/redundancy risk in that setting — [Optimal ECG-lead selection increases generalizability of deep learning on ECG abnormality classification](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsta.2020.0258) (abstract-level; full text was inaccessible, see Gaps). Conversely, a separate finding on the same general 12-lead-vs-fewer-lead question reported that "on the 12-lead subset, convergence was generally faster and validation F1 scores attained higher and more stable levels ... suggesting that the increased sample size and richer input information facilitate more robust generalization" — [Enhancing ECG Classification Generalization Through Unified Multi-Dataset Training, PMC13030569](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030569/). These two findings point in opposite directions and are flagged as a direct conflict below.
- For dual-channel QRS-complex detection, "dual-channel input is better than using a single channel," attributed to the auxiliary (inverted-signal) channel "increas[ing] the diversity of the input so the model learns variations better" — a case where added channels improved rather than hurt performance, though this is a signal-processing augmentation-style channel (a transform of the same signal) rather than a physically distinct additional feature — [Short-time detection of QRS complexes using dual channels based on U-Net and bidirectional LSTM, arXiv:1912.09223](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.09223)

### Inferences
- Because RNN/GRU/LSTM input weight matrices are shaped [hidden_size × (input_size + hidden_size)], moving from 1 to 4 input channels increases only the input-to-hidden block by roughly 3×hidden_size extra weights, typically a small fraction of a recurrent network's total parameter count (which is dominated by the hidden-to-hidden recurrent weights); the same applies to a transformer's input embedding/linear projection layer, whose parameter increase from 1 to 4 channels is confined to a single [d_model × in_channels] matrix. In both families, the increase in overfitting risk from 4x input channels should be small relative to the risk already carried by the recurrent/attention parameters, unless in_channels is made large relative to hidden_size/d_model.
- For a 1D-CNN, the parameter increase is concentrated in the very first layer; using a small number of first-layer filters, a depthwise-separable or grouped first convolution, or channel dropout on the raw input are all established, low-effort ways (per the sources above) to keep a 4-channel input from proportionally increasing overfitting risk relative to a 1-channel input, consistent with the general finding that these techniques were developed precisely for this purpose in small-data CNN settings.
- The two conflicting ECG findings above (more leads helping vs. more leads requiring redundancy-elimination to generalize) suggest that whether extra channels raise overfitting risk in practice is data-dependent: if the extra channels each carry independently useful signal (as clinical hypothesis suggests torsion/angle/diameter may, alongside curvature), risk is more likely to behave like the "richer input, better generalization" case; if the extra channels are highly redundant with the first, risk is more likely to behave like the "redundancy inflates overfitting" case. This project's specific channels (curvature, torsion, angle, diameter) are geometrically related but not simple transforms of one another, which argues, by analogy, closer to the "independently informative multi-lead" case than the "12-lead redundancy" case, but no source directly validates this analogy for coronary centerline features specifically.

### Gaps
- No source directly measured the same small architecture's train/test generalization gap under 1-channel vs 4-channel input on a shared held-out clinical cohort; every finding above is either about a different domain (ECG leads, sensor activity recognition, image channels) or about the architectural mechanics of parameter scaling, not an empirical overfitting-gap comparison for this exact use case.
- The abstract of the "Optimal ECG-lead selection" paper could not be retrieved in full (both the Royal Society page and the PubMed page returned access-blocked/CAPTCHA responses); the finding above rests only on the search-engine snippet, not a verified primary-source read.
- No literature was found specifically quantifying the parameter increase or overfitting-risk change for a small transformer's positional/input embedding when going from 1 to 4 input channels in a clinical sequence setting; the inference above is derived from general transformer architecture mechanics, not a cited clinical ML transformer study.

## What regularization strategies are specifically recommended in the clinical/medical deep learning literature for small-cohort neural network training?

### Takeaway
The literature converges on a standard toolkit, dropout, weight decay (L2), early stopping, data augmentation, and transfer learning/pretraining, all repeatedly recommended for small clinical cohorts, plus nested cross-validation as the validation-side complement to keep the resulting performance estimate honest. None of these techniques is reported with a universal numeric setting (e.g., a single "correct" dropout rate) that generalizes across small-cohort studies; recommendations are architecture- and dataset-specific.

### Cited Findings
- Standard techniques cited for reducing overfitting broadly: "Lasso and Ridge regularization, weight decay, batch normalization, early stopping, data augmentation and dropout" — [Regularization techniques in Deep Learning, search synthesis](https://medium.com/@pierre.lgsm/regularization-techniques-in-deep-learning-bdf06862a5ff)
- "Dropout injects stochastic regularization that effectively curbs overfitting, which is otherwise pronounced in small medical cohorts," with a specific claim that "for cohorts of around 1,000–1,500 samples, well-regularized MLPs with dropout and weight decay substantially reduce the risk of overfitting" — [search synthesis of small-cohort MLP regularization literature] (note: this specific N-range claim could not be traced to a single verifiable primary source within this search session; see Gaps)
- In time-series forecasting specifically, "dropout regularizes models like RNNs/GRUs to avoid learning unstable patterns," but with the caveat that "dropping many neurons may remove essential information from small datasets" — i.e. dropout rate needs to be tuned down, not maximized, as data shrinks — [search synthesis, regularization in deep learning](https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/deep-learning/dropout-regularization-in-deep-learning/)
- Early stopping "automatically terminates training when a performance measure (e.g., validation loss, accuracy) ceases to improve," standard practice for small-cohort training to avoid continuing to fit noise after the validation curve turns — [Regularization techniques in Deep Learning](https://medium.com/@pierre.lgsm/regularization-techniques-in-deep-learning-bdf06862a5ff)
- Transfer learning: "fine-tuning a pre-trained network allows classification of data in the target domain, occasionally with few images, using knowledge acquired in another domain," and begins "with a well-trained network on a source dataset and fine-tunes only on the target sample, which helps control overfitting and variance, common pitfalls in small clinical studies" — but with the explicit limitation that "if the source task is not sufficiently similar to the target task ... the result can be negative transfer learning" — [Fine-tuning pre-trained neural networks for medical image classification in small clinical datasets, Multimedia Tools and Applications](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11042-023-16529-w); [transfer learning search synthesis](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12421)
- Nested cross-validation is specifically recommended for small/high-dimensional settings because "the magnitude of [] optimistic bias is largest in small-sample, high-dimensional settings," and ordinary (non-nested) CV in this regime "produces systematically inflated accuracy estimates that grow with the hyperparameter search space"; one synthesis states nested CV "is recommended to be used with datasets with a sample size of up to 1000 to reduce strongly biased performance" — [A Guide to Cross-Validation for AI in Medical Imaging, PMC10388213](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10388213/); [Machine learning algorithm validation with a limited sample size, PMC6837442](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6837442/)
- Nested CV's cost is explicit: it "removes the inflation at the cost of higher variance" in the resulting performance estimate, i.e. it trades optimism for wider confidence intervals, which is a real cost when the underlying cohort is only a few hundred patients — [search synthesis, nested cross-validation](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/nested-cross-validation)

### Inferences
- For this project's scale (hundreds of patients, small 1D-CNN/RNN/MLP/transformer), the combination most directly supported by the cited sources is: early stopping on a held-out validation split, moderate (not maximal) dropout tuned per-architecture, L2 weight decay, and nested (or repeated/outer) cross-validation for the final honest performance estimate rather than a single train/val/test split, given how small a few-hundred-patient cohort is relative to the "up to 1000" range where nested CV's bias-correction is reported to matter most.
- Transfer learning is the weakest fit of the standard toolkit for this specific project: it is best evidenced for CNNs on natural/medical images with an available large pretraining corpus (e.g. ImageNet-pretrained CNNs fine-tuned on small clinical image sets), and no source was found describing a standard large pretraining corpus for coronary centerline curvature/torsion/angle/diameter sequences, so this mitigation is likely not readily available for the sequence model in this thesis without constructing a synthetic or cross-cohort pretraining task.

### Gaps
- No source gave a specific, validated dropout-rate or weight-decay-coefficient number tied to cohort sizes in the low hundreds for 1D-CNN/RNN/transformer sequence models specifically (as opposed to image CNNs or generic MLPs); all numeric guidance found was for image classification or general tabular/MLP settings.
- The "N ≈ 1,000–1,500 for well-regularized MLPs" and "nested CV up to N=1000" figures both came from secondary syntheses rather than a verified read of primary text; they should be treated as approximate orientation points, not precise thresholds.
- No source specifically addressed data augmentation for centerline/geometric sequence data (e.g. jitter, elastic deformation, resampling along arc length) in a clinical small-cohort setting; augmentation guidance found was general (image/time-series) rather than specific to per-point vessel-geometry sequences.

## Do TRIPOD-AI/PROBAST-AI (or other reporting/validation standards) address predictor/channel count and small-sample risk for neural-network clinical prediction models, distinct from classical TRIPOD/PROBAST?

### Takeaway
TRIPOD-AI (reporting) and PROBAST-AI (risk-of-bias appraisal) exist specifically to extend the regression-era TRIPOD/PROBAST guidance to AI/ML-based prediction models, and the harmonized TRIPOD+AI statement (BMJ, April 2024) explicitly requires reporting of sample size adequacy; however, no source retrieved in this search gave a numeric or channel-count-specific rule from these guidelines (e.g. nothing analogous to "EPV ≥ 10" for neural network predictor/channel counts) — the guidance found is procedural/reporting-focused ("report and justify your sample size and predictor count") rather than a fixed threshold.

### Cited Findings
- TRIPOD-AI and PROBAST-AI were developed as "extensions to improve reporting and critical appraisal of prediction model studies based on artificial intelligence and machine learning techniques," via systematic reviews, Delphi consensus, and consensus meetings, following EQUATOR Network methodology — [Protocol for development of a reporting guideline (TRIPOD-AI) and risk of bias tool (PROBAST-AI), PMC8273461](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8273461/)
- TRIPOD+AI, published in the BMJ on 16 April 2024, provides "harmonised guidance for reporting prediction model studies irrespective of regression or machine learning methods, with an expanded 27-item checklist and detailed explanations" — [TRIPOD+AI statement, PMC11019967](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11019967/)
- Empirical audits of TRIPOD+AI adherence in ML-based clinical prediction studies report poor real-world compliance: in one COVID-19 prognostic-model review, "conventional models achiev[ed] 38.1% adherence and machine learning-based studies lagg[ed] behind at 28.4%," and separately, "only 30% of studies met the minimum sample size required for ML models," with 67% of 606 reviewed COVID-19 prognostic models trained/tested on an "inadequate sample size" — [Machine learning-based COVID-19 prognostic models lag behind in reporting quality, Diagnostic and Prognostic Research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41512-026-00218-x)
- A general methodological point made in the broader literature (not TRIPOD-specific but relevant to how these standards are applied): "modern ML algorithms (such as random forests, support vector machines, and neural networks) are more data-intensive and prone to overfitting compared to logistic regression, especially as model complexity increases, and require substantially larger sample sizes to achieve similar levels of robustness," and "machine learning studies sometimes include a high number of predictors relative to the number of training cases, which increases the risk of overfitting and poor generalizability" — [search synthesis around TRIPOD+AI/sample-size literature](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(25)00021-4/fulltext) (full text access-blocked; see Gaps)

### Inferences
- Given that TRIPOD+AI is a reporting checklist rather than a statistical derivation, it is unlikely to supply a numeric predictor/channel-count threshold the way EPV does for regression; its practical relevance to this thesis is to require explicit justification and reporting of (a) how the final sample size was determined, (b) how many candidate predictors/channels/features were considered vs. retained, and (c) validation methodology (e.g. nested CV) — i.e. it obliges the thesis to document the 1-channel-vs-4-channel decision and its sample-size rationale, rather than telling the thesis what the "safe" channel count is.

### Gaps
- The TRIPOD+AI supplement PDF (tripod-statement.org) and the full BMJ/PMC text could not be parsed for exact checklist item wording in this session (PDF text extraction and CAPTCHA-gated pages both failed); the characterization above is at the level of what secondary sources report about TRIPOD+AI's scope and adherence, not a verified quote of a specific item number addressing "predictor count" or "channel count."
- No PROBAST-AI-specific quantitative signaling question (of the kind PROBAST's classical version has for "were there enough events per predictor?") could be confirmed or denied from the sources retrieved; this is a genuine open question for a full guideline read rather than something this search could resolve.

## Is there empirical evidence comparing a single-channel vs multi-channel small network's generalization gap in a small-cohort clinical imaging or physiological signal setting, as a proxy for this project?

### Takeaway
Directly comparable single-vs-multi-channel generalization-gap evidence in a small-cohort setting was not found; the closest proxies are ECG lead-count studies, and these proxies point in different directions depending on whether the extra channels are redundant (worse: overfitting/reduced external generalization) or genuinely complementary (better: richer input, more stable validation performance). No study in the retrieved set used a cohort of comparable size (hundreds of subjects) to this thesis while varying only channel count.
### Cited Findings
- "Diagnostic redundancy exists in the 12-lead ECG, which could impose a systematic overfitting on deep learning, causing poor generalization," and an "optimal ECG-lead subset" (fewer, less redundant channels) was shown to "outperform... significantly the use of the complete 12-lead ECG on the external test set" — evidence that more channels can increase overfitting/harm external generalization when those channels are highly redundant — [Optimal ECG-lead selection increases generalizability of deep learning on ECG abnormality classification](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsta.2020.0258) (abstract/snippet level only, full text inaccessible)
- Conversely, "on the 12-lead subset, convergence was generally faster and validation F1 scores attained higher and more stable levels across all models, suggesting that the increased sample size and richer input information facilitate more robust generalization" than single-lead subsets — evidence in the opposite direction, that more (here, non-redundant/richer) channels can improve generalization stability — [Enhancing ECG Classification Generalization Through Unified Multi-Dataset Training, PMC13030569](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030569/)
- For dual-channel QRS detection, adding a second (transformed) channel was reported as unambiguously beneficial, framed as increasing "diversity of the input so the model learns variations better" — [arXiv:1912.09223](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.09223)
- Single-lead ECG deep learning is reported as an active, separate research area motivated by portability/cost rather than by an established finding that fewer channels generalize better per se; one study is described as "the first to systematically evaluate the diagnostic potential of single-lead ECG data using advanced deep learning models," implying the field still treats single-vs-multi-lead performance as an open empirical question rather than a settled one — [Deep Learning-Driven Single-Lead ECG Classification, PMC11817768](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11817768/)

### Inferences
- The ECG evidence base is genuinely mixed and appears to depend on redundancy: 12-lead ECG channels are known to be highly correlated with each other (same cardiac electrical event viewed from different electrode placements), which is a specific reason more leads can hurt generalization there. Coronary centerline curvature, torsion, angle, and diameter measured at the same point are geometrically related but are not the redundant multi-view case that 12-lead ECG represents (they capture different local geometric properties, not the same signal from different sensors), so the "redundancy causes overfitting" ECG finding is a weaker analogy for this project than the "richer complementary information improves generalization" finding, though neither was tested on a comparably small cohort.

### Gaps
- No small-cohort (order-of-hundreds-of-subjects) clinical imaging or physiological-signal study with a controlled 1-channel-vs-4-channel small-network comparison and reported train/test generalization gap was found in this search. This is the most direct empirical gap relative to the project's open question, and the report-writer should flag that this specific comparison likely does not exist in the literature and any claim about it in the thesis should be presented as a reasoned inference from architecture mechanics and adjacent-domain (ECG) evidence, not as a directly cited empirical result.
- The Royal Society ECG-lead-selection paper's full text (which would likely contain the most directly relevant numeric generalization-gap comparison) could not be accessed (403 Forbidden / CAPTCHA); a manual/institutional-access follow-up read of this paper is recommended if this question needs to be pinned down more precisely.
