# Denoising and Thresholding Methods for Noisy Torsion/Curvature Estimation

## Key Question 1: What established denoising/thresholding techniques exist for torsion (or curvature, third derivatives) from noisy discretized curves? Is a fixed-magnitude minimum threshold an established technique, or do practitioners prefer smoothing/spline regularization, scale-space, or statistical noise-floor characterization?

### Takeaway
No source found treats a fixed-magnitude "set-to-zero-below-X" threshold as an established denoising technique for torsion (or curvature). The consistent recommendation across differential-geometry, numerical-differentiation, and vascular-centerline literatures is to regularize the estimation itself — smoothing splines with a roughness penalty, total-variation-regularized differentiation, parametric curve fitting, or large-window Gaussian filtering/scale-space — rather than to post-hoc threshold the resulting derivative values.

### Cited Findings
- Chartrand's widely-cited work on numerical differentiation of noisy data states explicitly: "Denoising the data before or after differentiating does not generally give satisfactory results. Instead, the real secret is to regularize the differentiation itself" — the basis of total-variation-regularized (TVR) differentiation, which allows the derivative to be discontinuous rather than penalizing magnitude directly — [Chartrand, "Numerical Differentiation of Noisy, Nonsmooth Data"](https://jasoncantarella.com/downloads/chartrand-2007-numerical.pdf); implementation at [GitHub: regularized_differentiation](https://github.com/rickchartrand/regularized_differentiation).
- "Curvature and Torsion estimation of 3D functional data" (the paper already flagged in this project's prior research) uses penalized regression with smoothing splines, minimizing a roughness penalty λ∫‖θ''(t)‖²dt on second derivatives, and explicitly frames this as a *joint estimation* approach that "eliminates oscillations and noise more effectively" than post-hoc filtering of an already-differentiated signal — [arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02398).
- The same paper explicitly ranks the two quantities by estimation difficulty: "the torsion is a little more difficult to estimate, as it is linked to the third derivative," and its simulations show torsion errors consistently larger than curvature errors across noise scenarios — [arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02398).
- A parametric-curve-fitting approach to curvature/torsion estimation for sampled 3D curves (weighted least-squares fitting of second/third-order polynomials to local coordinate windows) is presented as more robust than direct finite-difference estimation, again a smoothing/model-fitting strategy rather than a threshold — [Curvature and torsion estimators based on parametric curve fitting](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0097849305001275).
- A SPIE paper on coronary-artery motion analysis reports using "a linear Gaussian filter with large window and variance" specifically to suppress noise before computing Frenet curvature/torsion, i.e. a smoothing-kernel approach — [Curvature and torsion estimation for coronary-artery motion analysis, SPIE](https://www.spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/5369/0000/Curvature-and-torsion-estimation-for-coronary-artery-motion-analysis/10.1117/12.537301.short).
- Scale-space / multiscale curvature estimation is an established alternative family: a scale-space algorithm adaptively selects "proper scale...at every point of interest," deliberately choosing scales "above the scale of noise" to recover reliable curvature, including for straight-line (near-zero-curvature) detection — [Scale Space Edge Curvature Estimation and Its Application to Straight Lines Detection](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233834389_Scale_Space_Edge_Curvature_Estimation_and_Its_Application_to_Straight_Lines_Detection); [Multiscale Geometric Methods for Data Sets I: Multiscale SVD, Noise and Curvature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1063520315001414).
- A discrete-differential-geometry torsion estimator based on cross-ratios proves second-order asymptotic convergence (τ₀ = τ + O(ε²)) as sampling density increases, but explicitly does not address noisy (as opposed to noise-free, densely sampled) data, and does not discuss thresholding or a noise/bias distinction at all — [Discrete curvature and torsion from cross-ratios, PMC8549994](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8549994/).
- Hard-thresholding is an established technique, but in an entirely different context (wavelet-coefficient denoising of signals/images), where the threshold is derived from an estimated noise standard deviation at each wavelet scale (k·σⱼ), not applied as a fixed cutoff on a raw derivative quantity like torsion — [A New Wavelet Denoising Method for Selecting Decomposition Levels and Noise Thresholds, PMC5079185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5079185/).

### Inferences
- The pattern across every source found (differential geometry, numerical analysis, vascular imaging) is the same: noise in high-order derivative quantities is handled by changing *how the derivative is computed* (regularized differentiation, penalized splines, wider smoothing kernels, coarser scale) rather than by filtering the *output* values after the fact with a fixed magnitude cutoff. A minimum-magnitude deadband on torsion itself would be a novel, unattested technique relative to this literature, not a recognized method being adapted.
- Wavelet hard-thresholding is superficially the closest analogue to a "minimum magnitude threshold," but even there the threshold is not an arbitrary fixed constant — it is derived per-scale from an estimated noise standard deviation. A defensible torsion threshold, if one were attempted at all, would need the same kind of noise-level characterization behind it (see Key Question 2/3), not a hand-picked constant.

### Gaps
- No source was found that reports having tried a fixed-magnitude threshold on torsion specifically and compared it against smoothing-based alternatives; the literature does not appear to consider it a candidate worth testing, but this is an absence-of-evidence finding, not a source that explicitly rules it out.
- Could not retrieve full text of the MDPI paper "Spline-Based Smoothing of Noisy Discrete Curves in the Frenet–Serret Framework: Sensitivity Analysis of Curvature and Torsion Estimation via CSI and TSI Indices" (HTTP 403 on fetch); its CSI/TSI sensitivity-index framework is likely directly relevant (title suggests a quantitative sensitivity comparison of curvature vs. torsion under noise) but could not be verified from primary text.

## Key Question 2: Does the literature distinguish random-noise error from systematic/estimator-specific bias in curvature/torsion, and is there direct evidence on which kind of error dominates in automated vascular centerline pipelines?

### Takeaway
Yes — one directly relevant vascular study explicitly separates the two error sources and shows both are present, but frames the *dominant, practically limiting* problem as systematic and pipeline-dependent: different established centerline/curvature-torsion extraction algorithms produce numbers that are not comparable to each other, driven by fixed pipeline choices (resampling length, smoothing factor) rather than by per-sample random noise that averaging could fix.

### Cited Findings
- "Automated landmarking of bends in vascular structures: a comparative study" directly compares two established algorithms (Piccinelli's and Bogunović's) for detecting curvature/torsion-based bends on the internal carotid artery and finds they are **not comparable**: "The two algorithms are not comparable in a meta-analysis," with Piccinelli detecting 6-8 bends versus Bogunović's consistent 4, and the largest divergence concentrated in specific anatomical bends — [PMC8626959](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8626959/).
- The same study found the centerline **resampling length** parameter *r* had the largest effect on the number of detected curvature/torsion-defined bends (5 to 15 bends depending on *r*), and that the smoothing factor λ had a moderate, algorithm-dependent effect (Piccinelli's algorithm: 6-7 bends for λ∈[1.2,1.5]; Bogunović's: consistently 4 bends over the same range) — i.e., the output is a direct function of fixed preprocessing choices, not just per-scan noise — [PMC8626959](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8626959/).
- The same study also reports a random/repeatability component: different segmentations of the same vessel produced variable bend counts, and Piccinelli's algorithm showed high inter-run variability (CV=47% for the superior bend) versus Bogunović's greater stability (CV<5%) — confirming both random and systematic (algorithm-specific) components coexist, but that their relative size is itself algorithm-dependent — [PMC8626959](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8626959/).
- A 2026 methods paper on automated centerline extraction from meshed vascular models is indexed as raising the concern that, for curvature/torsion computed from extracted centerlines, "input parameters, original source code, comparability, and robustness of the algorithms remain unknown" (per search-result summary; full text could not be retrieved, HTTP 403) — [Sanchez Van Moer et al. 2026, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cnm.70183).
- In the pure differential-geometry setting (noise-free but discretely sampled curves), error is characterized as purely a function of sampling density with a proven asymptotic convergence rate (O(ε²) for torsion) — a systematic, deterministic discretization bias that shrinks with finer sampling, distinct in kind from random measurement noise — [Discrete curvature and torsion from cross-ratios, PMC8549994](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8549994/).
- "Curvature and Torsion estimation of 3D functional data" demonstrates that under noisy observations, a naive ("extrinsic formula") estimator becomes "highly unstable" (error norm 396 vs. 0.605 for the paper's regularized method) — illustrating that estimator *choice*, not just noise level, systematically determines torsion error magnitude — [arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02398).

### Inferences
- The internal-carotid landmarking study is the strongest direct evidence found that curvature/torsion-derived vascular features carry a systematic, pipeline-specific bias component large enough to make two established, peer-reviewed algorithms non-comparable on the same vessels. This corroborates this project's prior concern: a magnitude threshold tuned against one extraction pipeline's noise characteristics would not transfer to another pipeline (different resampling length, different smoothing factor) because the offending error is baked into the pipeline's fixed choices, not randomly distributed around the true value.
- No source directly decomposes torsion error into a quantified random-vs-systematic variance ratio for a single pipeline (e.g., "X% of error is random, Y% is bias"). The evidence found is qualitative/comparative (across algorithms) rather than a formal bias-variance decomposition for torsion specifically.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the full text of Sanchez Van Moer et al. 2026 (HTTP 403) to confirm the exact wording and any quantitative claims about torsion comparability across extraction pipelines.
- No source was found that performs a formal statistical bias-variance decomposition of torsion error specifically (as opposed to curvature, or bend-count) from a real automated pipeline; the "Zhu/Pao 2008" study referenced in this project's prior research could not be independently located in this search session (search for it directly did not surface the original publication).
- No source explicitly states "torsion error in vascular centerlines is dominated by random noise" or the reverse in quantitative terms; the inference of systematic dominance rests on the cross-algorithm comparability failure above, which is suggestive but not a direct measurement of the random/systematic split.

## Key Question 3: Do biomedical vessel-tortuosity studies use a "significant tortuosity" threshold, and how was it derived (a priori convention, statistical/reference-population, or outcome-optimized)?

### Takeaway
Every coronary tortuosity study found, including the 737-patient cohort study this project previously identified, defines "significant" tortuosity using a fixed angular bend-count convention inherited from earlier papers, not a threshold statistically derived from a reference population or optimized against outcomes. By contrast, at least one retinal (retinopathy-of-prematurity) tortuosity study did derive its threshold empirically by optimizing sensitivity/specificity against a clinical outcome, showing the outcome-optimized approach exists in the broader vascular-tortuosity literature, but not in the coronary studies traced here.

### Cited Findings
- The 737-patient study ("The significant coronary tortuosity and atherosclerotic coronary artery disease; What is the relation?") defines significant coronary tortuosity (SCT) as either: **moderate** — "≥3 consecutive curvatures of 90° to 180° measured at end-diastole in a major epicardial coronary artery ≥2 mm in diameter" — or **severe** — "≥2 consecutive curvatures of ≥180°" in the same vessel class; 29.17% of the 737 patients met this SCT definition — [PMC6335987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6335987/).
- That study did not derive the 90°/180° cutoffs itself; it cites the moderate/severe definitions to two earlier papers (Jakob et al. 1996 and Groves et al. 2009), i.e. it adopts a pre-existing categorical clinical convention rather than deriving a threshold from its own 737-patient reference distribution or optimizing it against an outcome — [PMC6335987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6335987/).
- The paper itself notes that prior tortuosity definitions in the literature "varied considerably," citing an alternative convention of "≥3 consecutive bends ≥45°" used elsewhere — evidence that the field has multiple competing a priori conventions rather than one statistically grounded standard — [PMC6335987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6335987/).
- A separate coronary tortuosity study (PLOS ONE, "Clinical Implication of Coronary Tortuosity in Patients with Coronary Artery Disease") defines tortuosity as "≥3 bends (≥45° change in vessel direction) along the main trunk of at least one artery, present both in systole and diastole," citing Turgut et al. 2007 for this convention, again with no stated derivation rationale for the ≥45°/≥3-bends choice — [PLOS ONE 10.1371/journal.pone.0024232](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0024232).
- By contrast, a retinal study in retinopathy of prematurity derived its tortuosity threshold empirically: a "threshold vessel tortuosity value of 158.99 degrees at multiplier 8" was selected because it gave 82% sensitivity and 98% specificity for distinguishing abnormal from normal retinal tortuosity against a clinical reference standard — a data-driven, outcome-optimized threshold, structurally different from the coronary literature's inherited angular conventions (per search-result summary; primary source not independently fetched in this session).
- General reviews of vascular tortuosity measurement explicitly flag the absence of standardization: "there is currently a significant limitation regarding defined thresholds... the need for standardized calculation models and defined pathological thresholds" is described as an open problem in the field — [Vascular tortuosity: a mathematical modeling perspective](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1880654624002774); "Incorporation of this arterial phenotype into clinical practice requires standardization in terms of definition, measurement, and normalcy criteria" — [Arterial Tortuosity, Hypertension (AHA)](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.118.11647).

### Inferences
- The coronary "significant tortuosity" literature is best characterized as a chain of citation-inherited a priori conventions (Turgut 2007 → later coronary studies; Jakob 1996/Groves 2009 → the 737-patient study), not as independently statistically validated thresholds. None of the coronary sources found reports deriving its cutoff from a control-population noise/variability distribution or from outcome optimization.
- This matters directly for the torsion-threshold question: even in the domain (coronary tortuosity) where a "significant" threshold convention is most established, that convention was built on a simple visually-assessed angular bend count on 2D/3D angiography, not on a differential-geometry quantity like torsion computed from an automated centerline pipeline, and its own derivation was not statistically principled. It cannot be used as precedent for "thresholds work if chosen carefully" in the torsion case, since it was not chosen via a noise-characterization procedure either.
- Where outcome-optimized thresholds do exist in the broader tortuosity literature (the retinal ROP example), they are validated against a specific clinical endpoint (progression to threshold ROP disease) using sensitivity/specificity analysis on labeled data, a fundamentally different exercise from "denoising" a signal — see Key Question 4.

### Gaps
- Could not verify the exact derivation methodology of the Jakob et al. 1996 and Groves et al. 2009 papers underlying the 737-patient study's definitions; only that the 737-patient paper cites them without restating their own derivation rationale.
- Could not independently fetch primary source for the 158.99°/multiplier-8 ROP threshold to confirm the exact optimization procedure (only available via search-result summary, not the full paper).
- No coronary or retinal source found ties its tortuosity threshold specifically to a *torsion* value (all found thresholds are on bend angle / integrated curvature-based tortuosity indices, not on torsion as a distinct differential-geometry quantity). This is a gap in the literature itself, not just in this search: no vascular study located uses a "significant torsion" threshold analogous to "significant tortuosity."

## Key Question 4: Does successfully removing noise via a threshold, on its own, generate outcome evidence — or is "denoising" independent from "proving predictive value"?

### Takeaway
No source found conflates these two claims, and the literature surveyed for Key Questions 1-3 illustrates them as genuinely separate exercises: methods papers (Key Question 1) validate thresholds/regularization against known ground-truth curves or simulated noise, never against a disease outcome; outcome-linked thresholds (Key Question 3's ROP example) are validated against sensitivity/specificity for a clinical endpoint using an already-chosen tortuosity definition, not against a noise model. No study was found that does both in sequence for torsion (denoise, then separately demonstrate predictive value against CAD).

### Cited Findings
- The differential-geometry/numerical-methods papers validate their smoothing/regularization approaches purely against reconstruction accuracy relative to known analytical curves or simulated noise-free ground truth (e.g., L²-norm error of 0.605 vs. 396 in [arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02398)) — there is no outcome (disease) variable anywhere in this class of evidence.
- The outcome-optimized retinal tortuosity threshold (158.99°, 82%/98% sensitivity/specificity for ROP) is validated purely against a clinical classification endpoint; the underlying tortuosity metric's noise properties are not characterized or reported as part of that validation, per the available search-result summary.
- The coronary "significant tortuosity" studies (PMC6335987, PLOS ONE 0024232) use their inherited a priori bend-angle thresholds and then separately test statistical association with CAD outcomes (e.g., PMC6335987 reports SCT prevalence of 29.17% and association with sex/age) — but this is a test of the *already-defined* tortuosity metric's association with disease, not evidence that thresholding *cleaned up noise* in a way that then produced the association. The threshold's origin (Jakob 1996/Groves 2009 convention) was not itself validated as a denoising procedure.

### Inferences
- Based on the pattern across every source found: "a threshold removes noise" and "a threshold-defined feature predicts an outcome" are answered by structurally different types of study (numerical-methods/simulation papers for the former; clinical cohort/diagnostic-accuracy papers for the latter), and no source performs both steps for the same metric in the vascular tortuosity literature. This directly supports treating them as two independent, non-substitutable claims: even if a magnitude threshold could be shown (via synthetic/phantom testing) to suppress torsion's random-noise component, that would say nothing about whether the resulting "denoised" torsion values carry any relationship to CAD, since none of the coronary CAD-outcome studies found here were built on a denoised/thresholded torsion signal in the first place — they were built on manually-assessed bend angles, an entirely different measurement modality.
- Given the systematic/pipeline-dependent bias documented in Key Question 2 (non-comparable curvature/torsion outputs across established centerline-extraction algorithms), a threshold that appeared to "clean up" one pipeline's torsion output could not be assumed to generalize to another pipeline or, a fortiori, to carry predictive value for CAD, since no such transfer or outcome link exists in any source reviewed.

### Gaps
- No study was found that combines a torsion-specific denoising/thresholding step with a subsequent outcome (CAD) validation; this appears to be a genuine gap in the literature rather than something this search failed to locate, given the volume and consistency of related searches performed.
- No formal methodological or philosophy-of-measurement source was found that explicitly names/frames the "denoising is not validation" distinction in exactly these terms for differential-geometry biomarkers; the separation is inferred from the structural pattern of the studies found (per the Inferences above) rather than stated explicitly by any single cited source.
