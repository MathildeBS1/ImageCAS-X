## Does the garden-of-forking-paths / researcher-degrees-of-freedom literature say that having the freedom to choose a threshold, a smoothing method, and an aggregation method counts as hidden multiple comparisons even if only one combination is ever reported?

### Takeaway
Yes, unambiguously, and this is the central point of Gelman and Loken's paper: the problem does not require a "fishing expedition" or trying multiple thresholds and reporting the best one. It is enough that the researcher *could have* made a different, equally defensible choice of threshold/smoothing/aggregation under different data, and that this choice is contingent on looking at (or having beliefs shaped by) the data. The false-positive-rate inflation is driven by the existence of the branch points, not by how many branches were actually walked.

### Cited Findings
- Gelman and Loken's "garden of forking paths" argument: researchers can reach a multiple-comparisons problem "even when there is no 'fishing expedition' or 'p-hacking' and the research hypothesis was posited ahead of time" — the title and thesis of the paper itself — [The garden of forking paths, Gelman & Loken](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/unpublished/p_hacking.pdf); also indexed at [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-garden-of-forking-paths-:-Why-multiple-can-be-a-Gelman-Loken/b63e25900013605c16f4ad74c636cfbd8e9a3e8e) and [Wikipedia: Forking paths problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forking_paths_problem). (Full PDF text could not be machine-extracted in this session — it returned as a binary/compressed stream to the fetch tool — so exact wording below is drawn from the paper's title/abstract-level framing as corroborated across multiple independent secondary summaries, not from a directly quoted passage; this should be flagged as abstract/summary-level rather than full-text-verified.)
- The key mechanism, as consistently summarized across sources: "there can be a large number of potential comparisons when the details of data analysis are highly contingent on data, without the researcher having to perform any conscious procedure of fishing or examining multiple p-values" — it "suffices to have potential comparisons... analyses that would have been run under different data" — [Wikipedia: Forking paths problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forking_paths_problem); consistent framing also at [FORRT glossary](https://forrt.org/glossary/english/garden_of_forking_paths/)
- Named examples of "researcher degrees of freedom" in the Gelman/Loken framing include choosing among main effects or interactions, methods for data exclusion, whether to combine different studies/subgroups, and method of data analysis — a threshold choice, a choice of aggregation statistic (mean/max/quantile), and a choice of smoothing method are all instances of this same category ("method of data analysis" contingent on the data) — [Wikipedia: Forking paths problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forking_paths_problem)
- Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn's companion paper, "False-Positive Psychology" (Psychological Science, 2011), independently converges on the same conclusion from an experimental-design angle: flexibility in data collection, analysis, and reporting — even when a researcher believes only one analysis was run — "dramatically increases actual false-positive rates," to the point that "a researcher is more likely to falsely find evidence that an effect exists than to correctly find evidence that it does not" — [Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn 2011, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797611417632)
- Simmons et al. name four common researcher degrees of freedom: choosing sample size, using covariates, choosing among dependent variables, and reporting subsets of experimental conditions — the general category (any point where a "reasonable" researcher had more than one defensible option) is exactly the category a threshold value, an aggregation statistic, and a smoothing/window-size choice fall into — [Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn 2011](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797611417632)
- The specific statistical instance of "one threshold chosen, one reported, but the choice was still effectively a search" is directly and quantitatively confirmed in the biostatistics literature on "optimal cutpoints": Altman, Lausen, Sauerbrei, and Schumacher (1994, JNCI) showed that when a cutpoint is chosen to optimize the apparent association with outcome, "the false-positive rate is around 40% rather than the nominal 5%," "the P-value is far too small (P = 0.002 corresponds to a genuine P = 0.05)," and that this bias transfers into any subsequent multivariate model that includes the cutpoint-derived variable — [Altman et al. 1994, "Dangers of using 'optimal' cutpoints in the evaluation of prognostic factors," JNCI 86(11):829-835](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/86/11/829/955201) (DOI: 10.1093/jnci/86.11.829)
- PROBAST's own risk-of-bias signalling question 4.2 ("were continuous and categorical predictors handled appropriately?") explicitly names this exact failure mode: "if categorisation cut-off points are based on the development dataset, for example, by using methods to identify the optimal cut-off point, the model will be overfit and biased" — [PROBAST framework, signalling question 4.2, summarized via search of the PROBAST Explanation & Elaboration paper](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M18-1377)
- Multiverse analysis / specification curve analysis is the field's direct methodological response to this exact problem: it treats the set of "reasonable specifications" (which would include a set of reasonable thresholds, aggregations, and smoothing methods) as the object that should be reported, on the stated premise that "a researcher has many degrees of freedom when conducting a quantitative analysis of a data set" and that disclosing the whole set, not just the one chosen, is what restores honest inference — [Specification curve analysis and the multiverse, Statistical Modeling, Causal Inference and Social Science blog](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2024/11/12/specification-curve-analysis-and-the-multiverse/); [multiverse R package tutorial, Int J Psychology 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ijop.13229)

### Inferences
- Applied directly to this project's proposed torsion threshold: the fact that only one threshold/aggregation/smoothing combination would ever be reported does **not** exempt it from the forking-paths critique. The relevant question the literature asks is counterfactual: if the torsion values or the outcome data had come out slightly differently, would a different threshold, a different aggregation (mean vs max vs a quantile), or a different smoothing bandwidth have seemed equally reasonable and been chosen instead? Given that (per the project's own prior torsion notes) no study has ever established a "natural" torsion magnitude cutoff, a "natural" aggregation, or a "natural" smoothing bandwidth for coronary centerlines, the honest answer is yes — every one of those three choices is a fork, and each fork is a hidden comparison in Gelman and Loken's sense, regardless of researcher intent.
- The Altman et al. 1994 result is the sharpest, most quantitative anchor available: it shows this is not merely a philosophical concern but a measured ~8x inflation of the nominal false-positive rate (40% actual vs. 5% nominal) specifically for the "choose one cutpoint, report it as if pre-specified" pattern that a newly-invented torsion threshold would instantiate if the threshold were tuned by looking at how well it separates outcomes in this project's own data.
- Because PROBAST explicitly flags exactly this practice as a domain-4 (Analysis) risk-of-bias item, a torsion threshold constructed by inspecting outcome-vs-torsion relationships in the development cohort would very likely score "high risk of bias" on that PROBAST signalling question, independent of any biological argument for or against torsion itself.

### Gaps
- The primary Gelman & Loken PDF could not be machine-parsed into extractable prose in this session (it returned as an undecoded binary stream to the fetch tool); the findings above rely on convergent secondary/tertiary summaries (Wikipedia, FORRT glossary, Semantic Scholar abstract) rather than a directly quoted passage from the primary text. A researcher with direct PDF-reading tools should verify the exact wording before it is quoted verbatim in the thesis.
- No source located specifically enumerates "threshold + aggregation-statistic + smoothing-method" as a three-way combinatorial forking-paths example in print; the application of the general framework to this specific three-choice case is this note's own synthesis, not a directly citable worked example from the literature.

## How does adding a previously-excluded feature back via a new derived transformation (thresholding) interact with EPV / predictor-count accounting? Should the threshold consume an extra "degree of freedom" beyond the 1 parameter the thresholded value would occupy, and what do TRIPOD/PROBAST/general methodology recommend for disclosing this?

### Takeaway
The EPV/predictor-count literature and PROBAST both treat any data-dependent transformation choice as an additional source of optimism that is not captured merely by counting the final regression coefficient, and the specific sub-literature on cutpoint selection gives a direct, quantitative answer for the threshold case: choosing a cutpoint from the same data used to fit and evaluate the model requires either (a) a formal multiple-testing correction to the significance test used to justify the cutpoint, not just to the final coefficient's p-value, or (b) treating the cutpoint-derived variable's apparent performance as inherently optimistic and validating it out-of-sample. Neither TRIPOD nor PROBAST gives torsion-threshold-specific guidance (none exists, because this is a new, unpublished derived feature), but both frameworks' general machinery for "predictor handling" and "candidate predictor" counting applies directly.

### Cited Findings
- Already established in this project's prior research: candidate-predictor-parameter counting for EPV/sample-size purposes includes "any transformation or interaction term under consideration, not only final retained variables" — a 4-level categorical variable counts as 3 parameters, not 1 — [Statistical primer: sample size considerations for developing and validating clinical prediction models, PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/) (per prior note `clinical_prediction_methodology.md`). A hard threshold applied to a continuous variable is exactly this kind of transformation-under-consideration.
- Direct confirmation that a chosen cutpoint requires its own multiple-testing correction, separate from the coefficient's own significance test: "due to the multiple testing used to find the optimal cutpoint, an adjustment to the usual significance test is needed to preserve the type-I error rates" — [cutpoint selection literature synthesis, citing Cutpoint selection for discretizing a continuous covariate for GEE, PubMed 22639478](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22639478/)
- Faraggi and Simon (1996) specifically studied cross-validation as a correction method for the optimism induced by cutpoint selection in univariate survival analysis, i.e. the methodological literature treats "correct for the cutpoint search" as its own distinct problem from "correct for the number of predictors in the final model" — cited as reference 2 in Altman's follow-up letter on optimal cutpoints — [Altman follow-up letter, PMC2063091](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2063091/) (full text of the Faraggi/Simon paper itself was not independently fetched in this session; this is a secondary citation)
- PROBAST's Analysis domain (signalling question 4.2) states plainly that continuous predictors should not be dichotomised, and specifically that "if categorisation cut-off points are based on the development dataset... the model will be overfit and biased" — this is framed as a risk-of-bias item to be reported/assessed, not merely a stylistic preference — [PROBAST Explanation & Elaboration, summarized via search](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M18-1377)
- Royston, Altman, and Sauerbrei's widely cited methodology paper concludes dichotomizing continuous predictors in multiple regression is "a bad idea" generally (not limited to the optimal-cutpoint case), and the paper's abstract-level framing (title itself) is one of the most heavily cited statements of this position in the clinical prediction literature — [Royston, Altman, Sauerbrei 2006, "Dichotomizing continuous predictors in multiple regression: a bad idea," Statistics in Medicine 25:127-141](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sim.2331) / [PubMed 16217841](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16217841/) (the full abstract text could not be fetched directly in this session — 403 Forbidden from Wiley — so this is reported at the title/widely-cited-conclusion level, corroborated by multiple independent secondary sources, not from directly quoted abstract text)
- Frank Harrell's frequently cited applied-biostatistics guidance states categorization of continuous predictors "causes loss of power and precision of estimated means, odds, and hazards" and "assumes that the relationship between the predictor and response is flat within intervals," recommending restricted cubic splines instead, which "preserve the continuous nature of variables while still capturing complex relationships" without requiring a hard cut — [Problems Caused by Categorizing Continuous Variables, F. Harrell, summarized via search of Vanderbilt biostatistics materials](https://www.theanalysisfactor.com/problems-caused-by-categorizing-continuous-variables/)
- Already established in this project's prior research: this project's own Model 1 is already near the edge of what its expected sample size supports at 8 predictor parameters, and growing from 3 to 7-9 predictors is exactly the range where van Smeden et al.'s simulations show the required EPV must double (from 10 to 20) to control coefficient bias — [van Smeden et al., PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/) (per prior note `scalar_covariate_budget.md`)

### Inferences
- Formally counting "1 predictor parameter" for a thresholded torsion variable understates its true cost to the EPV budget if the threshold itself was chosen by looking at how well candidate thresholds separated outcomes in this cohort's data — the Altman et al. 1994 and cutpoint-selection literature treat that search as needing its own type-I-error correction, which is a cost over and above the coefficient's own degree of freedom. The honest accounting is not "torsion threshold = 1 parameter," but "torsion threshold = 1 fitted parameter plus an undisclosed number of implicit comparisons from the threshold search," and the size of that implicit cost depends entirely on how the threshold was constructed (see the third key question below for how to make that cost auditable/zero).
- Because PROBAST already treats outcome-informed cutpoint selection as a specific, named risk-of-bias item, a torsion threshold built this way would not just cost "extra EPV" in an abstract sense; it would independently fail a PROBAST domain-4 signalling question, which is a categorical (not merely a matter of degree) problem for a thesis chapter framed around fair, matched-settings comparison.
- The two literatures (EPV/predictor-counting and PROBAST/cutpoint-bias) point to the same practical rule: if a threshold construction method genuinely used no outcome information at all (see the "a priori rule" option below), it can be honestly counted as 1 predictor parameter, same as any other continuous-to-binary or continuous covariate. If it used outcome information in any way, including via "eyeballing" a plot of the candidate threshold against outcome before finalizing it, the 1-parameter accounting is optimistic and should be disclosed as such, or corrected via one of the specific mechanisms in the next section.

### Gaps
- No source was found giving a specific numeric "degrees of freedom penalty" to add for a single cutpoint search analogous to how spline degrees of freedom are counted (e.g., "a searched cutpoint costs X effective parameters"); the literature located here treats the fix as either (a) a formal significance-test correction at the point of cutpoint selection, or (b) full out-of-sample validation, rather than a fixed EPV penalty that could be added to this project's 8-parameter count as, e.g., "8 + 0.5". This means the project cannot currently produce a single revised EPV number for "Model 1 with a searched torsion threshold" from the literature; it can only state that the naive 1-parameter accounting is known to be an underestimate whenever the threshold was tuned on outcome data.
- Neither TRIPOD nor PROBAST has any threshold-specific or torsion-specific guidance (unsurprising, since this is a novel proposed feature, not a published practice); the applicable guidance is the general "handling continuous predictors" and "candidate predictor" machinery reviewed above, applied by inference, not a directly on-point published example.

## Is there specific guidance on treating a "resurrected" feature (excluded earlier for cause) that is later reconsidered with a new transformation? What is the preferred way to structure this so it does not silently become an undisclosed multiple-testing problem?

### Takeaway
No literature was found that names "resurrecting a previously excluded predictor via a new transformation" as its own labeled methodological scenario. However, three adjacent, well-established literatures converge on the same practical answer: (1) the forking-paths/pre-registration literature's general remedy — decide and disclose the analytical rule before looking at outcome data; (2) the confirmatory-vs-exploratory trial-methodology distinction, which requires that anything not locked in a statistical analysis plan (SAP) before database lock is, by definition, exploratory/post-hoc, however it is later dressed up; and (3) PROBAST/TRIPOD's general predictor-handling and transparency requirements. Together these imply the defensible options are: pre-register the exact threshold-construction rule before touching outcome data, use a fully independent calibration set the primary model's development/test split has never touched, or report it explicitly as a separate, clearly labeled exploratory/sensitivity analysis rather than folding it into the primary locked model.

### Cited Findings
- The forking-paths literature's own stated remedies (as consistently summarized) are pre-registering analysis plans before data collection, transparently reporting all analytical decisions, distinguishing exploratory from confirmatory research, and replicating findings in an independent sample — [Wikipedia summary of Gelman & Loken's recommendations, corroborated across search of the primary paper's reception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forking_paths_problem)
- In clinical-trial methodology, sensitivity analyses are only credible as robustness checks, not as a route to smuggle in a new result, when they are "completely pre-specified in the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) — at the latest before database lock and unblinding. Only pre-specified analyses can be considered part of the confirmatory analysis strategy; all others are classified as post-hoc and exploratory" — [search synthesis of SAP/sensitivity-analysis methodology, citing standard SAP practice](https://mediconomics.com/en/glossar/sensitivity-analysis/)
- Preregistration frameworks explicitly allow for "planned exploratory analyses (i.e., neither directional nor nondirectional a priori hypotheses have been formulated, but analyses are still planned)," which "have greater credibility than unplanned analyses because the preregistration specifies the number of statistical tests to be conducted" — directly supporting the idea that an exploratory torsion-threshold analysis can be done honestly, but only if its scope (including the space of candidate thresholds/aggregations considered) is fixed and disclosed in advance — [PCSAS, "Overcoming Challenges in Preregistration to Improve Statistical Inferences in Clinical Science," search synthesis](https://pcsas.org/newsletter/overcoming-challenges-in-preregistration-to-improve-statistical-inferences-in-clinical-science/)
- Already established in this project's prior research: no study anywhere links scalar or per-point torsion to a CAD outcome, and the one mechanistic pathway proposed (torsion drives helicity, which shapes WSS) explicitly failed to survive Bonferroni-Holm correction in a 2025 coronary-specific study (Shen et al., Royal Society Open Science) — [PMC12324882](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324882/) (per prior note `scalar_torsion.md`). This matters directly to this key question: because there is no prior external evidence pointing to where a torsion threshold "should" be, any threshold constructed by looking at this project's own outcome data is, by construction, a purely data-driven choice with no independent anchor to fall back on — it cannot claim the shelter of "confirming a value suggested by prior literature," unlike, e.g., the >80° bifurcation-angle threshold in Sun et al. 2017 which had at least one external clinical rationale (already noted in `torsion_helical_flow_wss.md`).
- General multiplicity guidance from the biostatistics literature reinforces that the number of implicit comparisons scales with the number of free choices in how a derived variable is constructed, not with how many are ultimately reported — this is the same principle stated in the cutpoint-selection literature (Altman et al. 1994; PROBAST 4.2) and in the multiverse/specification-curve literature (see first key question) applied here to the compound choice of threshold value + aggregation statistic + smoothing method as a single "how was this feature constructed" decision.

### Inferences
- Because torsion was already excluded from this project's model for cause (noise-sensitivity, no outcome evidence, contested mechanism), the burden of proof for reintroducing it is higher, not lower, than for an ordinary new candidate predictor: an ordinary candidate predictor consumes one EPV slot and is judged on its own predictive contribution; a "resurrected" predictor additionally needs its resurrection mechanism (the threshold) to be constructed in a way that does not quietly reopen the original exclusion decision through the back door. The three literatures above converge on the same structural answer regardless of which is emphasized: the threshold-construction rule must be fixed before it is allowed to see outcome data from the cohort it will be evaluated on.
- The cleanest way to operationalize "independent calibration" in this project's own data structure (per the project's own train/val/test split conventions already documented in CLAUDE.md/CLAUDE.thesis.md) would be to fix the torsion-threshold rule using only the training/validation split's non-outcome information (e.g., a distributional property of training-set torsion values, or a value taken from an entirely separate published dataset) and never let the test-split outcome labels touch the threshold-construction step at all — this is a direct application of "independent calibration set" without requiring new data collection, though it does consume some of the value of the val split.
- Explicitly labeling a torsion-threshold analysis as a separate exploratory/sensitivity analysis, reported alongside but not folded into, the primary locked 8-parameter Model 1, is the option best supported by the trial-methodology SAP literature and is also the option that best matches this project's own stated principle (see final key question) that every reported number needs a reproducible, non-ad-hoc code path — an exploratory analysis with its own explicit, disclosed construction rule satisfies that principle even if the primary model does not include torsion at all.

### Gaps
- No paper or guideline was found that names this exact scenario ("previously excluded predictor, later resurrected via a new transformation") as a labeled methodological category with its own recommended checklist; the guidance assembled above is a synthesis across three adjacent literatures (forking-paths, SAP/exploratory-vs-confirmatory trial methodology, and cutpoint-bias/PROBAST) rather than a single directly-on-point citation. This should be reported to the thesis as reasoned synthesis, not as an established named practice.
- No search was run in this session specifically for "feature resurrection" or "predictor reintroduction" as literal search terms in the clinical-prediction-modeling literature; given the very specific and unusual framing, it is plausible (though not confirmed) that no literature uses this framing at all, since most published prediction-model papers only ever report their final locked variable list rather than the sequence of earlier exclusion-then-reconsideration decisions that produced it.

## Given this project's own reproducibility principle, what would a reproducible, non-cherry-picked implementation of a torsion threshold look like structurally?

### Takeaway
The literature supports a clear ordering of three structural options by defensibility, from the framing already implicit in the EPV/PROBAST/forking-paths material reviewed above (no single paper proposes exactly this three-way ranking for torsion specifically, so the ranking itself is this project's synthesis): (1) a threshold fixed by an a priori rule stated and justified before any model fitting on this cohort's outcome data is the most defensible and lowest-cost option; (2) a threshold or smooth transform learned as part of the model itself (e.g., a spline term, or a soft/sigmoid gate with a fitted midpoint), evaluated with proper internal validation, is the second most defensible because the "search" is folded into the model-fitting machinery that EPV/optimism-correction already accounts for, rather than being a separate, hidden pre-processing step; (3) a threshold chosen by an outcome-informed grid search, even a cross-validated one restricted to a nested inner loop, is the weakest option for a cohort this small, because nested CV controls optimism in the performance *estimate* but does not remove the extra effective degrees of freedom the search itself consumes, and van Smeden/Riley's EPV machinery (already established in this project's prior research) was never calibrated to a workflow that includes a hyperparameter search step at all.

### Cited Findings
- Nested cross-validation's documented purpose is specifically to prevent the *performance estimate* from leaking information about the held-out test fold during hyperparameter/threshold search: "every outer fold gets its own hyperparameter search using only the training data from that fold... this strict separation eliminates test-set information leakage into model selection, thereby preventing overoptimistic error estimates" — [search synthesis of nested cross-validation methodology](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/nested-cross-validation-methods); consistent with [nestedcv R package documentation, PMC10125905](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10125905/)
- Nested CV's stated benefit is an honest *evaluation* of a model-plus-selection-procedure, not a guarantee that the selection procedure consumes no extra effective degrees of freedom relative to a fixed rule — the literature located here frames it as solving the leakage/optimism-in-the-estimate problem, which is a different problem from the EPV/sample-size-adequacy problem (already established in this project's prior research, where required EPV rises with the number of parameters/choices under consideration, not just with what is ultimately reported) — [PMC5045274, van Smeden et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/) (per prior note `clinical_prediction_methodology.md`), synthesized against the nested-CV sources above by this researcher, since no single source directly compares the two frameworks.
- The spline/smooth-transform alternative to a hard threshold is directly recommended in the dichotomization literature reviewed above: Royston, Altman, and Sauerbrei's paper is specifically framed against dichotomization and (per its widely cited conclusion) recommends retaining the continuous form or using flexible functions (e.g., fractional polynomials) instead of a cutpoint — [Royston, Altman, Sauerbrei 2006](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sim.2331); Harrell's parallel recommendation of restricted cubic splines makes the same point — [Harrell, Problems Caused by Categorizing Continuous Variables](https://www.theanalysisfactor.com/problems-caused-by-categorizing-continuous-variables/)
- PROBAST's own signalling question 4.2 gives an explicit, direct criterion for judging the a priori vs. outcome-informed distinction: whether "categorisation cut-off points are based on the development dataset" — a threshold set from a rule with no reference to this cohort's outcome data (e.g., a physical/geometric noise floor, a value taken from an external published reference range, or a fixed round-number convention stated and justified in the methods before fitting) would not trip this signalling question; a threshold tuned against this cohort's own outcome labels, cross-validated or not, would — [PROBAST Explanation & Elaboration, summarized via search](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M18-1377)
- Already established in this project's prior research: this exact "fixed by external/mechanistic rule vs. tuned on the model's own data" distinction has a concrete positive precedent and a concrete negative precedent already surfaced in the torsion/WSS research: the >80° bifurcation-angle cutoff in Sun et al. (2017) had an external clinical/predictive rationale (best sensitivity/specificity for stenosis in an independent patient series), whereas Altman et al.'s "optimal cutpoint" critique targets exactly the alternative pattern (a cutpoint chosen to maximize apparent association within the same dataset being analyzed) — [Sun et al., PMC5682403](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5682403/) (per prior note `torsion_helical_flow_wss.md`); [Altman et al. 1994](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/86/11/829/955201)

### Inferences
- Mapped onto this project's three named implementation options: "threshold fixed by an a priori rule stated and justified before any model fitting" is the option that would not trip PROBAST 4.2 and would not add any hidden forking-paths cost, provided the rule is genuinely fixed before looking at this cohort's outcome data (e.g., a noise-floor derived from a bench/phantom or from the torsion-estimator's own documented numerical instability threshold, independent of this cohort's CAD labels) and provided the aggregation statistic and smoothing method are likewise fixed by the same kind of independent, pre-specified rule, not chosen by whichever combination looks best on this data.
- "Threshold learned as a smooth/soft parameter within the model itself" (e.g., a fitted spline knot, or a sigmoid gate whose midpoint is a model parameter estimated jointly with the regression coefficients) is defensible specifically because it removes the separate, hidden "which threshold did we pick" decision entirely — the model-fitting machinery that TRIPOD's optimism-correction and this project's own EPV accounting already assume (one set of parameters, fit once, optimism-corrected by bootstrap) directly covers this case, unlike a pre-processing threshold search that sits outside that machinery and is invisible to standard EPV counting.
- "Threshold chosen by a cross-validated search over a candidate grid" is the weakest of the three for this project's cohort size specifically because of the interaction with the already-tight 8-parameter budget: nested CV requires enough data to support an inner loop for threshold selection *and* an outer loop for honest evaluation, on top of an already EPV-constrained modest cohort (per `scalar_covariate_budget.md`'s finding that this cohort may already sit below the EPV=20 needed for 7-9 predictors) — spending scarce events on an inner-loop threshold search compounds, rather than relieves, that constraint, even though nested CV would correctly report the resulting optimism once done.
- Across all three options, disclosure is what the literature converges on regardless of which structural choice is made: TRIPOD's reporting requirements, PROBAST's signalling questions, and the forking-paths/multiverse literature all point to the same minimum bar — state explicitly, in the methods, exactly how the threshold/aggregation/smoothing choice was made, whether it used any outcome information, and if it is reported as part of the primary locked model or as a separate exploratory/sensitivity analysis. None of the three structural options is exempt from this disclosure requirement; disclosure is necessary but not sufficient, since PROBAST would still flag an outcome-informed cutpoint as high risk of bias even if the paper discloses exactly how it was chosen.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a worked, quantitative comparison of "EPV cost of an a-priori-fixed threshold" vs. "EPV cost of a nested-CV-searched threshold" vs. "EPV cost of a smooth/spline-learned threshold" for a single case study — the ranking above is this researcher's synthesis from the separate literatures (EPV/PROBAST, dichotomization-vs-splines, nested CV), not a single paper's head-to-head comparison. If this exact comparison is needed at high confidence for the thesis, it may need to be established via the project's own simulation on its own cohort size rather than found in the literature.
- No source was found addressing whether a threshold set from a purely physical/instrumental noise-floor argument (as opposed to a clinical/outcome-referenced external value like the bifurcation-angle precedent) would be judged equally "pre-specified and outcome-independent" by PROBAST reviewers in practice; this is inferred by this researcher from PROBAST's stated criterion ("based on the development dataset" as the trigger condition), not confirmed by a worked PROBAST-assessed example of a noise-floor-derived cutpoint specifically.
