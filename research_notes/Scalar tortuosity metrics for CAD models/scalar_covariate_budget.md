# Multicollinearity and sample-size budget for a growing scalar covariate list in Model 1 (logistic regression) vs Model 2 (small NN)

Scope note: this file builds directly on two prior notes already in this repo -
`research_notes/Tortuosity feature count for CAD prediction/clinical_prediction_methodology.md`
(EPV/TRIPOD/PROBAST, interpretability) and
`.../regularization_correlated_features.md` (LASSO/elastic net/VIF/PCA behaviour on correlated
imaging features), plus the curvature-redundancy finding already recorded in
`.../multi_metric_tortuosity_evidence.md` (Kashyap et al. 2022, mean absolute curvature vs RMS
curvature). This note does not re-derive those findings; it extends them to the specific
question of budgeting a *growing* Model 1 predictor list and comparing that budget to Model 2's.

## Key Question 1: Concrete EPV arithmetic as Model 1 grows from 3 predictors to 7-9, for a cohort in the hundreds - where does this become unsafe?

### Takeaway
The most directly on-point number already surfaced in this project's own prior research is van Smeden et al.'s simulation finding that EPV=10 controls coefficient bias for 3- and 5-predictor models but EPV=20 is required to reach the same low-bias level once the model has 7, 10, or 12 predictors - i.e., growing Model 1 from 3 to 7-9 predictors is exactly the transition the literature identifies as requiring a doubling of the events-per-variable target, and a separate worked sample-size table (Riley/pmsampsize-style) shows the same doubling pattern in absolute event counts as predictor-parameter count rises from 15 to 25. No source gives a single bright-line "predictor count X is where it becomes unsafe" independent of the cohort's event count and outcome prevalence; the safe/unsafe boundary is a function of (predictor count x target EPV) versus (actual events in hand), not of predictor count alone.

### Cited Findings
- Already established in this project's prior research: at EPV=10, 3- and 5-predictor logistic models had low coefficient bias, but 7-, 10-, and 12-predictor models required EPV=20 to reach the same low-bias level; at EPV=10, external validation performance for the larger models was "clearly a poor choice" — [van Smeden et al., "Adequate sample size for developing prediction models is not simply related to events per variable," PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/) (per prior note `clinical_prediction_methodology.md`)
- At EPV=2 with a 12-predictor model, only 7 of 1,000 simulated models even converged — an illustration of the failure mode at the low end, cited to bound how bad "unsafe" can get, not as a claim that 7-9 predictors approaches EPV=2 — [PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)
- A separate worked example (pmsampsize-style calculation for a 90-day mortality prediction model, 3% event prevalence, targeting shrinkage >=0.9) gives a directly quotable table of how minimum required sample size and required events scale with predictor-parameter count: 25 parameters -> 9,658 minimum sample size / 290 required events; 20 parameters -> 7,726 / 232; 18 parameters -> 6,954 / 209; 15 parameters -> 5,795 / 174 — [Statistical primer: sample size considerations for developing and validating clinical prediction models, PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- That table shows the required-events figure scales roughly linearly with predictor-parameter count in this specific worked example (about 11.6 events required per predictor parameter, holding the 3% prevalence and 0.9 shrinkage target fixed): 290/25 = 11.6, 232/20 = 11.6, 209/18 = 11.6, 174/15 = 11.6 — an internally consistent per-parameter event requirement for that outcome prevalence and shrinkage target specifically (not a universal constant; it would shift with a different prevalence or shrinkage target) — [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- The same primer states that predictor-parameter counting includes every level of a categorical variable beyond the reference category, and any transformation or interaction term under consideration, not only final retained variables — e.g., a 4-level categorical variable (NYHA I-IV) contributes 3 predictor parameters, not 1 — [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- Already established in this project's prior research: reducing candidate predictors from 25 to 15 lowered the required minimum sample size from 9,658 to 5,795 at a fixed target of 174 events — the same worked example, cited previously for its headline number — [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/) (per prior note `clinical_prediction_methodology.md`)
- Steyerberg et al. (secondary-source figure, already in this project's prior research) found roughly 10-20 EPV needed to keep shrinkage above 0.9, but roughly 20 EPV needed for an accurate estimate of the c-statistic itself — [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/) (per prior note)
- The classic "1 variable per 10 events" criterion itself has been directly challenged as lacking a strong empirical rationale, and van Smeden's own later work argues that out-of-sample predictive performance (root Mean Squared Prediction Error) depends jointly on predictor count, total sample size, and event fraction, not on EPV as a single ratio — [No rationale for 1 variable per 10 events criterion for binary logistic regression analysis, PMC5122171](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5122171/); [van Smeden et al. 2019, "Sample size for binary logistic prediction models: beyond events per variable criteria," Statistical Methods in Medical Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0962280218784726)

### Inferences
- Applying the van Smeden 3-to-7/10/12-predictor bias finding directly to Model 1's stated growth path (3 predictors: curvature/tortuosity mean + age + sex, growing toward 7-9 with one or two more curvature-family metrics, 1-3 bifurcation angle scalars, dominance, Horton-Strahler ratio, and/or torsion): the growth from 3 to 7-9 predictors is precisely the range in which the literature says the *required* EPV doubles from 10 to 20. Concretely, if the original 3-predictor model was designed to a 10-EPV standard (30 events required), simply adding 4-6 more predictors without re-budgeting means the same 30-event cohort that was adequate at EPV=10/3-predictors is now only EPV=3.3-4.3 for a 7-9-predictor model - well inside the range (EPV=2-5) where van Smeden's simulations show severe bias and even non-convergence in a meaningful fraction of resamples. To stay at the literature's own recommended EPV=20 for a 7-9-predictor model requires 140-180 events, i.e., roughly 4.7-6x more events than the EPV=10/3-predictor budget (30 events) that may have justified the original design.
- Using the pmsampsize-style worked table's internally consistent ~11.6 events-per-parameter ratio (which is specific to that example's 3% prevalence and 0.9-shrinkage target, so it should be treated as illustrative of the scaling pattern, not transplanted numerically onto a CAD cohort with a different, likely much higher, event prevalence) as a sanity check: growing from 3 to 9 predictor parameters at a similar per-parameter requirement implies needing roughly 3x more events than a 3-predictor model at the same target, consistent in direction (though not magnitude, since CAD-cohort event rates are typically far higher than 3%) with the van Smeden EPV-doubling result above.
- Whether "the point at which this predictor count becomes clearly unsafe" has been crossed cannot be stated as a fixed predictor number without the cohort's actual event count and outcome prevalence, because both cited sources make the required sample size a function of (predictors x target EPV) versus events in hand, not of predictor count in isolation. For a cohort "in the hundreds" with a CAD-positive fraction anywhere in the range typical of CCTA-referred symptomatic cohorts (commonly 30-60% prevalence in this literature domain per the outcome-evidence note's angiography cohorts), 100-300 events would be available; per the EPV=20 target for 7-9 predictors (140-180 events needed), a cohort with fewer than roughly 150-180 CAD-positive cases is already in the zone the literature flags as producing biased coefficients and optimistic apparent performance for a 7-9-predictor Model 1, even though that same event count would be comfortably adequate (EPV well above 20) for the original 3-predictor design.
- Every categorical expansion counts against this budget: dominance, if coded as a 3-level categorical (right/left/co-dominant) rather than a binary right-vs-non-right variable, contributes 2 predictor parameters instead of 1, which by itself is equivalent in EPV terms to adding a whole extra continuous predictor - this is a concrete, avoidable way the candidate list could be quietly larger than "7-9" suggests.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a sample-size or EPV table computed specifically for a CAD-imaging cohort's typical event prevalence (as opposed to the 3% low-prevalence mortality example or generic EPV simulations); the concrete numbers above (11.6 events/parameter, 140-180 events for EPV=20 at 7-9 predictors) should be treated as illustrating the scaling pattern and the EPV-doubling threshold, not as a plug-in formula for this thesis's actual cohort without running the actual event count and prevalence through a pmsampsize-style calculation.
- I did not locate a source stating a single universal "maximum safe predictor count" for a several-hundred-patient cohort; per the Riley/van Smeden literature already cited in this project's prior research, that number is only well-defined once outcome prevalence, target shrinkage, and anticipated model R^2/C-statistic are specified.

## Key Question 2: Prune one of a correlated pair (mean absolute curvature vs RMS curvature) to make room in the predictor budget, versus keep both and rely on regularization - which is preferred in the small-sample literature?

### Takeaway
The literature converges on pruning (or clinically-guided combining) one of a highly correlated pair *before* model development as the mainstream, explicitly stated preference in small-sample clinical prediction modeling, with regularization (elastic net especially) recommended as a secondary safeguard on the reduced set rather than as a substitute for pruning; a direct, named example in the methodology literature is "not including both total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol." This is consistent with, and sharpens, this project's own prior finding that plain LASSO handles correlated predictor pairs unstably and that elastic net's "grouping effect" is the standard fix for *retaining* correlated predictors when both must be kept - but the newer material below shows the field's default guidance is still to prune first when there is a competing, less-correlated predictor domain waiting for room in the budget.

### Cited Findings
- "Exclude predictors that are highly correlated with others (for example, not including both total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol)" is stated as explicit guidance for handling multicollinearity, in the context of a broader recommendation for a "full model approach" in which predictors are pre-specified by clinical experts before model fitting rather than selected by a data-driven stepwise procedure — [Shipe et al., "Developing prediction models for clinical use using logistic regression: an overview," Journal of Thoracic Disease](https://jtd.amegroups.org/article/view/26585/html)
- The same source explains the mechanism for why pruning (not just adding regularization) is preferred: "Two variables that are highly correlated with one another may be individually correlated to the outcome; however, when combined in a multivariable model each becomes a weaker predictor" - i.e., keeping both correlated predictors together in an unregularized or lightly regularized model dilutes both coefficients' individual signal rather than adding information — [Shipe et al., Journal of Thoracic Disease](https://jtd.amegroups.org/article/view/26585/html)
- The same source explicitly favors "clinically-driven predictor identification" (pre-specification by domain experts, including deciding which of a correlated pair to keep) over purely "data-driven identification," on the grounds that pre-specification avoids predictor-selection bias and overfitting — [Shipe et al., Journal of Thoracic Disease](https://jtd.amegroups.org/article/view/26585/html)
- Already established in this project's prior research: LASSO handles correlated feature groups poorly and unstably (arbitrarily retaining one member of a correlated cluster across resamples), and elastic net's grouping effect (Zou & Hastie 2005) is the standard fix specifically for the case where correlated predictors should be kept together rather than pruned — [Zou & Hastie 2005, J R Statist Soc B](https://academic.oup.com/jrsssb/article/67/2/301/7109482) (per prior note `regularization_correlated_features.md`)
- Already established in this project's prior research: penalization/shrinkage methods themselves "produced unreliable clinical prediction models, especially when sample size was small," meaning regularization is not a costless substitute for reducing the predictor count in genuinely small cohorts — [Journal of Clinical Epidemiology, cited in prior note `clinical_prediction_methodology.md`](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(20)31209-9/fulltext)
- Already established in this project's prior research: the standard two-step pipeline in radiomics/imaging methodology is (1) correlation-based redundancy pruning first (drop or cluster features above a stated threshold, commonly 0.7-0.95 Pearson/Spearman), then (2) LASSO/elastic net on the reduced set - i.e., pruning-then-regularizing is the field's default sequence, not regularizing-in-place-of-pruning — [per prior note `regularization_correlated_features.md`, citing PMC9860004 and the PET radiomics multicollinearity study PubMed 40332568]
- Already established in this project's prior research: the two coronary tortuosity papers that report multiple correlated geometric metrics side by side (including Kashyap et al. 2022 itself, source of the mean-absolute-curvature/RMS-curvature redundancy flag) resolve redundancy by recommending a single "winning" metric (average absolute curvature) for future use, rather than combining the correlated metrics in one jointly regularized model - i.e., the one directly relevant vascular-geometry precedent in this specific sub-field itself chose pruning over joint retention — [Kashyap et al. 2022, Scientific Reports, PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/) (per prior note `multi_metric_tortuosity_evidence.md`)

### Inferences
- Combining the general clinical-prediction-methodology guidance (Shipe et al.: prune one of a correlated pair before adding predictors) with the domain-specific precedent (Kashyap et al. 2022's own recommendation to standardize on one curvature metric) gives converging, independent support for reducing mean absolute curvature and RMS curvature to one before adding new candidates (bifurcation angle, dominance, Horton-Strahler ratio, torsion) - this is not just a generic EPV-budget argument but matches how the one paper that established the mean-absolute-curvature/RMS-curvature redundancy in the first place chose to resolve it in its own recommendations.
- The "keep both and regularize" alternative is best framed as a fallback for cases where there is no principled basis to prefer one correlated predictor over the other (e.g., truly unknown which of two engineering choices for the same underlying construct is superior) - that is not the situation here, since Kashyap et al. 2022 already reports average absolute curvature as the stronger, more normally-distributed, and more literature-recommended of the two metrics relative to a mechanistic outcome (TAWSS); the pruning decision therefore has an evidentiary basis specific to this exact pair, not just a generic correlation-reduction rationale.
- Given the "regularization is not a free pass in small samples" finding already in this project's prior research, choosing to keep both curvature metrics on the argument that "elastic net will handle it" is the weaker-supported path in the literature specifically for cohorts in the hundreds - elastic net's grouping-effect benefit is best evidenced in larger-sample, higher-dimensional radiomics-style settings, not the small, hand-picked feature set scenario described in this task.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a numeric correlation-coefficient threshold specific to justify pruning mean absolute curvature vs RMS curvature by name (only the general 0.7-0.95 radiomics-style thresholds already noted in prior research, and Kashyap et al.'s R^2-against-TAWSS comparison rather than a direct pairwise correlation between the two curvature metrics themselves - already flagged as a gap in the prior note `multi_metric_tortuosity_evidence.md` and not resolved in this pass).
- I did not find a source that frames this specific choice ("prune to make budget room for a new predictor domain" vs "keep both and regularize") as a named, debated methodological question in its own right, with authors on both sides; the "prune first" position is the one stated affirmatively and repeatedly across sources, and no counter-source explicitly arguing "keep both correlated predictors and let elastic net decide, in a small clinical cohort" was located.

## Key Question 3: Does the safe predictor-count answer differ between Model 1 (logistic regression) and Model 2 (small neural network)? Is more permissiveness justified for Model 2's scalar covariates, and by how much?

### Takeaway
Yes, but the literature's support for being more permissive with Model 2 is much softer and less quantified than the EPV literature underpinning Model 1: neural-network sample-size heuristics exist (a classical "10x the number of weights" rule, and a more conservative "50x" rule from at least one applied paper), but they are folklore-grade rules of thumb rather than a body of simulation-validated guidance comparable to Riley/van Smeden's EPV work, and none of the sources found translate directly into a "how many extra scalar covariates is Model 2 allowed" number the way EPV directly answers that question for Model 1. The directionally correct justification for permissiveness is that a few added scalar covariates at the fusion/top layer of a small network add only a small, easily-budgeted number of new weights, in contrast to expanding per-point profile channels (which multiplies parameters through the whole preceding pathway) - this is consistent with, and should be read as extending, this project's own prior finding that Model 2's scalar-covariate fusion is not parameter-sensitive the way its profile-channel input is.

### Cited Findings
- A classical neural-network training heuristic ("Uncle Bernie's Rule," attributed to Bernard Widrow) states that training sample size should be roughly ten times the number of weights in the network — [search-synthesized attribution of Widrow's rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Widrow) (attribution surfaced via search synthesis, not independently confirmed against a primary Widrow publication in this session - treat the specific "10x" figure and its attribution as moderate-confidence folklore rather than a peer-reviewed simulation result)
- A more conservative alternative figure appears in at least one applied paper on artificial neural networks for discrete choice analysis: a minimum sample size of fifty times the number of network weights is recommended by that paper's authors, explicitly framed as more conservative than "the rule often used in the ANN community, which advises at least ten times the number of weights" — [Is your dataset big enough? Sample size requirements when using artificial neural networks for discrete choice analysis, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1755534518300058) (sourced via search-result synthesis; the paper's full text was not independently fetched in this session, so the exact "50x" figure and its stated rationale should be verified against the primary text before being reported as a precise citable number in the thesis)
- Separately, a widely cited empirical observation in the neural-network generalization literature is that "when the ratio of training sample size to the number of weights is larger than 30, overfitting will not occur" is a commonly accepted rule of thumb, but that even at a ratio greater than 30 the same literature source found overfitting can still occur - directly illustrating that, unlike EPV for logistic regression, no single samples-per-weight ratio for neural networks has been simulation-validated as reliably sufficient — [search synthesis of Amari-era neural network generalization literature, referencing Amari et al.'s "Asymptotic Statistical Theory of Overtraining and Cross-Validation"](https://mathsci.kaist.ac.kr/~nipl/mas557/Amari_97.pdf) (the "ratio > 30" claim and its refutation were retrieved via search-result synthesis rather than a direct fetch of the primary Amari paper in this session; flagged as moderate-confidence pending direct verification)
- For small sample sizes specifically (search-synthesized range: 20 to 1,000 samples), one source explicitly recommends favoring high-inductive-bias, interpretable models such as naive Bayes or elastic net over neural networks altogether, rather than attempting to fit even a small neural network — [search synthesis on small-sample tabular modeling, GCondNet-adjacent literature](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.06302v4) (this specific recommendation was returned via the search-summarization step; a direct fetch of the GCondNet paper's own text did not surface an explicit "d <= m/50" or equivalent stated rule, so this range and its exact source should be treated as unverified pending a closer read of whichever paper the search engine drew it from)
- A first-hidden-layer parameter-concentration effect is documented directly in the fetched GCondNet paper text: for tabular data with many input features, the first hidden layer of a standard MLP can contain the large majority of the network's total parameters (the paper's own genomics example: 98% of parameters in the first layer of a five-layer, 100-neurons-per-layer MLP on a ~20,000-feature dataset), and this concentration of learnable capacity in the first layer "contributes to overfitting of neural networks on high-dimensional, small sample datasets" — [GCondNet: A Novel Method for Improving Neural Networks on Small High-Dimensional Tabular Data, arXiv:2211.06302](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.06302v4) (this specific point was independently confirmed via direct fetch of the paper's text, unlike the "d<=m/50" figure above)

### Inferences
- The GCondNet first-layer-parameter-concentration finding, read directly rather than through search synthesis, gives the clearest available mechanistic justification (though not a numeric one) for this project's prior finding that Model 2's scalar-covariate count is not very parameter-sensitive while its per-point profile channel count is: parameter count in a standard MLP scales with the *width of the input layer*, so if scalar covariates are fused at or near the top of the network (a late-fusion design, adding only a handful of extra input units to a layer that is already narrow relative to the profile-encoding pathway), each added scalar covariate contributes far fewer new weights than an added per-point input channel would, which must pass its width through every layer of a deeper encoding pathway. This is a structural/architectural argument, not a simulation-validated numeric threshold in the same sense as van Smeden's EPV work.
- Because none of the neural-network sample-size heuristics found (10x, 30x, 50x weights) have been validated with the same simulation rigor as the EPV literature (van Smeden's controlled bias/convergence simulations across explicit predictor counts and EPV levels), it would be a citation-quality mismatch to present a specific "Model 2 can tolerate N more scalar covariates than Model 1" number; the honest position is that Model 2's tolerance is plausibly higher per added scalar covariate (because of the late-fusion architecture argument above) but this is qualitative/architectural reasoning, not a quantified budget the way EPV quantifies Model 1's budget.
- A practical, defensible way to operationalize "how much more permissive" without overclaiming precision: count the actual number of new weights each candidate scalar covariate adds at the fusion layer (typically fusion-layer-width x 1, i.e., a small, fixed, computable number per covariate for a late-fusion design) and compare that against whichever samples-per-weight heuristic is used (10x-50x), which will typically show that 5-6 added scalar covariates are a trivial fraction of Model 2's total weight budget even under the most conservative (50x) heuristic - but this arithmetic should be done concretely for Model 2's actual architecture rather than asserted in the abstract.

### Gaps
- No source was found giving a simulation-based (as opposed to folklore/heuristic) sample-size-per-parameter rule for small feed-forward neural networks analogous to van Smeden's EPV simulations for logistic regression; this confirms and extends this project's own prior finding that no exact neural-network parameter-budget rule comparable to EPV exists in the literature searched.
- The "50x weights" and "naive Bayes/elastic net preferred over NNs below ~1,000 samples" figures were both returned only via search-engine synthesis rather than confirmed by direct fetch of their apparent primary sources in this session (the ScienceDirect discrete-choice paper was paywalled/not fetched; the GCondNet direct fetch did not itself contain the small-sample range or "d<=m/50" claim attributed to it by the search synthesis). Both numbers should be independently verified before being reported as precise, citable figures in the thesis; they are included here as the best available leads rather than confirmed facts.
- No source was found specifically modeling the "late-fusion architecture reduces per-covariate parameter cost" argument in the exact terms used above (scalar tabular covariates fused near the output vs. per-point sequence channels multiplying through a deeper pathway) as an explicit, named principle in the neural-network methodology literature; the inference above is this researcher's synthesis of the GCondNet first-layer-concentration finding applied to Model 2's architecture, not a directly cited claim.

## Key Question 4: Is there a principled ordering for which scalar candidates to add first when the predictor budget is limited - cross-domain over same-domain stacking?

### Takeaway
The clearest, most directly stated guidance found is Shipe et al.'s "clinically-driven predictor identification" principle (pre-specify predictors by domain/clinical relevance, exclude one of any highly-correlated pair such as total cholesterol vs LDL cholesterol) combined with the PROBAST/TRIPOD emphasis on pre-specification over data-driven selection; neither source states the "cross-domain beats same-domain" heuristic in those exact words, so this remains this project's own synthesis (already established in prior research per the objective's framing) rather than a directly quotable rule from the literature - but the redundancy-pruning guidance and the multicollinearity-diagnosis literature both point toward the same practical ordering: remove within-domain redundancy first, because a same-domain addition (a second curvature metric) is mechanically more likely to be highly correlated with an existing predictor than a cross-domain addition (an angle, a topology ratio, a categorical variable) is, and highly correlated additions are the specific case every source flags as adding little independent information while still consuming EPV budget.

### Cited Findings
- Shipe et al.'s "full model approach" recommends predictors be "pre-specified by clinical experts in the field" (clinically-driven identification) as the preferred alternative to data-driven/stepwise selection, and its worked example of excluding one of a correlated pair (total cholesterol vs LDL cholesterol) is itself a same-domain (both lipid measures) redundancy the authors resolve by pruning, before any discussion of adding predictors from other domains — [Shipe et al., Journal of Thoracic Disease](https://jtd.amegroups.org/article/view/26585/html)
- Already established in this project's prior research: PROBAST's Analysis domain explicitly flags "data-driven predictor selection procedures performed without pre-specification" as a source of inflated/optimistic performance, and separately instructs that highly correlated (collinear) predictors "should be identified and appropriately handled during model development" - i.e., collinearity-handling is treated as a precondition of sound analysis, prior to and independent of how many predictors are ultimately included — [PROBAST explanation and elaboration, per prior note `regularization_correlated_features.md`]
- Already established in this project's prior research: mechanistically, two curvature-family metrics (mean absolute curvature, RMS curvature) are shown by Kashyap et al. 2022 to be closely related descriptors of the same underlying vessel-bending construct (both derived from the same curvature signal along the centerline, differing only in how they aggregate it - mean-absolute vs root-mean-square), whereas a bifurcation angle, the Horton-Strahler branching ratio, and coronary dominance are geometrically and conceptually distinct constructs (local branch-point angle, tree-topology branching pattern, and categorical anatomical variant respectively) with no shared mathematical derivation - supporting the general expectation (though not a directly cited correlation figure) that a second curvature metric is more likely to be highly correlated with the first than an angle, topology, or categorical addition would be — [Kashyap et al. 2022, PMC8764056, per prior notes `multi_metric_tortuosity_evidence.md` and `Choosing a coronary tortuosity descriptor/outcome_evidence.md`]
- Already established in this project's prior research: in the Edinburgh Type 2 Diabetes Study, adding a second geometric metric (fractal dimension) on top of a first (venular tortuosity) gave no additional discrimination benefit (C-statistic 0.624 to 0.640 for the first addition, no further gain from the second), a concrete empirical instance (albeit retinal, not coronary) of a same-general-domain second geometric addition failing to pay for its EPV cost — [Retinal venular tortuosity and fractal dimension predict incident retinopathy, PMC8012328](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8012328/) (per prior note `multi_metric_tortuosity_evidence.md`; flagged there as sourced via search-engine excerpt, not independently fetched)
- Already established in this project's prior research: the retinal CAD-detection study's large discrimination gain (AUROC 0.694 to 0.802) was driven mainly by adding clinical/traditional risk factors on top of a geometric-feature-only model, not by adding more geometric metrics to each other - a concrete instance of a genuinely cross-domain addition (clinical risk factors vs imaging geometry) outperforming further same-domain geometric stacking — [Retinal vascular phenotyping for early detection of CAD, PMC13052816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13052816/) (per prior note `multi_metric_tortuosity_evidence.md`)

### Inferences
- No source found states the "prioritize cross-domain predictors over same-domain stacking" rule as a named, general principle in the EPV/TRIPOD/PROBAST or radiomics-methodology literature; it should be reported as this project's own synthesis, supported by (a) the mechanistic redundancy argument (same-domain metrics share a derivation and are more likely to be collinear, consuming EPV budget for little independent information) and (b) the two concrete empirical precedents above (Edinburgh T2D's null second-geometric-metric result; the retinal CAD study's cross-domain gain), rather than as a directly citable rule from a named source.
- Given that framing, a defensible ordering for Model 1's candidate list, in terms of expected information gain per EPV unit spent, is: (1) reduce mean absolute curvature/RMS curvature to one (removes redundant same-domain within-pair correlation at zero information cost, per Kashyap et al. 2022's own recommendation, Key Question 2 above); (2) add predictors from domains with no existing representation in the model at all (bifurcation angle domain, branching-topology domain via Horton-Strahler ratio, categorical-anatomic domain via dominance) ahead of any further same-domain geometric refinement; (3) treat torsion, as another same general "local shape of the centerline" domain adjacent to curvature, with the same suspicion of within-domain redundancy applied to the curvature pair, until its correlation with the retained curvature metric is checked.
- This ordering is consistent with, and should be read as a direct application of, the "different information domains" framing already used elsewhere in this project's prior research (the retinal CAD study's clinical-plus-imaging combination outperforming imaging-only stacking) - the pattern generalizes from "imaging vs clinical" to "curvature-family vs angle vs topology vs categorical" as domains within the imaging side of the feature list.

### Gaps
- No coronary-specific or general clinical-prediction-methodology source was found that states a formal, citable "add across domains before adding within domain" rule; this entire key question's positive answer rests on synthesis from adjacent, indirectly relevant findings (redundancy-pruning guidance, the Edinburgh T2D null result, the retinal cross-domain gain) rather than a single paper making this exact argument, and should be presented in the thesis as reasoned synthesis, not as a directly quoted methodological rule.
- No study was found that ranks candidate predictor domains (angle vs topology vs categorical dominance vs torsion) against each other for expected marginal information gain in a coronary CAD-prediction context specifically; the ordering proposed above is a logical inference from the redundancy argument, not an empirically ranked result.
