# Coronary Dominance as a Scalar Covariate: LAD Relevance, CAD-Risk Association, Automated Determination, and Encoding

## Does coronary dominance meaningfully affect LAD geometry, length, or branch pattern?

### Takeaway
Contrary to the hypothesis that the LAD is dominance-invariant, direct primary evidence shows dominance significantly affects LAD length/course (whether it wraps around the apex to reach the inferior myocardial wall), with left dominance associated with a longer, apex-wrapping LAD. A separate anatomical study also links diagonal-branch territory size to LCx dominance/hypertrophy, though this is a related but distinct classification from RCA/LCx PDA-based dominance.

### Cited Findings
- In a retrospective CTA study of 100 patients (50 right-dominant, 50 left-dominant, young adult males 40-60y, normal coronary angiograms), dominance was defined by which artery gave rise to the PDA. LAD length/course was categorized as Type I (didn't reach apex), Type II (reached apex, no wrap), Type III (wrapped around apex to inferior myocardial wall, IMW). In right-dominant patients, 58% of LADs did not reach the IMW (Types I/II) and 42% reached/wrapped the IMW (Type III); in left-dominant patients, only 24% did not reach the IMW and 76% reached/wrapped it. Chi-square test: p = 0.001 (reported elsewhere in the same paper as p < 0.001). — [Abdul Wahaab et al., 2018, Egyptian J. Hospital Medicine 71(4):2944-2949](https://applications.emro.who.int/imemrf/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med_2018_71_4_2944_2949.pdf)
- The same paper's conclusion: "length of LAD artery appeared to be dependent on the dominance pattern... it was taller [longer] in left coronary dominant pattern," consistent with LAD wrapping the apex more often when the LCx (not RCA) is dominant. — [Abdul Wahaab et al., 2018](https://applications.emro.who.int/imemrf/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med_2018_71_4_2944_2949.pdf)
- This finding corroborates an earlier, independent primary study specifically on this question: Ilia R, Rosenshtein G, Weinstein JM, Cafri C, Abu-Ful A, Gueron M (2001), "Left anterior descending artery length in left and right coronary artery dominance," Coronary Artery Disease 12(1):77-78 — cited by Abdul Wahaab et al. as reporting the LAD in left dominance is "usually long and wraps around the apex." (Full text of the 2001 paper itself was not retrieved in this pass; cited here via the 2018 paper's discussion, which paraphrases its conclusion consistently with its own data.)
- A separate cadaveric + radiological anatomical study (n=430 hearts: 30 cadavers + 400 imaging) found dominance prevalence of 77% right / 14% left / 9% co-dominant, and reported that the number and myocardial territory of LAD diagonal branches was smaller in cases with a dominant LCx or a large obtuse marginal branch, because diagonal-branch territory is partly shared with obtuse-marginal (LCx) territory. — [Anatomical variations of the left coronary artery: a cadaveric and radiological study, European Journal of Anatomy](https://eurjanat.com/articles/anatomical-variations-of-the-left-coronary-artery-a-cadaveric-and-radiological-study/)
- The same study found LCA branching mode (bifurcation 74.1%, trifurcation 24.18%, tetrafurcation 0.46%) correlates with diagonal branch presence (100% of bifurcation cases had diagonal branches vs 97% of trifurcation cases vs 0% of tetrafurcation cases), but this "branching mode" classification (bifurcation/trifurcation of the LCA into LAD/LCx/ramus) is a distinct anatomical construct from RCA-vs-LCx PDA-based dominance, and the study does not report this branching-mode correlation as stratified directly by RCA/LCx dominance status. — [European Journal of Anatomy, cadaveric study](https://eurjanat.com/articles/anatomical-variations-of-the-left-coronary-artery-a-cadaveric-and-radiological-study/)

### Inferences
- The LAD is *not* dominance-invariant: its terminal course (apex-wrapping vs terminating short) and hence its effective length are directly, statistically significantly associated with RCA/LCx dominance in a dedicated CTA study (p=0.001, n=100). This is a genuine, distinct-from-RCA/LCx-confound finding: the mechanism differs from the RCA/LCx effects (which are about which vessel reaches the crux/PDA territory) — here the proposed mechanism (per the cited discussion) is that when the LCx is dominant and extends toward the crux, the LAD compensates by wrapping further around the apex to cover more of the inferior wall, rather than the PDA-supply mechanism itself directly acting on the LAD.
- The diagonal-branch-territory finding is a second, softer signal that dominance-adjacent anatomy (LCx size/dominance) shapes LAD-adjacent territory, but it is confounded with the separate LCA branching-mode (bifurcation/trifurcation) classification, so it should not be treated as an independent replication of the length/wrap finding above.
- For a whole-tree model, this means dominance is a plausible confound for LAD-derived geometric features (especially any feature sensitive to vessel length or terminal branching/wrap), not just for RCA and LCx as previously established.

### Gaps
- The 2018 CTA study (n=100) is a single, moderate-sized study restricted to young adult males (40-60y) with normal coronary angiograms; no larger or more diverse (age/sex-inclusive) replication of the LAD-length-vs-dominance finding was retrieved in this pass. The corroborating 2001 Ilia et al. paper was not retrieved in full text (only via secondary citation), so its own sample size and exact statistics are unverified here.
- No study was found that quantifies dominance's effect on LAD *tortuosity* specifically (curvature, not just terminal length/wrap category), which is the feature type most directly relevant to a curvature-vector-based model (Model 2 in this thesis). This is an open gap: the evidence establishes a length/course effect, not a directly measured curvature effect.
- No data found on whether codominant (as opposed to strictly left-dominant) patterns show an intermediate or LCx-dominant-like effect on LAD length.

## Is coronary dominance itself directly associated with CAD risk, presence, or severity (beyond being a geometric confound)?

### Takeaway
The literature is genuinely mixed and, in places, directly contradictory: several studies find left (and codominant) circulation independently associated with worse outcomes (mortality, MI, poorer LV function) especially in the context of acute coronary syndrome (ACS)/PCI, while at least one more recent multivariable CTA study found dominance pattern was *not* an independent predictor of severe CAD burden after adjusting for standard risk factors. This should be reported as a controversy, not a settled association.

### Cited Findings
- In a CTA cohort study, left-dominant coronary circulation was independently associated with non-fatal myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality, HR 3.20 (95% CI: 1.67-6.13), versus right dominance. Among patients with significant coronary stenosis, 3-year cumulative incidence of non-fatal MI + all-cause mortality was 35% in left-dominant vs 9.5% in right-dominant patients. In patients *without* significant CAD, there was no significant difference in event-free survival by dominance. Dominance was determined by which vessel (RCA vs LCx) gave rise to the PDA. — [Prognostic value of coronary vessel dominance in relation to significant CAD determined with CT coronary angiography, European Heart Journal 33(11):1367](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/33/11/1367/548529)
- A large systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies (255,718 patients total) found left-dominant circulation increases mortality in acute coronary syndrome. — [Khan et al., 2016, Catheterization and Cardiovascular Interventions](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ccd.26281) (title/topic confirmed via search index; the full abstract with exact pooled effect size could not be retrieved — see Gaps)
- Left and codominant coronary circulations are associated with higher in-hospital mortality among patients undergoing PCI for acute coronary syndromes, versus right-dominant circulation. — [Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes 2012](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circoutcomes.111.964593)
- Right dominance was independently associated with triple-vessel CAD when compared to controls in one study (i.e., in the opposite direction from the left-dominance-risk literature above, right dominance was the pattern linked to more extensive multivessel disease in this particular analysis). — [Impact of right coronary dominance on triple-vessel coronary artery disease, Medicine journal](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/md-journal/fulltext/10.1097/md.0000000000011685~impact-of-right-coronary-dominance-on-triple-vessel-coronary)
- In a more recent CTA study using multivariable logistic regression (dominance encoded as a 3-level categorical variable: right/left/codominant, alongside age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking), left coronary dominance was *not* independently associated with severe CAD (CAD-RADS 4-5) after adjustment; only increasing age and male sex remained independent predictors. — [Coronary Dominance on Coronary CT Angiography: Prevalence and Potential Implications for Electrocardiographic Correlation, MDPI J. Clin. Med. 15(13):5258 / PMC13363422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363422/)
- A 2024 JAHA narrative review states the evidence is mixed: it reports the CTA-based finding of left dominance as an independent predictor of non-fatal MI/mortality (matching the EHJ 2012 study above) but also notes at least one other study found left dominance was *not* independently associated with severe CAD after multivariable adjustment (matching the MDPI/PMC13363422 finding above), and separately reports that left and codominant patterns are associated with higher rates of left ventricular hypertrophy, particularly in women, and that lesions in the dominant artery are associated with higher rates of intraprocedural pacing need and AV nodal block in MI patients. — [Clinical Significance of Coronary Arterial Dominance: A Review of the Literature, JAHA, 2024](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.123.032851) (full text was blocked by a 403 error; this is drawn from the search engine's summary of the article, not a direct fetch — see Gaps)
- Prevalence figures consistently cited across sources: approximately 77-80% right-dominant, ~14% left-dominant, ~9% codominant (consistent with, and not contradicting, the ~80-85/7-13/2-5% range already established for this thesis).

### Inferences
- The safest characterization for the thesis is that coronary dominance's *direct* association with CAD risk/severity is contested: it appears most robust as a prognostic factor for adverse *events* (MI, mortality, especially in ACS/PCI settings) rather than as a predictor of baseline CAD *presence or extent* — the one study that specifically tested CAD-RADS severity as the outcome found no independent effect after adjustment, while the studies finding a strong effect (HR 3.20, meta-analysis of 255k patients) used mortality/MI as the outcome, largely in ACS or post-PCI populations.
- This distinction matters for the thesis's specific goal (predicting CAD presence/severity from geometry, not post-MI outcomes): the literature most relevant to the CAD-RADS/severity outcome (MDPI/PMC13363422) argues against a direct/independent dominance effect on CAD once age, sex, and standard risk factors are controlled, which is a more cautious basis for excluding dominance as a direct risk covariate than the outcomes literature would suggest.
- Given the contradiction, if dominance is included as a covariate, its role should probably be framed as a geometric confound to be adjusted for (as the RCA/LCx/LAD length findings support) rather than as a hypothesized independent CAD-severity risk factor in its own right, since evidence for the latter is inconsistent.

### Gaps
- The exact pooled hazard ratio / effect size from the Khan et al. 2016 meta-analysis (255,718 patients) could not be retrieved: PubMed presented a CAPTCHA page and the Wiley journal page returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch. This is a significant gap since it is the largest and most authoritative quantitative source on this question found in this pass; a manual/institutional-access retrieval of this paper is recommended before citing a specific effect size from it in the thesis.
- The JAHA 2024 review (the most comprehensive and recent source found) could not be fetched in full text (403 error); the summary above is a search-engine synthesis of its abstract/content, not a directly verified read. This review should be prioritized for a full read (e.g., via the paper-review skill or institutional access) given it appears to directly reconcile the very studies that conflict here.
- No study was found that examines whether dominance's association with adverse outcomes/CAD burden is itself mediated by the geometric confounds (RCA/LCx/LAD length and branch pattern) already established for this thesis, versus an independent physiological mechanism (e.g., "less balanced" perfusion redundancy). This mediation question is directly relevant to whether dominance should be a covariate on top of, or instead of, the length/branch-pattern features it correlates with, but no source addressed it directly.

## What is the standard, validated way to determine coronary dominance, and how automatable is it from a segmentation/centerline pipeline?

### Takeaway
The universally used clinical definition is which vessel (RCA or LCx) gives rise to the posterior descending artery (PDA) at the crux, assessed visually on invasive angiography or CTA by a reader. No source in this pass reports a validated, published method that derives dominance automatically and directly from a 3D segmentation/centerline tree the way this thesis's pipeline would need; the CT-tree-labeling literature explicitly treats dominance as a required, pre-known input rather than an algorithm output, and the automated methods that do exist operate on 2D angiographic images as a classification task, not on 3D centerline/segmentation geometry.

### Cited Findings
- The standard definition, repeated consistently across sources: right dominance = PDA (and posterolateral branches) arise from the RCA; left dominance = PDA arises from the LCx; codominance = both RCA and LCx contribute to the posterior/inferior territory. This is assessed by identifying, on angiography, which vessel's branch crosses the crux of the heart and supplies the PDA territory. — [Coronary Dominance on Coronary CT Angiography, PMC13363422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363422/); [Prognostic value of coronary vessel dominance, EHJ](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/33/11/1367/548529); [Length of LAD in relation to dominance, Abdul Wahaab et al. 2018](https://applications.emro.who.int/imemrf/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med_2018_71_4_2944_2949.pdf)
- A dedicated automatic coronary-tree-anatomy-labeling paper explicitly states that "the dominance type of the coronary tree should be known before labeling": the method requires separate, pre-built 3D anatomical models for right-dominant and left-dominant trees and a human/external decision about which model to apply, rather than deriving dominance itself. The paper's own stated future work is "automatic detection of the dominancy to choose the correct model... will be investigated in future work," indicating that, as of this paper, dominance detection from the tree was an acknowledged unsolved step, not a solved one. Reported labeling accuracy (89.2% right-dominant segments, 83.6% left-dominant segments) is for segment *labeling* given known dominance, not for dominance detection itself. — [Automatic identification of coronary tree anatomy in coronary CT angiography, PMC5677991](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5677991/)
- A separate geometric deep learning method (GraphSAGE-based, operating on centerline-derived graphs of nodes/edges with spatial/directional features) for automated coronary artery segment labeling does not address coronary dominance at all — it labels 13 arterial segment classes independently and has no dominance-related input or output. — [Automated Coronary Arteries Labeling Via Geometric Deep Learning, arXiv:2212.00386](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.00386v1)
- Automated dominance *classification* methods that do exist in the recent literature operate on 2D/video invasive angiographic images (not 3D CT segmentation or centerlines): a 2026 study used transformer-based video architectures (VideoMAEv2) to classify right- vs left-dominance directly from angiographic video, framed as eliminating the need for manual visual assessment of that video. — [Real-Time Coronary Artery Dominance Classification from Angiographic Images Using Advanced Deep Video Architectures, Diagnostics 15(10):1186 / PMC12110073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12110073/)
- Manual/visual assessment is noted in the literature to have known reliability limits, particularly for borderline/codominant cases or small-caliber RCAs, which is cited as part of the motivation for automated classification approaches. (Source: search-engine synthesis of results for automated dominance classification literature; specific paper not individually fetched — see Gaps.)

### Inferences
- Given that (a) the clinical rule is purely topological/geometric — "which vessel's centerline crosses the crux and continues into the PDA/posterior interventricular groove territory" — and (b) this thesis's pipeline already has a full centerline tree with vessel identity per branch (RCA vs LCx system), a rule-based, programmatic determination of dominance directly from centerline geometry (e.g., identify which system's terminal branch extends past a landmark corresponding to the crux/posterior interventricular groove, or which system supplies a length/territory beyond that landmark) is plausible in principle and would not require training a separate classifier. However, no published implementation of exactly this geometric/topological rule (applied to a 3D segmentation or centerline tree rather than a 2D angiographic image or a pre-labeled anatomical model) was found in this literature pass — this appears to be a genuine gap between what is clinically well-defined and what has been publicly implemented as an automated 3D-geometry-based algorithm.
- The one CT-tree-labeling paper that comes closest to this thesis's setting (PMC5677991) treats dominance as an input a human must supply, which is direct evidence that, as of that publication, no validated automatic method existed even in a paper specifically about automating coronary tree anatomy from CT segmentation — reinforcing that this would likely need to be built bespoke (a landmark-based rule: e.g., detect the crux position, e.g. as the point where a RCA/LCx branch crosses posteriorly, and check which system's branch continues distal to it) rather than adopted from an existing validated tool.

### Gaps
- No published, validated algorithm was found that automatically determines coronary dominance directly from a 3D vessel segmentation or centerline tree (as opposed to a human-labeled anatomical model, or a 2D angiographic image classifier). This is the central gap for the "how automatable is this" question, and should be reported to the user as such: it looks feasible via a geometry/landmark rule but is, as far as this search found, unimplemented and unvalidated in the literature.
- No accuracy/validation numbers were found for any rule-based (non-learned) geometric dominance-determination approach against a radiologist ground truth, which would be needed to know how much error such a self-built rule might introduce as a covariate.
- The claim about manual visual-assessment reliability limits (codominant/borderline cases, small RCAs) was only captured via a search-engine synthesis, not a directly fetched primary source — it should be treated as suggestive, not confirmed, until a primary source is checked.

## How should dominance best be encoded as a covariate (categorical, binary, or continuous/ordinal)?

### Takeaway
The literature's overwhelming default is a 3-level categorical variable (right / left / codominant), matching the clinical classification exactly; a binary collapse (right-dominant vs. left+codominant combined) also appears in outcomes studies, motivated by the idea that left and codominant circulations share a "less balanced perfusion" mechanism. No study was found that encodes dominance as a continuous or ordinal proxy (e.g., a relative RCA:LCx length or territory ratio) as a covariate in a CAD-prediction or outcomes regression model — this appears to be an unaddressed option in the literature rather than one that was tried and rejected.

### Cited Findings
- In a multivariable logistic regression predicting severe CAD (CAD-RADS 4-5) from CTA, coronary dominance was entered as a categorical variable with three levels: right, left, codominant. — [Coronary Dominance on Coronary CT Angiography, PMC13363422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363422/)
- In the EHJ prognostic-value study, the discussion frames left and codominant circulation together as representing "less well-balanced myocardial perfusion," conceptually grouping them against right dominance, consistent with a binary right-vs-not-right encoding being a natural collapse of the 3-level variable used in outcomes analyses of this kind. — [Prognostic value of coronary vessel dominance, EHJ 33(11):1367](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/33/11/1367/548529)
- The PCI/ACS in-hospital mortality study explicitly reports "left and codominant" as a combined exposure category compared against right-dominant, i.e., a binary encoding (right-dominant vs. left-or-codominant) used directly in that analysis. — [Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes 2012](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circoutcomes.111.964593)
- The dedicated LAD-length study used only two dominance groups by design (50 right, 50 left), excluding codominant cases entirely from the comparison, which is a design choice (not full 3-level encoding, and not a binary "not-right" collapse either, since codominant was excluded rather than merged). — [Abdul Wahaab et al. 2018](https://applications.emro.who.int/imemrf/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med_2018_71_4_2944_2949.pdf)

### Inferences
- For this thesis's whole-tree model, the literature supports either the full 3-level categorical encoding (matching clinical definition and most CAD-severity studies) or a binary right-vs-not-right collapse (matching outcomes-study precedent and the "less balanced perfusion" rationale) as defensible, precedented choices; a strict binary is simpler and has a mechanistic rationale but discards the (small, ~9%) codominant category's distinctness.
- No precedent was found for a continuous/ordinal proxy (e.g., relative RCA:LCx length ratio or territory-size ratio) as a covariate. Given that dominance is fundamentally a discrete anatomical fact (which vessel gives rise to the PDA) rather than a graded quantity, and given that no study operationalizes it continuously, introducing a continuous proxy would be a novel modeling choice for this thesis rather than one supported by precedent — it could be justified on the grounds that it captures gradation (e.g., a borderline/balanced case) that the categorical definition forces into a discrete bin, but this would need to be presented as a deviation/extension, not as following established practice.

### Gaps
- No source was found that directly compares model performance (e.g., calibration, discrimination) under different dominance encodings (3-level categorical vs. binary vs. continuous proxy) for any CAD-prediction or outcomes model. The choice of encoding in the literature appears to be driven by outcome-specific mechanistic framing (e.g., grouping left+codominant for a "balanced perfusion" hypothesis) rather than by a documented empirical comparison of encodings.
- No literature was found specifically addressing how dominance should be encoded in the context of a small neural network alongside a per-point curvature vector (i.e., Model 2's architecture) as opposed to a linear/logistic model (Model 1) — the encoding precedent above comes entirely from linear/logistic epidemiological studies, not from ML models structurally similar to Model 2.
