# Low (and oscillatory) WSS in arterial bends, with emphasis on human coronary arteries

Reading depth legend: [FULL] = full text read (page given); [ABS] = abstract only (via Europe PMC/PubMed record); [META] = bibliographic record only, content not read.

Draft sentence under review: "In a bend, the fast core of the flow is carried to the outer wall, while flow along the inner wall slows and can recirculate [chatzizisis2007ess]."

## 1. Mechanism: what happens to flow in a bend, and is "recirculate" accurate for coronary bends?

### Takeaway
The first half of the sentence is right: in a bend, centrifugal (inertial) effects push the fast core of the flow toward the outer wall, which sets up a pair of counter-rotating secondary (Dean) vortices and leaves slow, low-momentum fluid and low WSS on the inner wall. "Recirculate" overstates what happens in a typical coronary bend. Flow separation with a recirculation zone on the inner wall needs higher Dean numbers (tight bends, faster flow, e.g. the aortic arch). Coronary models at physiological conditions show low mean WSS on the inner wall that briefly reverses direction during the cycle (oscillatory WSS), not a stable recirculation bubble. The only coronary source that reports "slow recirculation" (Asakura & Karino 1990) pools bends and bifurcations in its abstract and used isolated post-mortem arteries.

### Cited Findings
- Ku 1997, p. 407 (section "Curved Tubes") [FULL]: Dean "developed a parameter relating the centrifugal forces to the viscous forces"; Dean number = (2δ)^1/2 · 4Re, with δ = tube radius / radius of curvature of the centerline; "by convention, the Dean number includes a few numerical constants (Pedley 1980)". — [Ku 1997, Annu Rev Fluid Mech 29:399–434](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.fluid.29.1.399) (full text read from [this copy](https://mriquestions.com/uploads/3/4/5/7/34572113/annual_review_flow.pdf))
- Ku 1997, p. 407 [FULL], verbatim: "Fully developed flow upstream of or through curved tubes exhibits velocity that skews toward the outer wall of the bend. For most arterial flows, skewing will be toward the outer wall. Secondary flow develops as counter-rotating vortices with flow in the middle that moves toward the outer wall. With higher Dean numbers, flow can separate along the inner curve. Pulsatile flow through a curved tube can induce complicated secondary flows with flow reversals." — [Ku 1997](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.fluid.29.1.399)
- Ku 1997, p. 407 [FULL], caveat: if inflow is not fully developed (entrance flow, e.g. aortic arch), "the core of the fluid in the curve can act like a potential vortex with velocity skewing toward the inner wall". So the outer-wall skew is the fully developed case, which Ku says applies to "most arterial flows". — [Ku 1997](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.fluid.29.1.399)
- Ku 1997, p. 414 [FULL], left coronary: the left main is short, giving "an entrance type flow at a low Womersley parameter of 3"; coronary flow is greater in diastole and can reverse in systole; the bifurcation "curves around the heart while branching"; "The curvatures likely set up secondary flows during part of the cardiac cycle." — [Ku 1997](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.fluid.29.1.399)
- Chang & Tarbell 1988 [ABS], pulsatile simulations of curved tubes "simulating coronary arteries" (resting, excited, drug-induced states), motivated by "reported observations of atherosclerotic plaque localization on the inner curvature of coronary arteries": at rest the flow is quasi-steady, "wall shear stress always highest on the outside wall and only a single secondary flow vortex in the half tube. However, reversal of wall shear stress direction at the inside wall does occur under resting flow conditions". Under excited conditions WSS sometimes peaks on the inside wall and reversal on the inside wall increases. No separation/recirculation zone is reported in the abstract. — [Chang LJ, Tarbell JM. J Biomech 1988;21(11):927–937. doi:10.1016/0021-9290(88)90131-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(88)90131-5)
- Qiu & Tarbell 2000 [ABS], compliant curved-tube coronary model with "characteristic coronary artery curvature and flow conditions": aspect ratio (radius of curvature / tube radius) λ = 10, mean Re = 150, Womersley α = 3, diameter variation 6%. Result: "mean WSS is about 50 percent lower on the inside wall than the outside wall while WSS oscillation is stronger on the inside wall." — [Qiu Y, Tarbell JM. J Biomech Eng 2000;122(1):77–85. doi:10.1115/1.429629](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.429629)
- Qiu & Tarbell 2000b [ABS], same model with oxygen transport: Sherwood number about 55 on the outside wall vs about 2 on the inside wall, implying O2 transport at the inner wall "could be limited by the fluid phase". Note: this abstract labels the inner wall "(epicardial surface)", i.e. the side facing the heart surface; clinical papers call this the "myocardial side" of the vessel. — [Qiu Y, Tarbell JM. Ann Biomed Eng 2000;28(1):26–38. doi:10.1114/1.251](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.251)
- Myers et al. 2001 [ABS], CFD in an anatomically realistic human RCA (steady Re_D = 500; pulsatile mean Re_D = 233, Womersley 1.82): "Velocity profiles showed Dean-like secondary flow features that were remarkably sensitive to the local curvature"; the "rotation" of these profiles produced large WSS variations along the sidewalls; "The oscillatory shear index was very small and was attributed to flow reversal in the waveform, rather than separation." — [Myers JG, Moore JA, Ojha M, Johnston KW, Ethier CR. Ann Biomed Eng 2001;29(2):109–120. doi:10.1114/1.1349703](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.1349703)
- Asakura & Karino 1990 [ABS], flow visualization with high-speed cinemicrography in five isolated, transparent human coronary arterial trees prepared post-mortem: plaques and wall thickenings were "localized almost exclusively on the outer wall of one or both daughter vessels at major bifurcations and T-junctions ... and along the inner wall of curved segments"; "these sites were where flow was either slow or disturbed with the formation of slow recirculation and secondary flows and where wall shear stress was low"; concludes the major factors are "the low fluid velocity and the resultant low shear stress". The abstract does not say whether recirculation was seen at curves specifically or only at branch sites. — [Asakura T, Karino T. Circ Res 1990;66(4):1045–1066. doi:10.1161/01.RES.66.4.1045](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.RES.66.4.1045)
- Moore, Weydahl & Santamarina 2001 [ABS], curved coronary tube model with time-varying curvature (curvature ratio 0.08): "The overall flow patterns were similar to those observed in stationary curved tubes; velocity profile skewed toward the outer wall, secondary flow patterns, etc." — [Moore JE, Weydahl ES, Santamarina A. J Biomech Eng 2001;123(2):129–133. doi:10.1115/1.1351806](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.1351806)
- Chatzizisis et al. 2007 [ABS]: lesions form "at specific arterial regions, where low and oscillatory endothelial shear stress (ESS) occur". I could not open the full text (JACC paywall), so I could not verify which page, if any, describes the bend mechanism or uses the word "recirculation". — [Chatzizisis YS, Coskun AU, Jonas M, Edelman ER, Feldman CL, Stone PH. J Am Coll Cardiol 2007;49(25):2379–2393. doi:10.1016/j.jacc.2007.02.059](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2007.02.059)

### Inferences
- Precise mechanism for a clinical reader: in a bend the fastest blood swings toward the outer wall (it "overshoots" the turn), a pair of slow swirls forms across the vessel, and blood along the inner wall moves slowly, so WSS there is low on average and, in the pulsatile coronary flow, briefly reverses direction during each heartbeat.
- "Can recirculate" is defensible only via Asakura & Karino 1990, and only loosely. The engineering literature (Ku p. 407; Chang & Tarbell 1988; Myers 2001) puts separation at higher Dean numbers than coronary bends normally reach, and attributes inner-wall oscillation in coronaries to the pulsatile waveform rather than separation. Safer wording: "slows and may briefly reverse direction during the cardiac cycle".
- Chatzizisis 2007 is a biology/remodeling review. It supports "low and oscillatory ESS at specific regions" but is not the best source for the fluid mechanics of a bend. Ku 1997 (p. 407) is the citable review for the mechanism; Asakura & Karino 1990 for the human coronary observation; Chang & Tarbell 1988 or Qiu & Tarbell 2000 for coronary-scale curved-tube numbers.
- Suggested rewrite: "In a bend, the fastest-moving blood is carried toward the outer wall, so flow along the inner wall is slow and wall shear stress there is low and may reverse direction during the cardiac cycle [ku1997blood; asakura1990flow]."

### Gaps
- Full text of Asakura & Karino 1990 not accessible (AHA returned 403); whether recirculation was observed in curved segments, and under steady or pulsatile perfusion, is unverified.
- Full text of Chatzizisis 2007 not read; cannot confirm it describes bend flow at all.
- No source found giving a threshold Dean number for inner-wall separation in coronary-scale geometry; Ku says only "higher Dean numbers". Pedley 1980 (The Fluid Mechanics of Large Blood Vessels, Cambridge UP) is the cited textbook treatment; not consulted.

## 2. Primary origin (Dean) and classic arterial references

### Takeaway
Dean's two Philosophical Magazine papers (1927, 1928) are the origin of the secondary-vortex theory for curved pipes. Caro, Fitz-Gerald & Schroter 1971 is the classic origin of the low-shear hypothesis of atherogenesis. Ku 1997 and Malek 1999 are the standard reviews.

Credited with formulating the fully developed curved-pipe problem and showing a pair of counter-rotating secondary vortices. — [Taylor & Francis record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14786440708564324); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/XVI.-Note-on-the-motion-of-fluid-in-a-curved-pipe-Dean/b7f8e4a13b9f57f1257d426cdaebb8e5f7d4235d)

## 3. Evidence that plaque concentrates on the inner wall of coronary bends, and contrary findings

### Takeaway
Ex vivo flow studies, in vivo IVUS and CCTA all find more plaque on the inner curvature (myocardial side) of coronary arteries, and the effect is stronger in more curved segments and in the LAD. It is not universal: the RCA and LCX follow the rule less well, disease and stenting blur it, one patient-specific RCA CFD study found the opposite WSS pattern at bends, and helical flow from vessel torsion modifies WSS.

### Cited Findings
Supporting:
- Asakura & Karino 1990 [ABS]: plaques "along the inner wall of curved segments" in 5 post-mortem human coronary trees. — [Circ Res 1990;66:1045–1066](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.RES.66.4.1045)
- Iwami et al. 1998 [ABS], IVUS in patients with CAD: "Coronary atherosclerosis preferentially formed at the inner arc of the curved coronary vessels, and greater vessel curvatures were associated with greater distributions of atherosclerotic lesions along the inner coronary artery wall." — [Iwami T, Fujii T, Miura T, et al. Am J Cardiol 1998;82(3):381–384. doi:10.1016/S0002-9149(98)00340-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0002-9149(98)00340-3)
- Wahle et al. 2006 [FULL, author preprint], IVUS fused with biplane angiography: plaque/curvature index computed in 60 vessels; plaque/WSS correlation in 48. "Circumferential plaque distribution depends on local vessel curvature in the majority of vessels"; correlation of circumferential plaque thickness "stronger ... with local curvature than with wall shear stress" (abstract). §3.3.1: the mean index rises steadily as low-curvature regions are excluded, i.e. the hypothesis is better supported at higher curvature; "almost two thirds of the vessels satisfied rPC > 0.5 for all thresholds"; support was stronger in the LAD (87% of LADs for all or at least half of thresholds) and "less supportive" in the RCA and LCX. §3.4: the inverse plaque/WSS relation "could not be found" directly and needed restriction to subsets; it was significantly stronger (p < 0.025) in cross sections with positive remodeling without luminal narrowing, and absent when < 35% of cross sections lacked luminal narrowing. Discussion: the LAD fits a curved-tube model; RCA and LCX are "more complex (and more tortuous)" with flow "that can no longer be explained by the curved-tube model"; stenting distorts the curvature/plaque relation. — [Wahle A, Lopez JJ, Olszewski ME, Vigmostad SC, Chandran KB, Rossen JD, Sonka M. Med Image Anal 2006;10(4):615–631. doi:10.1016/j.media.2006.03.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2006.03.002) ([PMC2590653](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2590653/); preprint read at [uiowa.edu](https://user.engineering.uiowa.edu/~awahle/WahLIT/Papers/wahl06a-cfd.pdf))
- Enrico et al. 2009 [ABS], CCTA, 73 patients, 382 plaques: 80% eccentric; "a significant (p < 0.01) majority (55%) were on the myocardial side, while 17.3% were lateral, and 27.7% epicardial"; of non-calcified and mixed plaques 45.1% myocardial vs 20.6% epicardial. The authors attribute this to "low shear stress (myocardial vessel wall)". Note it measures orientation relative to the myocardium, not local bend curvature. — [Enrico B, Suranyi P, Thilo C, Bonomo L, Costello P, Schoepf UJ. Eur Radiol 2009;19(4):837–844. doi:10.1007/s00330-008-1223-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00330-008-1223-3)
- Komiyama et al. 2016 [ABS], IVUS/VH-IVUS, 30 arteries, 1441 cross sections, eccentric plaques in non-branching segments only: myocardial vs epicardial side 46.7 ± 7.5% vs 12.5 ± 4.2% (p = 0.003); myocardial vs lateral not significant; eccentric thin-cap fibroatheromas more often on the myocardial side. — [Komiyama H, Takano H, Nakamura S, et al. Cardiovasc Ultrasound 2016;14:47. doi:10.1186/s12947-016-0090-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12947-016-0090-3)

Contrary or complicating:
- Johnston & Johnston 2007 [ABS], CFD in four human RCAs from biplane angiograms (single time point): large between-artery differences in WSS; strong correlation of WSS with reciprocal radius (lumen size), and "a weaker correlation between high curvature and extremes of WSS, with high WSS on the 'inside' of a bend and low WSS on the 'outside' of a bend. This is in contrast to the situation for a simple curved tube with constant radius where the inverse is observed." They still find a low-WSS region proximal to the acute margin where inner-wall WSS is lower than outer. — [Johnston BM, Johnston PR. Phys Med Biol 2007;52(9):2531–2544. doi:10.1088/0031-9155/52/9/013](https://doi.org/10.1088/0031-9155/52/9/013)
- Myers et al. 2001 [ABS]: in a realistic RCA the Dean-like profiles "rotate" with the 3D curvature, so low WSS moves to the sidewalls rather than staying on the inner wall; "geometric effects (particularly local three-dimensional curvature) dominate RCA hemodynamics". — [Ann Biomed Eng 2001;29:109–120](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.1349703)
- Wahle et al. 2006 [FULL]: weaker support in RCA and LCX; direct plaque/WSS correlation absent without restricting to early (non-stenotic, positively remodeled) disease. — [Med Image Anal 2006;10:615–631](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2006.03.002)
- Cardiac motion: Santamarina et al. 1998 [ABS], curved coronary tube with radius of curvature varying sinusoidally at 1 Hz, Re 300: wall shear rates varied by up to 52% of the static mean within 10 diameters of the inlet; dynamic results within 6% of quasi-static. — [Santamarina A, Weydahl E, Siegel JM, Moore JE. Ann Biomed Eng 1998;26(6):944–954. doi:10.1114/1.113](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.113). Moore et al. 2001 [ABS]: at curvature ratio 0.08 and 50% change in radius of curvature, wall shear rate at mid-wall varied by up to 94% of the mean; inner-wall shear-rate amplitude scaled with δ·ε·α; dynamic curvature matters when εδα > 0.05; "The mean wall shear rate was well predicted with a static geometry." — [J Biomech Eng 2001;123:129–133](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.1351806)
- Helical flow/torsion: De Nisco et al. 2019 [ABS], 30 swine coronary arteries: "counter-rotating bi-helical flow structures"; unfavourable WSS "strongly and inversely associated with helicity intensity (r = −0.91; p < 0.001)"; "vascular torsion dictates helical flow features (r = 0.64; p < 0.001)". — [De Nisco G, Kok AM, Chiastra C, et al. Ann Biomed Eng 2019;47(2):425–438. doi:10.1007/s10439-018-02169-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10439-018-02169-x). De Nisco et al. 2020 [ABS], 15 coronary arteries of 5 hypercholesterolaemic minipigs, CCTA + IVUS at baseline and ~9.4 months: helical flow intensity strongly positively associated with WSS magnitude (p < 0.001); segments with high baseline helicity had significantly lower wall-thickness growth (p < 0.05). — [De Nisco G, Hoogendoorn A, Chiastra C, et al. Atherosclerosis 2020;300:39–46. doi:10.1016/j.atherosclerosis.2020.01.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atherosclerosis.2020.01.027)

### Inferences
- "Inner wall of a bend" and "myocardial side" overlap for epicardial coronaries that wrap around the heart (Qiu & Tarbell's "inner wall (epicardial surface)"), which is why CCTA/IVUS myocardial-side studies are often read as bend evidence. They are not the same measurement; flag this if Enrico 2009 or Komiyama 2016 are cited as bend evidence.
- The curved-tube picture is cleanest in the LAD and least reliable in the tortuous RCA, where 3D curvature, torsion and lumen-diameter changes dominate. For a thesis on CCTA-derived topology this argues for "tends to" rather than "always".

### Gaps
- No large CCTA study found that measures local centerline curvature and correlates it with inner- vs outer-wall plaque; Enrico 2009 uses myocardial/epicardial orientation only.
- Histology series beyond Asakura & Karino (e.g. Friedman) not retrieved.
- Effect of cardiac motion on the inner-wall low-WSS location in vivo is model-based only.

## 4. Does tighter curvature lower inner-wall WSS more? Quantitative relations

### Takeaway
Qualitatively yes: the Dean number grows with the square root of the curvature ratio, and higher Dean numbers mean a stronger outer-wall skew, stronger secondary flow and eventually inner-wall separation. Coronary-specific numbers are model-dependent: about 50% lower mean WSS on the inner than outer wall at λ = 10, Re 150. In vivo, plaque is more inner-wall-predominant in more curved segments (Iwami 1998; Wahle 2006). I found no simple validated formula for inner-wall WSS as a function of curvature in coronary arteries.

### Cited Findings
- Dean number = (2δ)^1/2 · 4Re, δ = tube radius / centerline radius of curvature (Ku's convention, p. 407); "With higher Dean numbers, flow can separate along the inner curve." — [Ku 1997](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.fluid.29.1.399)
- Coronary curved-tube model, λ = R/a = 10 (δ = 0.1), Re 150, α = 3: inner-wall mean WSS about 50% lower than outer wall, with stronger oscillation on the inner wall. — [Qiu & Tarbell 2000, J Biomech Eng 122:77–85](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.429629)
- Inner-wall O2 flux about 1/25 of the outer wall in the same model (Sh ~2 vs ~55). — [Qiu & Tarbell 2000, Ann Biomed Eng 28:26–38](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.251)
- Under exercise-like flow, WSS can peak on the inside wall and inner-wall reversal increases, so the inner/outer contrast depends on flow state, not only on curvature. — [Chang & Tarbell 1988, J Biomech 21:927–937](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(88)90131-5)
- Time-varying curvature: inner-wall shear-rate amplitude scales with δ·ε·α (mean curvature ratio × relative change in radius of curvature × Womersley number). — [Moore et al. 2001, J Biomech Eng 123:129–133](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.1351806)
- In vivo: "greater vessel curvatures were associated with greater distributions of atherosclerotic lesions along the inner coronary artery wall" (IVUS). — [Iwami et al. 1998, Am J Cardiol 82:381–384](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0002-9149(98)00340-3)
- In vivo: the plaque/curvature index rose steadily with the curvature threshold (2.31 to 22.94°/cm), i.e. stronger support at higher curvature (§3.3.1, Fig. 9). — [Wahle et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2006.03.002)
- Counterpoint: in four patient-specific RCAs, WSS correlated strongly with reciprocal lumen radius and only weakly with curvature. — [Johnston & Johnston 2007](https://doi.org/10.1088/0031-9155/52/9/013)

### Inferences
- In a clinical sentence it is fair to say that sharper bends tend to have lower inner-wall shear and more inner-wall plaque, citing Iwami 1998 and Wahle 2006, but not to give a dose-response number.
- Curvature alone is a weak proxy for WSS in tortuous patient-specific coronaries (Wahle 2006; Johnston & Johnston 2007; Myers 2001); lumen diameter, torsion/helicity and flow rate matter at least as much.

### Gaps
- No coronary study found giving inner-wall WSS as an explicit function of curvature ratio across a range of physiological Dean numbers.
- Typical Dean numbers for human coronary bends were not found stated directly; only model parameters (δ ≈ 0.08–0.1, Re 150–500) are available.
