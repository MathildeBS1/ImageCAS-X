# Presenting a segment-level geometry association at person level without diluting or overstating it

Reading guide. Each finding is tagged **[METHOD]** (established methodology), **[GUIDELINE]** (reporting guideline or consensus recommendation), **[APPLIED]** (practice of individual papers, not a recommendation) or **[OPINION]** (expert opinion, blog, slides). "Verified" means the statement was read in the source text during this research; "via X" means it was read only in a secondary source X; anything not read is listed under Gaps. Derivations made here (not taken from a source) are under Inferences and say so. Nothing below proposes a different primary model; the fixed design is one segment-level cloglog GLMM with person random intercept, pooled as 1 − ∏(1 − p_s).

Notation: segment s of person i, linear predictor η_is = α_s + x_i'γ + z_is'β, person intercept u_i, p_is = 1 − exp(−exp(η_is + u_i)), segment "cumulative hazard" H_is = exp(η_is).

## 1. Conditional (cluster-specific) versus marginal (population-averaged) effects: size of attenuation, formulas for logit and cloglog, which to report

### Takeaway
For the logit link with a normal intercept the marginal coefficient is approximately β_C/√(1 + c²σ²), c = 16√3/(15π); no such closed form or published constant was found for the cloglog link with a normal intercept (numerical integration is needed). Exact proportional attenuation β_M = α·β_C holds for a positive stable intercept on the hazard scale; a gamma intercept gives a closed-form marginal probability but not a marginal cloglog-linear model. The conditional hazard ratio is a legitimate main estimate if it is labelled as conditional on the person; marginal quantities are best given as integrated predicted risks, not as a rescaled coefficient.

### Cited Findings
- **[METHOD, verified via Pavlou]** Logit random-intercept model: β_M ≈ β_RE/√(c²σ_u² + 1) with c = 16√3/(15π) (≈ 0.588, so c² ≈ 0.346), attributed to Zeger, Liang & Albert 1988 (Biometrics 44:1049–1060). The fetch tool reported the equation as partly garbled in the HTML and reconstructed it; the same formula appears independently in Austin & Merlo 2017 — [Pavlou, Ambler, Seaman & Omar 2015, BMC Med Res Methodol 15:59, doi:10.1186/s12874-015-0046-6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751); [Austin & Merlo 2017, Stat Med 36:3257–3277, doi:10.1002/sim.7336](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471)
- **[METHOD, verified]** Austin & Merlo give the same conversion, α_PA = α_CS/√(1 + (16²·3/(15π)²)τ²), state that cluster-specific models tend to give larger covariate effects than population-average models, and in their case study the shrinkage factors were only 1.008 and 1.006 because the cluster variance was small — [Austin & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471)
- **[METHOD, verified in preprint version]** For a probit link with normal random effects the attenuation is exact: constant (1 + d'Σd)^(−1/2). For the logit-normal there is no closed form. For the **cloglog link the paper states no closed-form adjustment exists for most random-effects distributions**; a bridge distribution (Wang & Louis 2003) gives a closed-form, linear adjustment for logit and cloglog links with a single random intercept; for normal random effects numerical integration (e.g. Gaussian quadrature) is needed and no constant is given. Zeger et al. 1988 and Neuhaus et al. 1991 are cited for approximate attenuation factors 0 < c < 1 — [Gory, Craigmile & MacEachern, "Marginally interpretable generalized linear mixed models", arXiv:1610.01526 (preprint read; author list from memory, check)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1610.01526)
- **[METHOD, verified in same preprint]** With a **log link** and a random intercept independent of the covariates, slope parameters have both marginal and conditional interpretations (cited there to Ritz & Spiegelman 2004, Stat Methods Med Res) — [arXiv:1610.01526](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1610.01526)
- **[METHOD, abstract only]** Wang & Louis 2003 derive the bridge distribution for the random intercept in the **logistic** model, under which the marginal model is also logistic. The abstract does not mention cloglog or positive stable distributions — [Wang & Louis 2003, Biometrika 90(4):765–775, doi:10.1093/biomet/90.4.765](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/biomet/v90y2003i4p765-775.html)
- **[METHOD, verified]** Positive stable frailty W with Laplace transform E{exp(−sW)} = exp(−s^α), 0 < α ≤ 1: the marginal regression parameter equals α times the conditional one (γ = αβ), and both conditional and marginal hazards have proportional hazards form. This is derived for continuous-time clustered failure times, not for a cloglog GLMM — [Liu, Kalbfleisch & Schaubel 2011, Biometrics 67(1):8–17, doi:10.1111/j.1541-0420.2010.01444.x](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3913567)
- **[METHOD, abstract only]** Gamma random effects with a cloglog link give a closed-form likelihood for clustered binary data; the abstract says nothing on the marginal interpretation of the regression parameters — [Coull, Houseman & Betensky 2006, Biometrika 93(3):587–599, doi:10.1093/biomet/93.3.587](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/biomet/v93y2006i3p587-599.html)
- **[METHOD, recent preprint, search summary only]** A generalized log-gamma random intercept with cloglog link yields an explicit marginal form through conjugacy — [Fabio et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.04518 (preprint, not read in full)](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04518v1)
- **[METHOD, verified]** Austin & Merlo recommend population-average estimates specifically for interpreting **cluster-level** covariates, because a cluster-specific odds ratio for a variable that is constant within the cluster compares states that no cluster is observed in — [Austin & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471). This bears directly on the bifurcation angle and the diameter ratios, which are constant within person.

### Inferences
- (Derivation, not from a source.) Under the fixed model, P(Y_is = 1 | x) = 1 − E_u[exp(−e^u H_is)], i.e. one minus the Laplace transform of the frailty e^u at H_is. With **normal u** this has no closed form, consistent with the Gory et al. statement. With a **positive stable** e^u it equals 1 − exp(−H_is^α), so the marginal model is again cloglog with every coefficient multiplied by α (uses the Laplace transform verified in Liu et al. 2011). With a **gamma** frailty (mean 1, variance θ) it equals 1 − (1 + θH_is)^(−1/θ): closed form, but not linear on the cloglog scale, so the marginal hazard ratio depends on the covariate level.
- Since the cloglog link approaches the log link when p is small, and log-link slopes are the same conditionally and marginally, attenuation of the **segment-level** geometry coefficients is expected to be small when segment risks are low. This is an analogy from the log-link result, not a published cloglog result. At **person level** the risk is not small, so the conditional/marginal difference matters most for person-level predicted risks (section 6).
- The Zeger constant c = 16√3/(15π) belongs to the logistic distribution and must not be applied to the cloglog model. If a marginal coefficient is wanted it has to be obtained numerically (integrate predictions over u, then summarise), and the marginal cloglog relation will not be exactly linear.
- For a prognostic factor study whose prespecified main result is the LR test plus conditional HRs, the consistent presentation is: conditional HR as the estimate, explicitly worded "comparing two segments of persons with the same random intercept / the same unmeasured person-level susceptibility", plus integrated (marginal) predicted risks for interpretation on the risk scale. For the three person-level geometry terms the conditional wording is the weakest (Austin & Merlo point above); their effect is better illustrated with marginal predicted person risks.

### Gaps
- Zeger, Liang & Albert 1988; Neuhaus, Kalbfleisch & Hauck 1991 (Biometrika 78:25–40); Neuhaus & Jewell 1993 (Biometrika 80:807–815, attenuation from omitted covariates) were **not read in the original**. The often-quoted Neuhaus et al. approximation β_M ≈ β_C(1 − ρ) and the Neuhaus–Jewell result that omitting a covariate attenuates the others toward zero in non-collapsible links could not be verified here.
- No source was found that gives an attenuation constant for the **cloglog link with a normal random intercept**. This is a gap in what was found, possibly in the literature.
- Whether Wang & Louis 2003 itself treats cloglog/positive stable was not verified (only Gory et al. say the bridge approach covers cloglog). Hougaard 1986 (Biometrika 73:387–396) was not read.
- Non-collapsibility of hazard and odds ratios (Greenland, Robins & Pearl 1999; Daniel, Zhang & Farewell 2021 Biom J; Martinussen & Vansteelandt) was not fetched; no sourced statement is given.
- No guideline was found that says which of conditional or marginal should be the main estimate in a prognostic factor study.

## 2. Between-person versus within-person effect of a segment-level covariate

### Takeaway
In a random-intercept model the single coefficient of a segment-varying covariate is a weighted average of its between-person and within-person effects; it is only interpretable as either one if the two are equal. A within–between split is an accepted technique and can be shown descriptively, but its within-person part must not be presented as the main estimate under this design.

### Cited Findings
- **[METHOD, abstract-level via aggregator listings]** Neuhaus & Kalbfleisch: standard models ignore the distinction between between- and within-cluster covariate effects; the two often differ in example data and ignoring this can mislead; conditional likelihood estimates purely within-cluster effects, whereas mixture (random-effects) models estimate a weighted average of between- and within-cluster effects — [Neuhaus & Kalbfleisch 1998, Biometrics 54(2):638–645, doi:10.2307/3109770](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/between-and-within-cluster-covariate-effects-in-the-analysis-of-clustered-data/812074010140475393-922)
- **[METHOD, abstract only]** Begg & Parides review approaches for separating individual-level and cluster-level covariate effects, emphasise the interpretation of the cluster-level effect, and give model-fitting recommendations that depend on the analyst's intent — [Begg & Parides 2003, Stat Med 22(16):2591–2602, doi:10.1002/sim.1524](https://www.scilit.net/publications/cf3c6d7891d13950bce89c8251544077)
- **[METHOD/OPINION, search summary of open-access paper]** Bell, Fairbrother & Jones recommend the within–between random-effects (REWB) model as a starting point, note that within, between and contextual effects "should not be assumed to be the same", and report simulations in which non-normal random intercepts caused only modest bias while omitted random slopes made standard errors too small — [Bell, Fairbrother & Jones 2019, Quality & Quantity 53:1051–1074, doi:10.1007/s11135-018-0802-x](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/134123/)
- **[METHOD, search summary]** For predictions from random-intercept logistic models, marginal predictions were miscalibrated when confounding by cluster (within ≠ between) or informative cluster size was ignored; adding the cluster mean of the exposure improved calibration and discrimination — [Pavlou, Ambler & Omar 2021, BMC Med Res Methodol 21:135, doi:10.1186/s12874-021-01321-x](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12874-021-01321-x)

### Inferences
- Curvature and taper vary both between people (person mean) and within people (segment deviation from the person mean, and systematically by segment position, part of which the segment intercepts absorb). The per-SD HR for them is therefore a blend; the honest wording is "per SD higher curvature, comparing segments with the same position and the same person-level covariates and random intercept, whether that difference arises between or within persons". "Within the same person" is an overstatement; "between persons" equally so.
- The LM bifurcation angle and the diameter ratios have no within-person variation, so their coefficients are purely between-person and are identified only against the random-intercept distribution (they are the terms most exposed to the conditional/marginal distinction of section 1).
- A descriptive secondary display is defensible within the design: report for curvature and taper the share of variance that is between persons, between segment positions and residual, and optionally refit with person-mean and deviation terms as a labelled sensitivity display (not tested, not replacing the main model). Equal between and within coefficients would support reading the single β simply; unequal ones would be reported as a limitation. This follows the technique in the sources above, but no source was found that prescribes it for a prognostic factor study.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Neuhaus & Kalbfleisch 1998 and Begg & Parides 2003 were not read; the exact weighting formula for the blended coefficient in a nonlinear mixed model was not verified. Mundlak 1978 (Econometrica) was not consulted.
- No source specific to the cloglog link.

## 3. Scaling of the hazard ratio: per SD of what, per unit, or quantile contrasts

### Takeaway
No guideline was found that fixes the scaling; the choice of SD (all segments pooled, within segment position, within person) changes the number without changing the model or the LR test, so the SD used must be stated with its value in physical units. A quantile contrast (e.g. interquartile range) is the usual alternative.

### Cited Findings
- **[GUIDELINE, verified]** PROGRESS 2 asks for continuous factors to be analysed "on their continuous scale, thereby avoiding the use of arbitrary cut-points", for examination of non-linear relationships, and for effect sizes with confidence intervals; it gives no recommendation on per-SD versus per-unit scaling — [Riley et al. 2013, PLoS Med 10(2):e1001380, doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1001380](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1001380)
- **[OPINION, secondary web source]** Standardised coefficients depend on the sample SD of the predictor, so the same per-unit effect gives different standardised effects in populations with different spread — [University of Virginia Library, "The Shortcomings of Standardized Regression Coefficients"](https://library.virginia.edu/data/articles/the-shortcomings-of-standardized-regression-coefficients). Greenland, Schlesselman & Criqui 1986 ("The fallacy of employing standardized regression coefficients and correlations as measures of effect", Am J Epidemiol) is the standard primary reference for this point but was not retrieved.

### Inferences
- (Arithmetic, no source needed.) HR per k units = exp(kβ). With total SD² = between-position variance + between-person variance + residual, an HR per pooled SD is larger than an HR per within-position SD, which is larger than an HR per within-person SD. Because the model contains segment intercepts, the variation that actually identifies β is the within-position variation; scaling by the pooled SD expresses β over a contrast (e.g. proximal versus distal curvature) that the model does not use, and can make the HR look larger than the contrast the data support. The within-position SD is therefore the scaling most consistent with the fitted model; whichever is chosen, give SDs in physical units in the same table so readers can rescale.
- Per-SD HRs make the four geometry terms comparable with each other in a forest plot but are cohort-specific; per-unit HRs (per 0.1 mm⁻¹, per 10°) are transportable. Reporting both in one table costs one column.

### Gaps
- Harrell's Regression Modeling Strategies recommendation of interquartile-range effects (default of `summary.rms`) could **not be verified in the book**; only an informal R-help thread surfaced. Treat as "widely attributed to Harrell, not verified here".
- No statement on scaling was found in REMARK, TRIPOD or STROBE during this search (STROBE and TRIPOD full texts were not read).

## 4. Why ΔC is a poor measure of the strength of an association; what the guidelines ask for

### Takeaway
A small ΔC is the expected result for a genuine factor added to a reasonable base model and says little about the strength of the association; conversely a hazard ratio alone does not show classification ability. The prognostic factor guidelines ask for adjusted effect estimates with confidence intervals over established factors, not for discrimination gains.

### Cited Findings
- **[METHOD, abstract/secondary]** Pepe et al.: an odds ratio as high as 3 corresponds to a poor classifier (at 10% false positives it detects only about 25% of cases); a single association measure does not describe a marker's ability to classify — [Pepe, Janes, Longton, Leisenring & Newcomb 2004, Am J Epidemiol 159(9):882–890, doi:10.1093/aje/kwh101](https://edrn.nci.nih.gov/data-and-resources/publications/15105181-919-limitations-of-the-odds-ratio-in-gauging-the-performance-of-a-diagnostic-prognostic-or-screening-marker). This cuts both ways: it explains a small ΔC, and it forbids presenting an HR as evidence of predictive usefulness.
- **[OPINION, verified]** Harrell: the c-index "does not sufficiently reward extreme predictions that are correct"; comparing two c-indexes "is a low-power procedure"; the likelihood ratio χ² test is the "gold-standard" for added value. Adequacy index = LR_A/LR_AB (base model A, combined AB); fraction of new information = 1 − adequacy; also 1 − var(pre-test predicted risk)/var(post-test predicted risk), and relative explained variation as a ratio of variances of predicted values. Suggested displays: back-to-back histograms of predicted risk without and with the new variables, and a scatter of post- versus pre-test risk. NRI and IDI "are not needed" — [Harrell, "Statistically Efficient Ways to Quantify Added Predictive Value of New Measurements", fharrell.com (blog, not peer reviewed)](https://www.fharrell.com/post/addvalue/)
- **[METHOD, search summary]** Cook 2007: the AUC is an insensitive measure for model comparison and has little direct clinical relevance — [Cook 2007, Circulation 115:928–935; summary on author's site](https://ncook.bwh.harvard.edu/)
- **[METHOD, abstract]** Pencina et al. 2008 argue for measures in addition to the AUC when judging a new marker — [Pencina, D'Agostino Sr, D'Agostino Jr & Vasan 2008, Stat Med 27:157–172, doi:10.1002/sim.2929](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/sim.2929)
- **[METHOD, abstract/search summary]** Pencina et al. 2012: the behaviour of incremental-value metrics depends on the strength of the baseline model. Commentary by Kerr, Bansal & Pepe in the same issue: with a near-perfect baseline model any measure of incremental value should be small, and ΔAUC, IDI and NRI(>0) are not clinically meaningful — [Pencina, D'Agostino, Pencina, Janssens & Greenland 2012, Am J Epidemiol 176(6):473–481, doi:10.1093/aje/kws207](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22875756/)
- **[OPINION, lecture slides]** A ΔAUC of 0.02 is "large" on the AUC scale, and p-values for ΔAUC are invalid — [Kerr, SISCER course slides, University of Washington](https://si.biostat.washington.edu/sites/default/files/modules/Kerr_SISCER2023mod5_part3_update12July2023.pdf). Supports the thesis decision to estimate but never test ΔC.
- **[GUIDELINE, verified]** PROGRESS 2: prognostic factor studies should include "multivariable analyses that assess a factor's prognostic value over existing prognostic factors", report effect sizes with confidence intervals (extractable in only 35.5% of assessments in one review), pre-specify factors and outcomes with a protocol and analysis plan, label exploratory studies as such, and be replicated. It makes **no recommendation about the C statistic** — [Riley et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1001380)
- **[GUIDELINE, search summary quoting items]** REMARK item 16: for key multivariable analyses report estimated effects (e.g. hazard ratio) with confidence intervals for the marker and, at least for the final model, all other variables. Item 17: provide estimated effects with confidence intervals from an analysis in which the marker and standard prognostic variables are included, regardless of statistical significance — [Altman, McShane, Sauerbrei & Taube 2012, PLoS Med / BMC Med 10:51](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362085). REMARK is written for tumour markers; PROGRESS 2 states most of it generalises.
- **[GUIDELINE, abstract]** TRIPOD-Cluster is a 19-item checklist for prediction models developed or validated in clustered data and stresses reporting heterogeneity in performance across clusters; it concerns prediction models with clusters such as studies or hospitals — [Debray et al. 2023, BMJ 380:e071018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36750242/)

### Inferences
- The thesis hierarchy (LR test and HRs main; ΔC and calibration secondary, estimated not tested) matches PROGRESS 2 and REMARK items 16–17, and matches Harrell's position that the LR test is the efficient test of added information.
- REMARK item 16 implies the main table should show all ten covariates' HRs (six risk factors and four geometry terms), not geometry alone.
- TRIPOD-Cluster is only loosely applicable: the thesis is not a prediction model study and its clusters are persons, not centres. Citing it for the clustered-data handling is reasonable; claiming compliance is not.
- A sentence such as "ΔC was small, so geometry is unimportant" and its mirror "the HR is significant, so geometry improves prediction" are both unsupported (Pepe 2004).

### Gaps
- Not read: TRIPOD 2015, TRIPOD+AI 2024, STROBE, QUIPS, CHARMS, PROGRESS 1 and 3, Kerr et al. 2014 (Epidemiology, on NRI), Vickers' papers, Riley et al. "Prognosis Research in Healthcare", Steyerberg's book. No claims are made from them.
- The specific statement that a marker needs a very large independent association to shift the AUC meaningfully (commonly attributed to Pepe 2004 and Ware 2006 NEJM) was not verified in the primary texts beyond the OR = 3 example.

## 5. Measures that express a segment-level effect at person level without diluting it

### Takeaway
The measures with the firmest footing for this design are model-based predicted person risks contrasted across geometry (integrated over the random intercept), the median hazard ratio as a yardstick for the HRs, the LR-based fraction of new information, and, with caveats, a latent-scale variance partition; explained-variation formulas exist for cloglog (π²/6), heterogeneity measures were derived for logit or Cox frailty models and transfer only by argument.

### Cited Findings
- **[METHOD, verified]** Median odds ratio: MOR = exp(√(2τ²)·Φ⁻¹(0.75)), Φ⁻¹(0.75) = 0.6745; latent VPC/ICC for logit τ²/(τ² + π²/3), probit τ²/(τ² + 1); simulation-based VPC (Goldstein) depends on covariate pattern; 80% interval odds ratio and proportion of opposed odds ratios for cluster-level covariates; proportional change in variance (τ²_null − τ²_full)/τ²_null, which must be rescaled for binary outcomes and "should not be treated like" R²; Snijders–Bosker R² = σ²_F/(σ²_F + τ² + π²/3) — [Austin & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471). All formulas are for the **logit** link (the MOR concept originates with Larsen & Merlo 2005, Am J Epidemiol, not read).
- **[METHOD, verified]** Median hazard ratio for a Cox frailty model with normal random effects on the log-hazard scale: MHR = exp(√(2σ²)·Φ⁻¹(0.75)); for gamma frailty with variance θ it is the 75th percentile of F(2/θ, 2/θ). Recommended use: report it beside the frailty variance and set it next to the covariate HRs to judge relative magnitude (in the case study the clustering effect, MHR 1.15, was smaller than 10 of 11 patient-characteristic HRs) — [Austin, Wagner & Merlo 2017, Stat Med 36(6):928–938, doi:10.1002/sim.7188](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5299617)
- **[METHOD, verified in preprint of published paper]** The latent distributions for logit, probit and cloglog links have theoretical variances π²/3, 1 and **π²/6**; marginal R² is the proportion of total variance explained by fixed effects, conditional R² by fixed plus random effects; for binary data the observation-level alternative 1/(p(1−p)) always gives different (larger variance, hence smaller R²/ICC) values than the theoretical one — [Nakagawa, Johnson & Schielzeth 2017, J R Soc Interface 14:20170213, doi:10.1098/rsif.2017.0213; text read in bioRxiv v4](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/095851v4.full)
- **[OPINION, verified]** Adequacy index LR_base/LR_full and fraction of new information 1 − LR_base/LR_full; 1 − var(P̂_base)/var(P̂_full); back-to-back histograms of predicted risk — [Harrell, fharrell.com](https://www.fharrell.com/post/addvalue/)
- **[METHOD, abstract/search summary]** Skrondal & Rabe-Hesketh distinguish predicted probabilities for a new unit in a hypothetical cluster (random effect fixed), in a new randomly sampled cluster (population-averaged, integrate over the random-effect distribution) and in an existing cluster (posterior/cluster-averaged), with standard errors assessed by simulation for random-intercept logistic models — [Skrondal & Rabe-Hesketh 2009, J R Stat Soc A 172(3):659–687, doi:10.1111/j.1467-985X.2009.00587.x](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v172y2009i3p659-687.html)

### Inferences
- The cloglog coefficients and the person intercept are on the log-hazard scale, so the Austin–Wagner–Merlo formula MHR = exp(0.954·σ_u) applies to the fitted model by the same argument (difference of two normal intercepts). This transfer is an inference; no source was found that defines the MHR for a discrete-time cloglog GLMM. Use: "the median HR between two persons with identical covariates is X; the geometry HR per SD is Y". This places the geometry effect against unexplained person-to-person heterogeneity without any dilution through pooling.
- A latent VPC of σ_u²/(σ_u² + π²/6) and marginal/conditional R² on the latent scale are computable for cloglog from the Nakagawa constant. The Gumbel latent variable is asymmetric and the R² is not a proportion of variance in observed plaque; present as descriptive, and prefer the difference in marginal R² between the base and full model over its absolute value. Austin & Merlo's warning about rescaling variances across models with different fixed effects applies.
- Person-level contrasts that avoid dilution, all computed from the one fitted model: (a) average predicted person risk with geometry set to its 10th versus 90th percentile, covariates as observed, integrated over u (standardised risks and their difference); (b) the same for "all nine segments high versus all low" and for "one segment high" (see section 6 for the closed form); (c) partial-effect curves of predicted person risk against each geometry term. These are model-based predictions, to be described as such and never as effects of changing geometry (no causal wording). Confidence bands by person-level bootstrap or parametric simulation; no source read here prescribes one.
- The fraction of new information from the LR statistics is the measure closest to the prespecified test and is unaffected by pooling, conditional/marginal choice or SD scaling.

### Gaps
- No source was found that develops the MOR, MHR, IOR, POOR or PCV for a cloglog GLMM specifically.
- Marginal standardisation / g-computation references (e.g. Muller & MacLehose 2014 Int J Epidemiol; Localio et al. 2007) were not retrieved.
- Whether R² for binary GLMMs is a useful quantity at all is debated; the debate was not reviewed.

## 6. The aggregation step 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) under the cloglog link

### Takeaway
Conditional on the person intercept, the pooled person risk is exactly a cloglog model whose cumulative hazard is the sum of the segment hazards, so a person-constant covariate keeps its hazard ratio unchanged at person level, while a covariate raised in one segment scales the person hazard only by that segment's share. Integration over the random intercept is required for correct person-level risks; setting it to zero gives miscalibrated risks.

### Cited Findings
- **[METHOD, abstract/search summary]** Hedeker, Siddiqui & Hu develop random-effects models for correlated grouped-time survival data with proportional hazards (cloglog) and proportional odds (logit) versions, as ordinal or as person-period binary models, with normal or rectangular random effects by quadrature — [Hedeker, Siddiqui & Hu 2000, Stat Methods Med Res 9(2):161–179, PMID 10946432](https://bibsonomy.org/bibtex/24b0e02cebf0d53f4e9cbdbe037249cd5/jkd)
- **[OPINION, slides via search summary]** For grouped-time data the cloglog link "would seem to be the best choice", in agreement with the continuous-time Cox proportional hazards model — [Hedeker, clustered grouped-time survival slides, SSI](https://ssicentraldev.azurewebsites.net/images/pdfs/Survival_clust.pdf)
- **[METHOD, verified]** Logistic random-intercept model: correct marginal risk is P̂ = ∫ expit(α + u + x'β) f(u) du. Setting u = 0 gave "poorly calibrated overall marginal predictions (calibration slope <1 and calibration in the large ≠ 0)": at ICC 0.30 slope 0.788 and intercept 0.142; at ICC 0.05 slope 0.948 and intercept −0.008. "The correct way to obtain marginal risk predictions from a random intercepts model is to integrate over the distribution of random effects." Simulation evidence, logit link — [Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751)
- **[METHOD]** Types of prediction (hypothetical cluster with u fixed, new cluster integrated, existing cluster posterior) — [Skrondal & Rabe-Hesketh 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v172y2009i3p659-687.html)

### Inferences (derivations made here; algebra only, no source)
- Conditional on u_i and assuming conditional independence of segments (the model's assumption): 1 − ∏_s(1 − p_is) = 1 − exp(−e^{u_i} Σ_s H_is). Hence cloglog(P_i | u_i) = u_i + log Σ_s exp(η_is): the person-level model is again cloglog, with person cumulative hazard equal to the sum of segment cumulative hazards. This is the grouped-time proportional hazards property (Prentice & Gloeckler 1978) applied across segments instead of across time intervals.
- **Person-constant covariate** (bifurcation angle, a diameter ratio entered for all segments, the six risk factors): e^{γx} factors out of the sum, so the person-level conditional HR equals the segment-level HR exactly. No dilution from pooling. If a diameter ratio enters only the segments of one artery, it behaves as the next case.
- **Segment-varying covariate** (curvature, taper): raising z by one SD in segment s alone multiplies the person hazard by 1 + w_is(e^β − 1), where w_is = H_is/Σ_t H_it is that segment's share of the person's hazard. Raising it by one SD in all nine segments multiplies the person hazard by e^β. With nine similar segments, w ≈ 1/9, so an HR of 1.30 in one segment gives a person-level HR of about 1.03, whereas the same shift in all segments gives 1.30. This is the precise form of the "dilution": it is a property of the contrast chosen, not a loss of information, and it argues for reporting both contrasts with their definitions. The share w_is is larger in segments with high baseline hazard (large α_s), so the same local change matters more at person level in segments where plaque is common.
- **Marginal person risk**: P_i = 1 − E_u[exp(−e^u Σ_s H_is)], a one-dimensional integral over u of the pooled expression (Gauss–Hermite). It is **not** equal to 1 − ∏(1 − p̄_is) with marginal segment risks p̄_is, because segments of one person share u and are positively dependent; pooling marginal segment risks as if independent overstates person risk. Pooling with u = 0 gives the risk of a "median" person, generally not the population-average risk (direction by Jensen's inequality; magnitude shown for logit by Pavlou 2015, not for cloglog).
- Consequence for ΔC at person level: curvature and taper enter person risk only through Σ_s exp(η_is), a hazard-weighted aggregate dominated by the high-hazard segments, so person-level discrimination uses mostly the between-person component of these covariates. A small person-level ΔC together with a clear segment-level HR is the expected pattern and not a contradiction. Segment-level C (overall and within person) uses the within-person component; reporting both, labelled, shows where the information sits.

### Gaps
- Prentice & Gloeckler 1978 (Biometrics 34:57–67), Allison 1982 (Sociological Methodology) and Singer & Willett 2003 were **not read**; the full text of Hedeker et al. 2000 could not be opened (PDF fetch returned empty). The statement that cloglog coefficients equal continuous-time log hazard ratios and are invariant to interval length is standard but unverified here.
- No source was found that writes out the pooling 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) across anatomical sites under a cloglog GLMM, or the share formula above. The derivation should be checked and can be stated in the thesis as a property of the model.
- Evidence on miscalibration from ignoring u is simulation-based and for the logit link only.

## 7. How comparable multi-site-within-person studies present results

### Takeaway
Applied coronary papers typically show lesion- or segment-level and patient-level predictors side by side in one table of hazard or odds ratios, and ophthalmology has explicit tutorials endorsing the site as the unit of analysis with clustering handled by mixed or marginal models. None of what was found is a methodological recommendation on avoiding dilution, and the statistical methods of the coronary papers could mostly not be verified.

### Cited Findings
- **[APPLIED, secondary reports of the NEJM tables]** PROSPECT (Stone et al. 2011, NEJM 364:226–235) reported independent predictors of nonculprit-lesion events at lesion level (plaque burden ≥ 70% HR 5.03, minimal lumen area ≤ 4.0 mm² HR 3.21, thin-cap fibroatheroma HR 3.35) and at patient level (insulin-requiring diabetes HR 3.32, 95% CI 1.43–7.72; previous PCI HR 2.03, 1.15–3.59) as separate blocks — [TCTMD report of PROSPECT](https://www.tctmd.com/news/prospect-published-combined-ivus-identifies-risky-nonculprit-acs-lesions). Values from secondary sources; verify in the original before citing. The lesion-level predictors are dichotomised, which PROGRESS 2 advises against.
- **[APPLIED, abstract]** Samady et al. 2011 (Circulation 124:779–788) analysed 2,249 IVUS segments by low/intermediate/high wall shear stress categories — [search summary; related segment-level WSS work](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4306111). Whether a patient random effect was used was not verified.
- **[APPLIED, abstract]** PARADIGM: 1,297 patients, 3,218 non-obstructive lesions, 76 lesions (2.4%) in 60 patients became obstructive; a per-lesion model was reported superior to a per-patient model for predicting obstructive lesion development — [van Rosendael/ PARADIGM investigators 2020, Int J Cardiovasc Imaging, doi:10.1007/s10554-020-01960-z (author attribution not verified)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/). This is a comparison of two models' discrimination, i.e. outside the thesis design; it illustrates that lesion-level information is diluted in patient-level summaries.
- **[METHOD, tutorial, abstract/search summary]** Ying, Maguire, Glynn & Rosner: for correlated eye data, ignoring inter-eye correlation leaves coefficients unbiased but variances wrong; mixed-effects and marginal (GEE) models with the eye as unit of analysis give valid inference — [Ying et al. 2017, Ophthalmic Epidemiol 24(2):130–140, doi:10.1080/09286586.2016.1259636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5597052); binary-outcome tutorial: Ying et al. 2018, Ophthalmic Epidemiol 25(1):1–12, doi:10.1080/09286586.2017.1320413 ([listing](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/17eaa971ce12ca3d643a98c09d35fae6e/jkd))
- **[METHOD, search summary]** Glynn & Rosner: using the eye as the unit of analysis with the correlation handled properly makes the best use of the data and keeps covariate effects interpretable — [Glynn & Rosner 2012, Ophthalmic Epidemiol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3454458)

### Inferences
- The common applied layout (one table, site-level block and person-level block of adjusted ratios, each with CI) is compatible with the thesis table: four geometry HRs marked by level of measurement (segment or person/artery) beside the six risk-factor HRs.
- The applied coronary literature gives little precedent for explicit conditional-versus-marginal labelling or for within–between reporting; doing so would be more careful than common practice, not a deviation from it.

### Gaps
- Not searched or not verified: PREDICTION (Stone 2012 Circulation), SCOT-HEART and CONFIRM lesion-level analyses, Gijsen/Wentzel/Bourantas WSS studies' statistical methods and table layouts, periodontal site/tooth-level methodology, carotid and vertebral analogues. The original PROSPECT article was not opened.
- No paper was found that pools site-level risks to a person-level risk by 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) and reports both levels.

## 8. Concrete presentation recommendations tied to the sources

### Takeaway
Headline: the LR test of the geometry block and the conditional per-SD hazard ratios with 95% CI for all model terms; then translate to person level with integrated predicted risks under explicitly defined contrasts, and give the median hazard ratio and fraction of new information as context. Keep ΔC and calibration as labelled secondary estimates at both levels.

### Cited Findings (each recommendation with the source it rests on)
- Headline number: LR χ² (df, p) for the geometry block, as the efficient test of added information — [Harrell (opinion)](https://www.fharrell.com/post/addvalue/); adjusted effect estimates with CIs over established factors — [PROGRESS 2 (guideline)](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1001380)
- Main table: HR with 95% CI for geometry **and** all other covariates, regardless of significance — [REMARK items 16–17 (guideline)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362085). Add the random-intercept SD with the median hazard ratio beside it — [Austin, Wagner & Merlo 2017 (method; transfer to cloglog GLMM is an inference)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5299617)
- Continuous geometry kept continuous, linearity examined — [PROGRESS 2](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1001380)
- Person-level risks obtained by integrating over the random intercept, never with u = 0 presented as population risk — [Pavlou et al. 2015 (method, logit simulations)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751); state which type of prediction is shown — [Skrondal & Rabe-Hesketh 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v172y2009i3p659-687.html)
- Added information: fraction of new information 1 − LR_base/LR_full and back-to-back histograms of predicted person risk for base and full model — [Harrell (opinion)](https://www.fharrell.com/post/addvalue/)
- ΔC reported with interval, not tested, with the caveat that it is insensitive — [Cook 2007](https://ncook.bwh.harvard.edu/); [Pepe et al. 2004](https://edrn.nci.nih.gov/data-and-resources/publications/15105181-919-limitations-of-the-odds-ratio-in-gauging-the-performance-of-a-diagnostic-prognostic-or-screening-marker); [Kerr slides (opinion)](https://si.biostat.washington.edu/sites/default/files/modules/Kerr_SISCER2023mod5_part3_update12July2023.pdf)
- Explained variation, if shown: latent-scale marginal R² for base and full model using π²/6, labelled descriptive — [Nakagawa et al. 2017](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/095851v4.full); caution on comparing variances across models — [Austin & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471)
- Wording for the single β of curvature and taper as a blend of between- and within-person information — [Neuhaus & Kalbfleisch 1998](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/between-and-within-cluster-covariate-effects-in-the-analysis-of-clustered-data/812074010140475393-922)
- Person-level geometry terms (angle, ratios): interpret through population-averaged predicted risks, since cluster-specific ratios for cluster-constant covariates are awkward — [Austin & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471)

### Inferences (proposed layout; a synthesis, not taken from any one source)
- **Table 1 (main):** ten covariates, HR per SD (SD definition and value in units stated; per-unit HR in a second column), 95% CI, level of measurement; below: LR test of the geometry block, σ_u, median hazard ratio, fraction of new information.
- **Figure 1:** forest plot of the four geometry HRs per SD (optionally with the six risk factors for scale and the MHR as a reference line).
- **Figure 2:** predicted person risk (integrated over u, other covariates as observed) across each geometry term, with band; for curvature and taper two curves or two tabulated contrasts: all segments shifted, and one named segment shifted (e.g. the proximal LAD), using the share formula of section 6.
- **Table 2 (secondary):** C and calibration at segment level and at person level, base versus full, ΔC with interval, no p-values; optionally within-person C; calibration plots at both levels from cross-validated, u-integrated predictions.
- **Secondary descriptive display:** variance of curvature and taper split into between-person, between-position and residual parts; optional person-mean/deviation refit labelled as sensitivity.
- **Statements to avoid:** reading the conditional HR as a population-averaged effect; applying the logit constant 0.346 to the cloglog model; "within the same person, a segment with higher curvature..." for β (it is a blend); "geometry increases/causes plaque" or risk-change wording for the predicted-risk contrasts; "geometry improves prediction" on the strength of an HR; "geometry is unimportant" on the strength of a small ΔC; presenting u = 0 pooled risks as average person risk; describing a one-segment contrast and an all-segment contrast without saying which is which; comparing per-SD HRs across studies with different SD definitions; presenting the fitted model as a tool.
