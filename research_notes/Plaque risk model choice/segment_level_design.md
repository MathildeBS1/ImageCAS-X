# Segment-level outcomes, participant-level risk: multilevel design for the Herlev–Østerbro CCTA follow-up

Scope note: 16 search/fetch calls. Statistical-method facts that are standard textbook results but were not retrieved and verified in this session are put under Inferences and marked "(not verified this session)". Everything under Cited Findings has a source that was retrieved.

## 1. Model family: GLMM vs GEE vs cluster-robust GLM, cloglog with log(interval) offset, conditional vs marginal, roll-up to participant risk, testing "geometry adds"

### Takeaway
Use a complementary log-log (cloglog) binary model per segment with offset log(scan interval), so coefficients are log hazard ratios under a constant-hazard assumption and unequal intervals are handled exactly. Fit it as a marginal model (GEE or GLM with participant-clustered sandwich SEs) if the target is population-average segment risk and a participant-level roll-up; use a GLMM with participant (and artery) random intercepts if heterogeneity between participants is itself of interest. Test "geometry adds" at the participant level with a block Wald test (cluster-robust) plus participant-level bootstrap and participant-grouped cross-validation of discrimination and calibration.

### Cited Findings
- The grouped-data (interval-censored) survival model is equivalent to a binary-response model with a complementary log-log link; this is the discrete analog of the continuous proportional hazards model — [SAS PROC LOGISTIC documentation, interval-censored survival example](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/63962/HTML/default/statug_logistic_sect072.htm)
- With unequal interval lengths, adding log(exposure) as an offset makes the cloglog model equivalent to a piecewise-constant proportional hazards model, and the coefficients equal continuous-time log hazard ratios — [MetricGate, discrete-time survival with cloglog (2026, non-peer-reviewed tutorial)](https://metricgate.com/docs/discrete-time-survival-cloglog/); concept also in [Prentice–Gloeckler discrete PH note](https://metricgate.com/docs/prentice-gloeckler-discrete-ph/). Treat as secondary sources; the primary reference is Prentice & Gloeckler (Biometrics 1978), not retrieved here.
- Hubbard et al. (Epidemiology 2010;21:467–474), "To GEE or not to GEE", argue that mixed models rest on unverifiable assumptions about the random-effects distribution that can bias inference, and that population-average (GEE) models are often preferable when the question is an average association — [Berkeley CTML record](https://ctml.berkeley.edu/publications/gee-or-not-gee-comparing-population-average-and-mixed-models-estimating-associations)
- Counterpoint (Subramanian & O'Malley 2010 commentary on Hubbard): marginal and mixed models have "fundamentally distinct targets of inference" (population-average vs cluster-conditional); mixed models are the right choice when heterogeneity between clusters is itself of interest; GEE answers only "on average is there an association" — [PMC3085136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3085136/)
- The same commentary notes that with small ICCs (2–5% in neighbourhood studies) the practical difference between the two approaches is inconsequential — [PMC3085136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3085136/)
- Precedent for mixed models on segment-level CCTA data: Tuncay et al. (J Cardiovasc Comput Tomogr 2018) used linear mixed models for segment- and artery-level comparisons of curvature/tortuosity in 73 asymptomatic higher-risk individuals — [UMC Utrecht record](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/non-invasive-assessment-of-coronary-artery-geometry-using-coronar/)
- Precedent for GEE: CCTA geometry vs plaque type and significant stenosis assessed by generalized estimating equations — [search-result snippet, PMC10644144](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10644144) (full text not fetched; confirm before citing)
- Precedent for logistic/linear mixed models "correcting for intra-patient clustering" at vessel level in CCTA plaque vs perfusion work — [search-result snippet, PMC7554067](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7554067) (not fetched)

### Inferences
- The cloglog link has a structural advantage over logit for the roll-up. If each segment s has hazard λ_s = exp(x_s β) and segments are conditionally independent given the participant, P(any event in interval t) = 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) = 1 − exp(−t Σ λ_s). The participant-level hazard is the sum of segment hazards, so the participant model is again cloglog with offset log t. Under the logit link the roll-up has no closed form. (Derivation is standard; not verified this session.)
- The product formula 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) assumes independence of segments given covariates. With a marginal (GEE/robust) fit this independence does not hold, because segments within a person are positively correlated, so the formula overstates participant risk. Two fixes: (a) fit a GLMM and integrate over the random intercept, P_i = 1 − E_b[∏(1 − p_s(b))], by Gauss–Hermite quadrature or Monte Carlo; or (b) recalibrate the rolled-up score against the observed participant-level outcome with a one-parameter logistic/cloglog calibration on held-out folds. Option (b) is simpler and is what the participant-level validation needs anyway. (Not verified this session.)
- Conditional vs marginal: for a random-intercept logistic GLMM with variance σ², marginal log-odds ratios are attenuated by roughly 1/√(1 + 0.346σ²) (Zeger, Liang & Albert, Biometrics 1988). For the cloglog GLMM a normal random intercept gives no closed form, but a log-gamma (gamma frailty) random effect does. Report which estimand is used; the participant-risk question needs marginal (integrated) predictions, not predictions at b = 0. (Not verified this session.)
- Recommended structure: segment (≈16–18 per person) within artery (3–4) within participant. An artery-level random intercept is usually weakly identified with about 4–6 segments per artery and a rare outcome. Start with a participant random intercept only and add artery as a sensitivity analysis. Under GEE, cluster on participant with an exchangeable or independence working correlation; independence plus sandwich SEs is the most robust when cluster-level covariates (risk factors) are mixed with segment-level ones.
- Testing "geometry adds": (1) a block Wald test of all geometry coefficients using the cluster-robust covariance, or an LRT in the GLMM (LRTs are not valid under GEE, which has no likelihood); (2) the participant-level ΔAUC, Δ log-likelihood or Brier change of the rolled-up risk, comparing Model 1 (risk factors) with Model 2 (risk factors + geometry), with CIs from a participant-level (cluster) bootstrap that resamples whole participants and refits; (3) grouped k-fold CV with folds split by participant, never by segment, otherwise segments of the same person leak across folds. This is consistent with the participant-bootstrap plan in `regression_methodology.md`.
- Segment-level geometry, participant-level risk factors: put both in one segment-level model. Geometry varies within person, so its effect is identified mostly within person and is protected from person-level confounding if a person-mean term is added (within/between decomposition, "Mundlak" / hybrid model). Recommended: include the participant mean of each geometry variable as a covariate. The within-person coefficient answers "is this segment more at risk because of its local geometry"; the between-person coefficient answers "do people with more tortuous trees have more plaque". Only the between-person component contributes to *who* develops plaque. (Not verified this session.)

### Gaps
- No retrieved paper fits a cloglog GLMM with a log-interval offset to serial-CCTA segment outcomes. This appears to be an uncommon specification in the CCTA literature (PARADIGM papers use annualised change and logistic models, see section 4).
- Primary references for Zeger–Liang–Albert attenuation, Prentice–Gloeckler and the Mundlak within/between decomposition were not fetched; cite from the original papers after checking.

## 2. Effective sample size with clustered binary outcomes; ICC of plaque across segments; Riley criteria for clustered data

### Takeaway
For the participant-level question, the unit of sample size is the participant and the number of participants with an event. Segment counts do not multiply information for person-level predictors (risk factors), and only partly for segment-level geometry (design effect 1 + (m − 1)ICC). Riley et al.'s criteria (shrinkage ≥ 0.9, optimism in Nagelkerke R² ≤ 0.05, precise overall risk) should be applied at the participant level. No published extension of these criteria to clustered segment-level development data was found.

### Cited Findings
- Riley et al. minimum sample size for binary-outcome prediction models: three criteria. (i) Global shrinkage factor ≥ 0.9. (ii) Absolute difference ≤ 0.05 between apparent and adjusted Nagelkerke R². (iii) Precise estimation of overall risk. Requires a prespecified anticipated Cox–Snell R². Implemented in R package `pmsampsize` — [pmsampsize reference manual](https://rdrr.io/pkg/pmsampsize/man/pmsampsize.html); papers: Riley et al. BMJ 2020 and Stat Med 2019 (Part II binary and time-to-event) — [PMC6519266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519266)
- Riley et al. (2024, arXiv 2407.09293; later published) add a five-step approach targeting precision of individual risk estimates, with "prediction and classification instability plots" and the `pmstabilityss` software; it does not specifically address clustered data — [arXiv 2407.09293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09293v1)
- Riley et al. (2025, arXiv 2504.18730) propose a general sample size framework for developing or updating a prediction model (Fisher-information based) — [arXiv 2504.18730](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18730v1) (abstract-level only)
- Example of the magnitude of within-person correlation for neighbourhood data: ICC typically < 2–5% — [PMC3085136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3085136/) (shown for contrast: coronary segments within a person are expected to correlate much more strongly)

### Inferences
- Design effect: DEFF = 1 + (m − 1)·ICC, n_eff = N_segments / DEFF (Kish; not verified this session). With m = 17 segments per person:
  - ICC 0.05 → DEFF 1.8 → 34,000 segments ≈ 18,900 effective;
  - ICC 0.20 → DEFF 4.2 → ≈ 8,100 effective;
  - ICC 0.40 → DEFF 7.4 → ≈ 4,600 effective.
  These figures apply to segment-level covariates only, and only approximately for binary outcomes. For participant-level covariates (age, LDL...) the effective n is essentially the 2,000 participants, and the relevant event count is participants with ≥1 event.
- Expected ICC for plaque across segments: no direct estimate was found (see Gaps). Plaque is strongly clustered within persons: extent scores such as SIS are driven by person-level risk, and about half of a general population has zero plaque. Latent-scale ICCs of 0.3–0.6 are plausible for prevalent plaque. For *incident* plaque over a few years, the ICC is probably lower but still well above 0.1. Treat ICC as unknown and estimate it from the data as σ²/(σ² + π²/3) on the logit scale, or σ²/(σ² + π²/6) on the cloglog scale. Report it.
- Practical Riley application: define the parameter count P for the participant-level model (risk factors ~8 df + geometry block ~5–10 df ≈ 15–20 df), anticipate the outcome prevalence (section 5), and take Cox–Snell R² from a conservative 15% of max R². Then check that 2,000 participants meets criterion (i). Example (not computed with the software here): at ~30–40% participant-level event proportion and P ≈ 20, the required n is typically several hundred to ~1,500. 2,000 participants is then likely adequate for the participant-level model, but not for many separate domain- or artery-specific models.
- A segment-level model with many segment-level interaction terms (geometry × baseline status × artery) spends df against an effective n far below the raw segment count. Penalise (ridge) or restrict the specification in advance.

### Gaps
- No published ICC for plaque presence or incident plaque across coronary segments within persons was found in this search. Recommend estimating it in the cohort, or reading SCAPIS/CONFIRM segment-level papers in full.
- No extension of Riley et al.'s criteria to multilevel/clustered *development* data was located in this session (there is related work on clustered *validation* sample size, e.g. multicentre IPD, not retrieved).

## 3. Baseline plaque: incidence vs progression, reverse causation via remodeling

### Takeaway
Plaque changes the lumen. Up to roughly 40% stenosis, compensatory (positive) remodeling preserves lumen area by enlarging the vessel (Glagov 1987). So lumen-based geometry (radius, daughter ratio, curvature of the lumen centreline, bifurcation angle) at diseased segments is partly a consequence of the outcome process. The primary analysis should be incident plaque in baseline plaque-free segments, using geometry from those segments. Progression in diseased segments is a separate secondary model, or enters via an interaction with baseline status, with baseline burden adjusted for.

### Cited Findings
- Glagov et al. (NEJM 1987; 136 left-main sections at autopsy): lumen area did not decrease as stenosis rose from 0% to 40%. It fell markedly above 40%. "Human coronary arteries enlarge in relation to plaque area and functionally important lumen stenosis may be delayed until the lesion occupies 40 percent of the internal elastic lamina area" — [search summary of NEJM 1987; JCI Insight reference entry](https://insight.jci.org/references/scholar/6633/B1)
- Because lumen is preserved by outward vessel enlargement, a diseased segment can have a normal-looking lumen but an enlarged vessel; local shear and geometry computed from the lumen therefore change as plaque forms — inference grounded in the Glagov finding above; PREDICTION reconstructed lumen, external elastic membrane and plaque from IVUS and found low-ESS areas developed "increased local plaque thickness and excessive expansive remodeling" over 6–10 months — [TCTMD report on PREDICTION](https://www.tctmd.com/news/endothelial-shear-stress-works-prediction-tool)
- Separate-model precedent: PARADIGM analysed patients with no baseline plaque separately (n = 402, new plaque in 35.6% over a median 3.6 years) from patients with existing non-obstructive lesions (n = 1,297, progression to obstruction) — [Won et al., Cardiovasc Diabetol 2022, PMC9655903](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9655903/); [Lee et al., Int J Cardiovasc Imaging 2020, PubMed 32779077](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/)

### Inferences
- Recommended hierarchy of analyses:
  1. Primary: incident plaque in segments that were plaque-free at baseline, all participants included. Geometry is measured at baseline in those segments. Model: cloglog GLMM/GEE with offset log(interval), participant risk factors, segment geometry, artery/segment-position indicator, and participant baseline SIS (segment involvement score) as an adjustment. Baseline SIS captures person-level susceptibility, and plaque in neighbouring segments may alter flow.
  2. Secondary: progression in segments with baseline plaque, as a separate model. Geometry here is confounded by remodeling, so adjust for baseline plaque burden in that segment, and if available use vessel (outer-wall) rather than lumen geometry.
  3. Alternative to 1+2: one model with a baseline-status × geometry interaction. It is more efficient for shared terms (risk factors), but the interaction spends df and still needs remodeling-robust geometry for the diseased stratum.
  4. Sensitivity: restrict to participants with zero plaque at baseline (CAC 0 and no non-calcified plaque). This removes all reverse causation and person-level confounding by existing disease, at the cost of events.
- Geometry choices that reduce reverse causation: (a) centreline curvature and bifurcation angle are less affected by wall thickening than radius-based measures; curvature of the lumen centreline shifts only where plaque is eccentric; (b) daughter radius ratio and lumen-volume/myocardial-mass are directly affected by plaque, so for these use only plaque-free segments or outer-vessel contours; (c) reference-diameter approaches (interpolating healthy diameter across a lesion) are a third option.
- The participant-level outcome ("new or progressing plaque anywhere") mixes both processes. If the participant-level model includes geometry summarised over diseased segments, it inherits reverse causation. Build the participant-level geometry summary from plaque-free segments, or vessel-wall geometry, only.
- Baseline plaque burden is a mediator/collider risk if geometry causes plaque: geometry → baseline plaque → future plaque. Adjusting for baseline SIS answers "does geometry add beyond current disease", which is the clinically relevant incremental question. State it explicitly.

### Gaps
- No retrieved CCTA study quantifies how much lumen-centreline curvature or bifurcation angle changes with plaque development (i.e., size of the reverse-causation bias for each geometry measure).
- Whether CGPS/Herlev–Østerbro reads include outer-vessel contours per segment is unknown.

## 4. Precedents with segment- or lesion-level outcomes and how they modelled them

### Takeaway
The precedents either analyse many small vessel subsegments (PREDICTION, 1.5 mm IVUS segments), or lesions (PARADIGM), or segment-level cross-sectional geometry (Tuncay 2018). Clustering is handled by mixed models or GEE. None does what this thesis proposes: geometry measured in a general-population cohort, plaque-free at baseline, predicting incident plaque at follow-up and rolled up to person-level risk. That is the gap.

### Cited Findings
- PREDICTION (Stone et al., Circulation 2012): 506 ACS patients after PCI, 374 (74%) with repeat IVUS profiling at 6–10 months. Each artery was divided into 1.5 mm segments, with baseline ESS computed per segment from 3D lumen/EEM/plaque reconstruction. Low ESS gave "substantial additive independent prognostication" for plaque progression and lumen obstruction, and was linked to increased local plaque thickness, excessive expansive remodeling and need for PCI by 12 months — [TCTMD](https://www.tctmd.com/news/endothelial-shear-stress-works-prediction-tool); [Dash Harvard record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120379-0331-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b). The specific clustering method (GEE vs mixed) was not confirmed in retrieved text.
- PARADIGM statins (Lee et al., JACC Cardiovasc Imaging 2018): 1,255 patients (60 ± 9 y, 57% men), 1,079 lesions in statin-naive (n = 474) and 2,496 lesions in statin-taking patients (n = 781). This was a per-lesion analysis of annualised PAV progression: overall 1.76 ± 2.40 vs 2.04 ± 2.37 %/y (p = 0.002); new high-risk plaque features 0.9 vs 1.6 %/y; statins associated with a 35% reduction in HRP development — [Corewell/Beaumont record](https://scholarlyworks.corewellhealth.org/cardiology_articles/184)
- PARADIGM per-lesion vs per-patient (Lee et al., Int J Cardiovasc Imaging 2020): of 2,252 registry patients with serial CCTA ≥ 2 years apart, 1,297 had only non-obstructive lesions at baseline. Over 3.8 ± 1.6 years, 76 lesions (2.4%) in 60 patients became obstructive (> 50%). A model using per-lesion CCTA measures was superior to per-patient measures for predicting new obstructive lesions (p < 0.001) — [PubMed 32779077](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/); [Monzino record](https://www.cardiologicomonzino.it/en/scientific-papers/2020/08/21/per-lesion-versus-per-patient-analysis-of-coronary-artery-disease-in-p/2175/)
- PARADIGM risk-factor burden (n = 1,005 without known CAD, median inter-scan 3.3 years): annualised PAV progression (total, calcified, non-calcified) increased with ASCVD risk category — [PMC7382001 search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7382001)
- Tuncay et al. (J Cardiovasc Comput Tomogr 2018), 73 asymptomatic higher-risk individuals, centreline-based curvature and tortuosity, linear mixed models. Curvature was 16.7% higher in segments with > 70% stenosis (p < 0.001) and 13.8% higher in such arteries (p = 0.002). Tortuosity was associated with stenosis only at segment level (p = 0.002). Both were related to plaque presence at segment level (p < 0.001). This is cross-sectional, so reverse causation cannot be excluded — [UMC Utrecht record](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/non-invasive-assessment-of-coronary-artery-geometry-using-coronar/)
- CGPS CCTA cohort (Fuchs et al., Ann Intern Med 2023): 9,533 asymptomatic participants ≥ 40 y without known IHD. 46% had subclinical atherosclerosis (36% non-obstructive, 10% obstructive). Disease was classified by obstruction and extent (extensive = one third or more of the coronary tree, i.e. a segment-involvement concept). Over median 3.5 y, 71 MIs and 193 deaths occurred — [AuntMinnie Europe](https://www.auntminnieeurope.com/clinical-news/article/15657736/subclinical-atherosclerosis-increases-heart-attack-risk-8-fold); [ACC journal scan](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2023/04/03/16/15/subclinical-coronary-atherosclerosis)
- SCAPIS has produced a manually annotated CCTA dataset (469 participants with detailed segmentations) relating plaque characteristics to risk factors — [search-result snippet, PMC10199173](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10199173) (not fetched)

### Inferences
- PARADIGM's design shows two points relevant to Herlev–Østerbro. Splitting patients by baseline plaque status is accepted practice. Per-lesion information outperformed per-patient summaries, which supports the "segment outcomes sharpen participant risk" idea.
- CGPS already reports extent in a segment-involvement form (extensive vs non-extensive), so baseline SIS is likely available as an adjustment variable.

### Gaps
- SCAPIS serial or segment-level follow-up analyses: no repeat-CCTA SCAPIS result was found (SCAPIS is largely cross-sectional to date as far as this search shows).
- CONFIRM SIS/SSS methodology and ICONIC (case-control, lesion-level precursors of ACS) were not retrieved; cite from originals (Min et al., JACC 2007 for SIS/SSS; Motoyama/Chang ICONIC, JACC 2018) after checking.
- Han et al. 2020 (PARADIGM) not specifically retrieved.
- No longitudinal study found linking baseline CCTA bifurcation angle, curvature or daughter ratio to *incident* segment plaque in a general population. This supports the thesis gap claim, but a dedicated search is advised before stating it.

## 5. Expected event counts among ~2,000 asymptomatic participants

### Takeaway
Expect roughly 30–45% of participants with any new or progressing plaque over a 3–5 year interval. That is about 600–900 participants with events among 2,000, with participants already plaque-positive at baseline contributing disproportionately. Restricted to baseline plaque-free participants (about half), roughly 30–35% develop new plaque over ~3.5 years, about 300 events among ~1,000. Per segment, incidence will be low (a few percent), so segment-level events number in the hundreds to low thousands.

### Cited Findings
- PARADIGM, 402 patients with no baseline plaque (mean age 57.6, 49% men; clinically referred, not general population): newly detected plaque in 35.6% over a median 3.6 years (IQR 2.7–5.0) — [Won et al., Cardiovasc Diabetol 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9655903/)
- PARADIGM non-obstructive lesions: 2.4% of lesions (60 of 1,297 patients, ≈ 4.6% of patients) became obstructive over 3.8 years. Hard progression endpoints are rare — [PubMed 32779077](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/)
- PARADIGM lesion-level PAV progression ≈ 1.8–2.0 %/year; new high-risk features 0.9–1.6 % of lesions per year — [Lee 2018 record](https://scholarlyworks.corewellhealth.org/cardiology_articles/184)
- MESA (Kronmal et al., Circulation 2007; asymptomatic multi-ethnic cohort): incident CAC among those with CAC 0 at baseline reported as 6.1%/year in women and 14.8% in men (as summarised in search results; verify units, since some summaries give it as a cumulative proportion over the interval). Mean progression among those with CAC ≈ 24 Agatston units/year — [Kronmal et al. via search summary; CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/222226/cdc_222226_DS1.pdf). Treat the 14.8% figure with caution until checked against the paper.
- CGPS baseline: 46% of 9,533 asymptomatic participants had plaque; 54% had none — [ACC journal scan](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2023/04/03/16/15/subclinical-coronary-atherosclerosis)

### Inferences
- Back-of-envelope for 2,000 Herlev–Østerbro participants, assuming the CGPS baseline split holds (~54% plaque-free, ~46% plaque-positive) and a 3–5 year interval:
  - Plaque-free at baseline (~1,080): new plaque ~25–35% (PARADIGM 35.6% is a clinically referred, higher-risk sample, so general population is probably lower) → ~270–380 participants with events.
  - Plaque-positive at baseline (~920): new plaque elsewhere or progression is far more common, plausibly 50–70% depending on the progression definition → ~460–640.
  - Total ≈ 700–1,000 participants with an event (35–50%). The participant-level outcome is not rare, which favours a person-level model with ~15–20 df under Riley criteria.
- Segment level: with ~17 segments × 1,080 plaque-free persons ≈ 18,000 at-risk segments, and typically 1–2 new plaque segments per incident person, there are ~300–700 incident segment events. That is enough for a geometry block of ~5–10 df even after a design effect.
- The definition of "progression" (visual grade change vs quantitative PAV threshold such as ≥ 1%/year in PARADIGM) moves the event count heavily. Specify it before analysis, and expect reader-variability noise at segment level.
- Scan interval varies, so cumulative proportions above are only rough. The cloglog offset model converts them to rates. Report events per 1,000 segment-years.

### Gaps
- No published general-population (non-referred) serial CCTA incidence of new plaque was retrieved. CGPS or Herlev–Østerbro follow-up rates, and any SCAPIS repeat imaging, were not found.
- MESA incident CAC rate units (per year vs cumulative) were not verified against the primary paper.
- Segment-level incidence rates (events per segment-year) in serial CCTA were not found in retrieved sources.
