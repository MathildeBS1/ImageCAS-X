# Coronary modelling practice for segment/lesion outcomes nested in patients, and guidance for clustered prediction models

*Evidence grades used below. FULL TEXT: the methods section was read (through a fetch tool that summarises and returns short quotes, so check wording against the paper before quoting it in the thesis). ABSTRACT: only the abstract or a repository record was seen. SNIPPET: only a search-engine excerpt was seen. Publisher sites (JACC, Circulation, NEJM, Lancet, BMJ, OUP) and most PMC pages blocked retrieval, so several of the named landmark studies could not be read; they are listed under Gaps rather than guessed. Seven web searches were used.*

## 1. What models do serial-imaging and per-lesion/per-segment coronary studies use to handle within-patient clustering?

### Takeaway
In the papers whose methods could be read, the dominant approach for many sub-units per patient is a mixed model with a random intercept for patient or vessel, almost always linear (continuous change in plaque burden); lesion-level time-to-event studies use marginal Cox models with a robust sandwich variance, with shared frailty at most as a sensitivity analysis. None of them turned sub-unit predictions into a patient-level risk; patient-level papers from the same cohorts simply aggregate plaque per patient and fit ordinary Cox or logistic models.

### Inferences
- A random intercept for the patient (or vessel) is the conventional device in the intravascular shear-stress literature, so the thesis's random person intercept is in line with practice. What is unusual is the combination: a binary incident-plaque outcome, a non-identity link and a prediction aim. The read studies are association studies of continuous change with tens of patients and thousands of sub-units; the thesis has the opposite shape (many persons, nine segments each).
- Two traditions are visible: mixed models where the sub-unit is a slice/sector/segment with a continuous outcome, and marginal Cox with robust variance where the sub-unit is a lesion with a clinical event. GEE appears for artery-level and geometry comparisons (Bax 2022 abstract, ESCR poster). The thesis sits closer to the first tradition in structure but has the binary-event character of the second.
- Several studies cluster on vessel rather than patient and leave the patient level unmodelled (Hartman 2021 has 41 vessels in 40 patients), and some thin the data to reduce dependence (random 25% of sectors; averaging to 3 mm regions). The thesis's fixed anatomical segments with a person intercept avoid both shortcuts.
- Studies that wanted a patient-level statement either analysed patients directly with aggregated plaque (SCOT-HEART, PARADIGM Yoon) or reduced to one lesion per patient (Costopoulos 2020). Deriving the person risk from the segment model is therefore not standard practice in what was read and will need its own justification.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved (publisher blocks), so the stated model is unknown from this research: PROSPECT (Stone 2011, NEJM), the published PROSPECT II paper (Erlinge 2021, Lancet), EMERALD (Lee 2019, JACC CVI), PREDICTION (Stone 2012, Circulation) and Stone 2018 (JACC CVI), the PARADIGM lesion papers in JACC CVI (Lee 2018 statins; Lee 2020 high-risk plaque and progression to obstruction), Samady 2011 full methods, Ferraro 2020 and Conte 2021 (ICONIC). No SCOT-HEART per-lesion analysis was found; the two SCOT-HEART papers seen are per-patient.
- ICONIC and PROSPECT II entries rest on search excerpts. The PROSPECT II wording is from the protocol and SAP, not from the published methods; the SAP PDF was downloaded but its text could not be extracted.
- Geometry-versus-plaque studies are thinly covered: only Tuncay 2018 and a 2014 poster, both at snippet level. No study with bifurcation angle, taper or daughter-diameter ratio as a predictor of plaque in a clustered per-segment model was found; the Rampidis 2022 review of coronary geometry and plaque (Diagnostics, PMC9497479) could not be opened and is the obvious next source.
- Link functions are essentially never stated in the binary GLMMs seen (Hartman 2021, Eshtehardi 2012).

## 2. Is the cloglog link or a length offset used anywhere in this literature? Has any study pooled segment risks to a patient-level risk?

### Takeaway
No study whose methods were read uses a complementary log-log link, an offset for segment or lesion length, or a pooling of sub-unit risks into a patient-level risk. This is absence in a limited sample (about eight full texts plus abstracts), not proof of absence in the field.

### Cited Findings
- Binary sub-unit outcomes, where modelled, were fitted with a generalised linear mixed model whose link is not stated: plaque growth defined as a 15% increase in plaque burden, vessel as random factor — [Hartman 2021, PMC8586146](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8586146/fullTextXML); SAS GLIMMIX for a binary WSS category — [Eshtehardi 2012, PMC3487351](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3487351/fullTextXML)
- No offset is mentioned in any of the read methods sections; exposure size is instead standardised by design, with fixed-size units (0.5 mm frames, 1.5 mm x 45 degree sectors, 3 mm regions) — [Costopoulos 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6503452/fullTextXML); [Hartman 2023](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10153640/fullTextXML); [Kok 2019](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/the-influence-of-multidirectional-shear-stress-on-plaque-progression-and-composition-changes-in-human-coronary-arteries)
- The closest thing to a patient-versus-sub-unit comparison is the PARADIGM analysis that compared a model with per-lesion CCTA measures (C 0.895) against one with per-patient measures (C 0.825) for progression to obstructive lesions in 3,218 lesions of 1,297 patients; the abstract does not say whether lesion predictions were aggregated to patients or how clustering entered the C-statistic comparison — [Leiden repository record of Lee 2020](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/3232666)
- Patient-level risk in the named cohorts is obtained by summing plaque per patient before modelling, not by pooling modelled sub-unit risks — [Williams 2020, SCOT-HEART](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7195857/fullTextXML); [Yoon 2021, PARADIGM](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8385056/fullTextXML)
- Time-to-event lesion studies handle the time dimension through Cox models rather than a grouped-time binary link — [PROSPECT II protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/65/NCT02171065/Prot_000.pdf); [Chang 2018, ICONIC](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2018.02.079) (both snippet level)

### Inferences
- On this evidence the cloglog link, the log-length offset and the pooled person risk are all departures from coronary imaging practice. They are defensible as the binary-data analogue of what the lesion literature does with Cox models: cloglog is the grouped-time proportional hazards link and the random intercept corresponds to the shared frailty that PROSPECT II planned as a robustness analysis. The thesis notes already carry that argument (Prentice 1978 for the link, Duchateau 2008 for frailty); an examiner should be told explicitly that the model is a discrete-time counterpart of a shared-frailty Cox model rather than a novel construction.
- Because the intravascular studies neutralise exposure by cutting equal-sized units, while the thesis uses anatomical segments of unequal length, the length offset answers a problem those studies do not have. That is a reason to justify it, and to show the alternative with a free coefficient on log length, as the thesis notes already plan.
- The Lee 2020 comparison shows that cardiology readers accept the question "does sub-unit information beat patient-level information", but gives no precedent for the pooling formula itself.

### Gaps
- No targeted search for "complementary log-log" or "offset" in coronary imaging was run (search budget), so the negative finding is limited to the papers listed above.
- Whether Lee 2020 aggregated lesion predictions to patients could not be determined; the full text (doi 10.1007/s10554-020-01960-z) should be checked because it is the nearest precedent.
- Statistical literature on aggregating sub-unit risks under a random effect was outside this brief and was not searched.

## 3. What do TRIPOD-Cluster and related guidance require for development and validation of prediction models with clustered data?

### Takeaway
TRIPOD-Cluster is a 19-item reporting checklist written for clustering of individuals within studies, centres or practices; it states that repeated measurements clustered within individuals are beyond its scope, so it applies to the thesis by analogy only. Its relevant expectations are: say how clustering was handled, say which intercept was used to compute predictions (cluster-specific, pooled, or integrated over the random-intercept distribution), repeat all model-building steps inside any resampling, and report performance with uncertainty.

### Cited Findings
All from the TRIPOD-Cluster explanation and elaboration (Debray, Collins, Riley, Snell, Van Calster, Reitsma, Moons; BMJ 2023;380:e071058), of which roughly the first 200,000 of 355,000 characters were read — [PMC9903176](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9903176/)
- Scope: clusters are studies in an individual participant data meta-analysis, centres in a multicentre study, or practices/hospitals in health-record data. Data with repeated measurements are described as "clustered within individuals" and their analysis "is beyond the scope of TRIPOD-Cluster".
- Ignoring clustering: without recognition of clustering, estimated probabilities may be "too close to the overall outcome frequency"; ignoring heterogeneity in baseline risk can give "predicted risks that are systematically too low or too high".
- Handling in development (item 8c): report "if and how heterogeneity in the model parameters was identified and dealt with"; options tabulated are common, random or fixed (stratified) intercepts and coefficients, including "random effects that allow for heterogeneity in the intercept and predictor effects". Analysis techniques "typically adopt hierarchical models (eg, random effects) to account for clustering". GEE is not discussed in the part read.
- Which prediction (item 8d): with random intercepts, one can "use a study specific intercept term", "use the pooled intercept term", or "integrate over the distribution of random intercept terms", and "These approaches typically lead to different calibration performance." Item 12b asks for the intercept or baseline estimate and all parameters needed to apply the model to new individuals.
- Abstract (item 2): state "How clustering was dealt with in the analysis".
- Performance (items 8e, 8f, 13a, 13b): calibration (plot, intercept, slope) and discrimination; performance reported "with uncertainty intervals ... overall and for each cluster"; heterogeneity summarised by random-effects meta-analysis, between-cluster variance, I-squared or a prediction interval for performance "in new settings". The c-statistic depends on case mix, so it can differ between clusters for an unchanged model.
- Internal validation (item 8b, Box 1): "all aspects of model building should be incorporated when performing internal validation using resampling methods", otherwise results are overoptimistic; resampling "can be applied to the entire IPD set or to each cluster"; internal-external cross-validation develops the model on all clusters but one and validates on the excluded cluster.
- Sample size (item 5): clustering "does not affect the sample size needed much" for development; at least 100 events and 100 non-events (preferably more than 200 events) for validation; "formal guidance about the minimal number of clusters or minimum sample size per cluster is currently lacking".
- The checklist and the explanation paper are separate BMJ articles (e071018 and e071058) — [search record of both papers](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9903176/)

### Inferences
- TRIPOD-Cluster cannot be cited as a requirement that the thesis meets or fails, because within-person clustering is explicitly outside it. It can be cited for three transferable principles: declare the clustering method, declare the type of prediction, and keep every estimation step inside the resampling loop.
- The item 8d options map directly onto the thesis's planned person risk: setting the random intercept to zero is the "pooled intercept" option, and integrating over the random intercept is the third option. The thesis notes already cite Pavlou 2015 for the miscalibration of the zero-intercept prediction; TRIPOD-Cluster supports stating the choice, not the choice itself.
- Per-cluster performance and its heterogeneity (items 8f, 13) have no meaningful analogue when the cluster is a person with nine segments and the evaluated quantity is one risk per person. The analogue of "resample clusters" is resampling or folding by person, which the thesis plans. The planned within-participant C is the nearest equivalent of within-cluster discrimination.
- The guidance says nothing in the part read about likelihood-ratio tests of predictor blocks; that part of the plan needs support from elsewhere.

### Gaps
- TRIPOD+AI (Collins 2024, BMJ) returned HTTP 403; PROBAST and the Riley and Steyerberg textbooks were not accessed. Nothing in these notes may be attributed to them.
- The last third of the TRIPOD-Cluster explanation paper (items after about 13, discussion) was not read, and the quotes above were relayed by a summarising fetch tool.
- Bouwmeester 2013 (BMC Med Res Methodol, random-intercept versus standard logistic prediction) and the simulation literature on marginal versus conditional prediction and within-cluster C could not be opened; Pavlou 2015 is known only through the thesis's own notes.
- No guidance was found on whether resampling must be by cluster when clusters are persons; the statement in TRIPOD-Cluster concerns studies or centres.

## 4. Are there published criticisms of per-segment or per-lesion analyses in cardiology that ignore clustering, or recommendations on GEE versus mixed models in cardiovascular imaging?

### Takeaway
Yes: a EuroIntervention methods paper (Lingsma et al. 2013) found that only 5 of 15 intracoronary imaging studies with multiple observations per patient clearly accounted for clustering and recommends always using multilevel models. No source comparing GEE with mixed models for cardiovascular imaging was found.

### Cited Findings
All from Lingsma H, Nauta S, van Leeuwen N, Borsboom G, Bruining N, Steyerberg E. Tools & Techniques: Analysis of clustered data in interventional cardiology: current practice and methodological advice. EuroIntervention 2013;9(1), doi 10.4244/EIJV9I1A23 (FULL TEXT) — [EuroIntervention](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/tools-amp-techniques-analysis-of-clustered-data-in-interventional-cardiology-current-practice-and-methodological-advice)
- Review of 2012 OCT studies in six cardiology journals: 17 included, 15 (88%) drew inferences from multiple observations per patient; of those, 5 (33%) clearly accounted for clustering, 3 (20%) were unclear and 7 (46%) clearly did not, mostly using t-tests.
- "Ignoring this correlation in the analysis leads to a too strong statistical inference" and "Ignoring clustering using naive models will exaggerate statistical significance."
- Recommendation: "We therefore recommend to always use multilevel models when analysing multiple observations per patient." Averaging to patient level is valid for patient-level questions but inefficient. The level of analysis should follow the research question.
- A random patient intercept gives within-patient (conditional) differences, whereas a naive model mixes within- and between-patient variation.
- Worked example (16 patients, 484 paired cross-sections, late recoil of a bioresorbable scaffold): the mean was the same across methods but the 95% interval widened from -0.80 to -0.49 mm2 (naive) to -1.27 to -0.05 mm2 (multilevel), close to patient-level averaging (-1.32 to -0.01 mm2). A plaque-type difference of -0.29 (-0.66 to 0.09) in the naive model shrank to -0.06 (-0.38 to 0.26) in the multilevel model.
- The paper does not discuss GEE, binary outcomes, intraclass correlation or reporting standards.
- Practice since then still varies: among the later studies read for question 1, all sub-unit analyses modelled clustering, but several clustered on vessel only or subsampled sectors — [Hartman 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8586146/fullTextXML); [Kok 2019](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/the-influence-of-multidirectional-shear-stress-on-plaque-progression-and-composition-changes-in-human-coronary-arteries)

### Inferences
- The thesis can cite Lingsma 2013 both for the criticism and for the recommendation of a random patient intercept; the co-authorship of Steyerberg links it to the prediction-modelling literature the thesis already uses. It is a 2013 paper about continuous outcomes, so it supports the random intercept, not the link, offset or pooling.
- Relative to documented practice (a third of studies handling clustering in 2012; linear mixed models with vessel-level intercepts later), a likelihood-based binary GLMM with a person intercept, person-level cross-validation and an explicit marginal person risk is at or above the norm on clustering. It is unconventional on link, offset and aggregation.
- What an examiner would likely want justified, given the above: (a) GLMM rather than GEE, since GEE and marginal Cox are what the CCTA cohort papers use and a population-averaged effect is the more familiar estimand; (b) cloglog rather than logit, with a sensitivity fit; (c) the offset with coefficient fixed at 1 versus free; (d) conditional versus marginal interpretation of hazard ratios and which prediction is evaluated; (e) the pooling step and its independence-given-the-intercept assumption; (f) that resampling is by person and includes standardisation and variance estimation; (g) quadrature settings, as binary clusters of nine make approximation quality relevant.

### Gaps
- No tutorial or commentary on GEE versus mixed models aimed at cardiovascular imaging researchers was found, and none on choosing between marginal and conditional estimands for per-lesion analyses.
- No more recent audit than Lingsma 2013 of how often per-segment or per-lesion cardiology papers ignore clustering was found; the 2012 figures may not describe current practice.
- No published criticism specific to CCTA per-segment analyses (as opposed to intracoronary imaging) was found.
