## 1. Cloglog versus logit link, and the offset on log segment length

### Takeaway
At segment-level risks this low the data cannot tell cloglog from logit, so the link is chosen for interpretation (hazard ratios, closure under "any segment") and not for fit; the choice only starts to matter where risk is high, which here is the aggregated person-level risk, exactly the quantity that is evaluated. A fixed offset on log length is an untested proportionality assumption for which no coronary precedent was found; a free coefficient costs one parameter and removes it.

### Cited Findings
- Both logit and cloglog are valid links for binomial-likelihood grouped/discrete-time survival models; the two converge as the interval (or the interval hazard) shrinks, because for small hazards log-odds ≈ log-hazard. **[search snippet of Rodríguez's GLM notes; page not opened]** — [Rodríguez, GLM notes §7.6](https://grodri.github.io/glms/notes/c7s6)
- Jenkins (Statalist) states that the grouped-data logit model gives very similar estimates to the cloglog model in practice. **[search snippet; forum post, not peer reviewed]** — [Statalist 2008](https://www.stata.com/statalist/archive/2008-01/msg00755.html)
- Stata's manual describes cloglog as asymmetric and as the usual choice "when the positive (or negative) outcome is rare"; it gives no evidence of better fit or prediction. **[background report]** — [Stata mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)
- The hazard-ratio reading of cloglog coefficients rests on proportional hazards within the window (Prentice & Gloeckler 1978). **[background report; DOI 10.2307/2529588 was only seen on an aggregator]** — [SAS PROC LOGISTIC documentation](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/63962/HTML/default/statug_logistic_sect072.htm)
- No coronary study using segment length as an offset/exposure was found in the earlier background research (about 19 searches), nor in this round. **[background report]** — [Han et al. 2022, ICONIC, used distance from ostium as a predictor instead](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8792800)
- In the latent-variable form the cloglog level-1 variance is π²/6 against π²/3 for logit, so σ² and ICC values are not comparable between links. **[background report]** — [Stata mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Severity of link misspecification for the coefficients and the LR test: low.** With per-segment risks of a few percent, 1 − exp(−e^η) ≈ e^η ≈ logit⁻¹(η); the geometry coefficients, their ranking and the LR statistic will be nearly identical under either link. A reviewer cannot be answered with "cloglog fits better"; the honest justification is interpretive (HR invariant to window length under PH; closure of "any segment" into exp(−e^b S_i)).
- **Severity for the person-level risk: moderate and not checkable from the segment fit.** The person risk 1 − E[exp(−e^b S_i)] is not small (it is P(any of 9 segments)); here the cloglog and logit conditional forms diverge, and the result also depends on the whole frailty distribution (section 2). Segment-level fit statistics cannot detect this; only person-level calibration can.
- **Proportional hazards over the window is an assumption, not a result.** With one follow-up scan per person (current-status data) PH cannot be tested within the data; a free coefficient on log follow-up time imposes a Weibull-type baseline. If follow-up times vary little, the window-invariance argument for cloglog buys nothing in this cohort and the HR label is interpretive only.
- **Offset on log length.** Fixing the coefficient at 1 asserts hazard ∝ length (homogeneous Poisson process along the vessel). Plaque is known to concentrate near ostia and bifurcations, which predicts a coefficient below 1. If the offset is wrong: (a) with segment-position intercepts in the model most of the error is absorbed by those intercepts, because length varies mainly between positions; the damage is then confined to within-position length variation; (b) any geometry feature correlated with length within position (curvature and taper are computed per segment and are length-dependent by construction) inherits the residual length effect, biasing exactly the block under test. A free coefficient with a reported CI for γ = 1 is the defensible default; the offset version can be a sensitivity analysis. Cost: 1 df in the base model.
- Measurement issue specific to an offset: an offset enters with no error allowance. Segment length from an automatic centerline with landmark-defined boundaries is noisy; error in an offset is not attenuated as it would be in an estimated coefficient but is passed straight into the linear predictor.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed simulation comparing predictive performance (calibration, Brier) of cloglog versus logit GLMMs at low event rates was opened. Suresh, Severn & Ghosh 2022 (BMC Med Res Methodol 22:207, doi:10.1186/s12874-022-01679-6) is the likely reference but Springer blocked the fetch; **unverified**.
- Textbook statements (Agresti, *Categorical Data Analysis*; Singer & Willett; Allison) that link functions are hard to discriminate empirically without very large samples are recalled from background knowledge and were not opened; **unverified**.
- Whether hazard is proportional to coronary segment length has no empirical source in either research round.

## 2. Random intercept: distribution misspecification, sparse clusters, zero variance, quadrature, and the LR test

### Takeaway
The literature's reassurance ("slopes are robust to the random-effects shape") covers the geometry coefficients but not the two things this model leans on hardest: σ̂², which is badly estimated in small binary clusters with few events, and the person-level risk, which is a functional of the entire assumed frailty distribution. The LR test for the geometry block is the least fragile part at this sample size, provided residual within-person dependence is absent and the quadrature is accurate.

### Cited Findings
**Misspecified random-effects distribution**
- McCulloch & Neuhaus (Stat Sci 2011;26:388-402) examine the effect of a misspecified random-effects shape on prediction, covariate-effect inference, prediction of random effects and variance estimation, and conclude that maximum likelihood is fairly robust across many common situations. **[abstract only; the PDF downloaded but could not be rendered, so the paper's table of which quantities are robust was NOT read]** — [arXiv 1201.1980](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.1980)
- Secondary summaries of this debate: misspecification "often produces little bias when estimating slope coefficients but may yield biased intercepts and variance-components estimators"; Litière, Alonso & Molenberghs (Stat Med 2008;27:3125-44) report that bias in mean-structure parameters is small only while the random-effects variance is small, that variance-component estimates are "always severely biased", and that power of fixed-effect tests can be affected; Heagerty & Kurland (2001) found high relative bias only for highly skewed distributions with large heterogeneity; Agresti, Caffo & Ohman-Strickland (2004) reported fixed-effect MLEs can be very sensitive to the assumed random-effect model; Neuhaus, McCulloch & Boylan (2012/2013) found little effect on covariate effects and CI performance. **[all search snippets; none of these primaries were opened]** — [Litière et al. 2008, doi:10.1002/sim.3157](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.3157); [Neuhaus, McCulloch & Boylan, doi:10.1002/sim.5682](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/sim.5682)
- In a multicentre prediction simulation, random-intercept logistic regression was "robust against violations of the normality assumption" (uniform and extreme-value centre intercepts) for calibration. Design: 5 or 50 centres, 50 or 200 patients each, event rate 0.3. **[primary, opened]** — [Falconieri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7383814). Note the design is far from the thesis (large clusters, common outcome, logit, centre-specific predictions).

**Small clusters, few events, variance estimation**
- Moineddin, Matheson & Glazier 2007 (adaptive quadrature, SAS NLMIXED; 30/50/100 groups; group size 5/30/50; prevalence about 30%): with group size 5 and 30 groups, relative bias of the random-intercept SD/variance was +174% at ICC 0.04, +24% at ICC 0.17, +16% at ICC 0.38; fixed-effect bias 9-16%; variance estimates were positively biased in all group-size-5 conditions. Convergence was 80.4% at group size 5 (56% in the worst cell) versus 99%+ at 30-50; 77% at 10% prevalence. Group size 5 with 10% prevalence gave about 12% fixed-effect bias and 50% random-effect bias. Variance-component CIs under-covered (non-coverage 7-17%). They recommend at least 50 groups of at least 50, and for rare outcomes more than one expected event per group. **[primary, opened]** — [Moineddin et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1955447/)
- Austin 2010 (5-20 clusters, 5-50 subjects per cluster, random-intercept variance 1, outcome rate about 25%): "estimation of variance components tended to be poor when there were only five subjects per cluster", regardless of software; adaptive-quadrature methods beat PQL (which is biased downward); with at least 20 subjects per cluster, fixed-effect bias was below about 5% even with five clusters; type I error for a null fixed effect was acceptable for most procedures except at five subjects per cluster. **[primary, opened]** — [Austin 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2949382)
- Secondary summaries: multilevel models overestimate between-cluster variance when cluster size is below about 5; sparse clustered data cause little fixed-effect bias when there are a reasonable number of clusters and most clusters have several observations. **[search snippets; papers (Clarke 2008; McNeish 2014) not opened]** — [McNeish, doi:10.1007/s10648-014-9287-x](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-014-9287-x)
- Wynants et al. 2015 report that the amount of clustering (ICC 0, 5, 20%) "was not meaningfully associated with the models' predictive performance" in multilevel logistic prediction models. **[repository abstract only]** — [Wynants et al. 2015](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/91572)

**Variance at or near zero**
- A singular fit means the random-effects covariance is of "less than full rank" (variance at or near zero); the GLMM FAQ names small numbers of random-effect levels and over-complex random structures as the usual causes. **[primary, opened]** — [Bolker, GLMM FAQ](https://bbolker.github.io/mixedmodels-misc/glmmFAQ.html)
- The LR test of σ² = 0 is on the boundary; it is conservative, and for a single variance the naive p-value "is approximately twice as large as it should be" (Self & Liang 1987; Stram & Lee 1994; Pinheiro & Bates 2000, as cited by the FAQ). **[FAQ opened; the cited primaries not opened]** — [Bolker, GLMM FAQ](https://bbolker.github.io/mixedmodels-misc/glmmFAQ.html)
- On synthetic data the project found that in a log-σ parameterisation an optimiser started near the bound can stop at σ = 0 and report success, 28-454 log-likelihood units below the optimum. **[background report, own simulation]** — no external source.

**Laplace versus adaptive quadrature**
- GLMM FAQ ranking: PQL is "biased for large variance or small means"; Laplace is "More accurate than PQL"; Gauss-Hermite quadrature is "More accurate than Laplace" but limited to 2-3 random effects. The FAQ makes no separate claim for binary data with small clusters. **[primary, opened]** — [Bolker, GLMM FAQ](https://bbolker.github.io/mixedmodels-misc/glmmFAQ.html)
- Laplace "has been known to produce biased parameter estimates", mainly in the variance components; Laplace bias depends on the discreteness of the response, is worst for binary data and falls with cluster size (Joe 2008); the evidence is mixed (Capanu, Gönen & Begg found Laplace adequate for small binary clusters); non-adaptive GH needs many nodes (Lesaffre & Spiessens 2001). **[all background report]** — [Stata mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf); [Joe 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/csdana/v52y2008i12p5066-5074.html)

**LR test for fixed effects**
- GLMM FAQ: the LR test is reliable "Not for fixed effects in finite-size cases (see Pinheiro & Bates 2000)"; reliability depends on the number of groups and on total sample minus number of parameters. Wald Z tests are ranked worst (Hauck-Donner effect for binomial models with extreme coefficients); parametric bootstrap is ranked best. **[primary, opened]** — [Bolker, GLMM FAQ](https://bbolker.github.io/mixedmodels-misc/glmmFAQ.html)
- In binary cluster-randomised-trial simulations with few clusters, LR tests were reported as unreliable at small to moderate sample sizes, and Wald tests with between-within or Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom were preferred; another paper reports LR tests with between-within denominator df keep type I error near nominal when ICC ≥ 0.01 with few covariates. **[search snippets; attribution to Li & Redden 2015 and to arXiv 2209.04364 is probable but not confirmed, primaries blocked/not opened]** — [Li & Redden 2015, doi:10.1186/s12874-015-0026-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-015-0026-x); [arXiv 2209.04364](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.04364v2)
- A spatial-GLMM paper reports LR tests tend to be anticonservative in small samples and corrects them with a parametric bootstrap needing as few as 100 replicates. **[search snippet]** — [BMC Med Res Methodol, s12874-021-01236-7](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12874-021-01236-7.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Where the thesis sits relative to the simulations.** The published small-cluster results come from designs with 5-100 clusters. The thesis has the opposite geometry: many clusters (hundreds to about 2000 persons), small fixed cluster size (9), rare events, most clusters all-zero. Two consequences:
  - *Fixed effects and the LR test (severity: low to moderate).* With accurate adaptive quadrature, ML is consistent as the number of clusters grows at fixed cluster size, so the "few clusters" small-sample problems (Austin; CRT literature) largely do not apply. The finite-sample warning in Pinheiro & Bates concerns the ratio of groups to parameters; with ≥ several hundred persons and 5 df this ratio is comfortable. The binding quantity is the number of events relative to parameters, not the number of persons (section 4).
  - *σ² (severity: high for precision, moderate for bias).* Information about σ² comes almost only from persons with two or more positive segments, since an all-zero or single-event cluster says little about within-person correlation beyond the marginal rate. With few events per person, the profile likelihood for σ² will be flat and skewed, a boundary estimate σ̂ = 0 has non-trivial probability even when σ > 0, and Wald intervals for σ² will under-cover (Moineddin: 7-17% non-coverage even in better-conditioned designs). Moineddin's rule "more than one expected event per group" is violated by design. None of the opened simulations reaches the thesis's regime (cluster size 9, prevalence of a few percent, 500+ clusters), so the size of the bias here is not known from the literature and should be established by a simulation at the fitted base-model parameters.
- **Intercept-variance trade-off at low risk.** For rare events the marginal segment risk is ≈ exp(β₀ + x'β + σ²/2), so the marginal segment rates identify only β₀ + σ²/2; β₀ and σ² are separated only through the clustering of events within persons. Segment-level calibration will therefore look fine for a wide range of σ², while the person-level risk 1 − E[exp(−e^b S_i)] changes with σ² (larger σ² at fixed marginal segment risk concentrates events in fewer persons and lowers P(any)). Imprecision in σ̂² is a direct source of person-level calibration-in-the-large error and is invisible in the segment fit and in the C-statistic.
- **Normality matters more here than in the cited robustness literature (severity: moderate, unquantified).** McCulloch & Neuhaus-type robustness is about slopes. The thesis's reported quantity is a Laplace transform of the frailty distribution evaluated at S_i, i.e. a functional of its whole shape including the left tail (persons with near-zero susceptibility). With binary outcomes in clusters of 9 and few events, the data contain almost no information about the frailty shape, so normal, gamma or a two-point "susceptible / not susceptible" mixture can fit the segment data equally well and give different P(any) for high-S_i persons. Falconieri's robustness finding does not transfer: it concerns centre-specific predictions with large clusters. This is the strongest argument for reporting a gamma-frailty refit (closed-form person risk) as a sensitivity analysis and for letting person-level calibration, not the segment likelihood, arbitrate.
- **Zero-variance fit.** If σ̂ = 0 the model collapses to independent segments and the person risk to the naive product; given the flat likelihood this is a plausible outcome in some cross-validation folds even if the full-data estimate is positive, which would make fold-to-fold person risks jump discontinuously. Fold-wise σ̂ should be tabulated, and cold starts from several σ values used before a zero is accepted. A boundary σ̂ also means the LR test for geometry is effectively a single-level LR test on pseudo-replicated rows; it is then only valid if segments really are independent within person.
- **Laplace is not acceptable as the primary fit here (severity: high if used, nil if avoided).** Clusters of 9 binary outcomes with rare events are the textbook worst case for Laplace, and unlike sampling error this bias does not shrink as persons are added. Since `glmmTMB` is Laplace-only, the fit must come from `lme4::glmer(nAGQ > 1)`, `GLMMadaptive` or Stata `mecloglog`; the doubled-node refit already planned is the right check, and both models in the LR test must use the same node count.
- **LR test validity is threatened less by small-sample χ² error than by misspecification.** The χ²₅ reference assumes the likelihood is correct. Residual within-person dependence beyond one shared intercept (section 5) or an informative number of evaluable segments makes the model-based LR anticonservative regardless of n. A parametric bootstrap of the LR statistic calibrates the small-sample error only under the fitted model, so it does not protect against this; a person-level cluster bootstrap or a sandwich-type check does.

### Gaps
- McCulloch & Neuhaus 2011: only the abstract was read. Their specific statements on (a) intercept and variance-component bias, (b) binary outcomes with small clusters, and (c) random-effects distributions that depend on covariates or cluster size could not be verified.
- Litière et al. 2007/2008, Heagerty & Kurland 2001, Agresti et al. 2004, Neuhaus et al. 2013: seen only through search snippets. Magnitudes are unverified.
- No simulation was found for random-intercept binary GLMMs in the regime "many clusters (≥ 500), cluster size about 9, prevalence ≤ 5%, most clusters all-zero". The frequency of σ̂ = 0 and the bias of σ̂² in that regime are unknown from the literature.
- Clarke 2008 (J Epidemiol Community Health, "When can group level clustering be ignored?") was not found by the search and not opened.
- Li & Redden 2015 full text (type I error rates by df method) blocked by Springer; numbers unverified.
- No source was found on the sensitivity of new-cluster *joint* predictions (P(any of m outcomes)) to the assumed random-effects distribution; the claim above is an inference.
- Pinheiro & Bates 2000 (the origin of the "LRT for fixed effects is anticonservative" statement) was not opened; it is cited via the GLMM FAQ, and it concerns linear mixed models.

## 3. Prediction: conditional versus marginal, calibration, and whether a random-intercept model predicts better for new clusters

### Takeaway
For a new person (new cluster), the published evidence is that a random-intercept model gives no better discrimination and at best equal calibration compared with a standard model that ignores clustering; its documented advantage exists only when the cluster effect is known. In this thesis the random intercept is therefore not justified by better segment predictions but by two other things: honest standard errors for the geometry test, and a joint distribution that lets segment risks be combined into P(any segment), which a model ignoring clustering cannot do correctly.

### Cited Findings
- Bouwmeester et al. 2013, simulations (20 centres / 1000 patients default; ICC 5, 15, 30%): at ICC 5%, external C-index 0.78 for both the standard model and the random-intercept model with the random effect set to zero; external calibration slope 0.96 (standard) versus 0.92 (RE = 0). At ICC 15%: external C 0.77 versus 0.77; calibration slope 0.97 versus 0.83; calibration intercept −0.00 versus 0.20. Conditional predictions using the cluster effect had higher apparent C (0.82, 0.85) but "cannot be used in new data of patients treated by new anesthesiologists". Abstract: the random-intercept model discriminates "better than the standard model only if the cluster effect is used for predictions". **[primary, opened]** — [Bouwmeester et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3658967/)
- Pavlou et al. 2015 (20 training centres, 1000 patients, validation in remaining centres, ICC 0.05-0.30): for new clusters, calibration slopes of standard logistic regression ignoring clustering, GEE, and random-effects predictions integrated over the random-effect distribution all lay in 0.956-0.984; predictions with the random effect set to zero fell to 0.948, 0.922, 0.884 and 0.788 at ICC 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.30. Integrated predictions were "comparable to that of standard MLE (Ignoring clustering) and GEE"; the paper does not claim the random-effects model predicts better for new clusters. The uncertainty of σ̂²_u plugged into the integral was not examined, and overfitting was ruled out because "the number of events was large". Mis-calibration of the b = 0 shortcut is small at low ICC ("the ICC rarely exceeds 0.15"). **[primary, opened]** — [Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/)
- Falconieri et al. 2020 (5 or 50 centres × 50 or 200 patients, event rate 0.3, ICC 5% or 20%): with large samples conditional models gave calibrated centre-level predictions while marginal models did not; "Small sample sizes led to overfitting and unreliable predictions"; for new centres they recommend the random intercept set to zero (average-centre prediction), stating that "using the average center effects yields better predictions in new centers than using a marginal model". **[primary, opened]** — [Falconieri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7383814)
- Conflict to note: Falconieri recommend b = 0 for new centres; Pavlou show b = 0 is mis-calibrated against the *population-average* target. The two papers evaluate calibration against different targets (within-centre versus overall). **[both primaries opened; the reconciliation is my reading]** — [Falconieri 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7383814); [Pavlou 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/)
- Pavlou, Ambler & Omar 2021 (30 clusters of about 100, ICC 0.2): when cluster size is informative or covariates are confounded by cluster, marginal predictions from GLMM and GEE were *more* mis-calibrated than those from independence estimating equations; cluster-specific predictions are more accurate only "as long as the random effects are estimated reasonably well"; they suggest the number of clusters be more than 5 and "close to 50 when possible". The paper flags its own reported calibration-in-the-large values (0.20, 0.17) as containing uncorrected typos. **[primary, opened]** — [Pavlou, Ambler & Omar 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8254921/)
- Wynants et al. 2018: "models should reflect the data structure, while the level of model validation should reflect the research question". **[background report]** — [Wynants et al. 2018](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/106551)
- All of the above are logit models with large clusters (centres); none is cloglog and none has clusters of about 9. **[stated in the opened papers' designs]** — [Bouwmeester 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3658967/)

### Inferences
- **Segment-level prediction for a new person gains nothing from the random intercept.** Bouwmeester and Pavlou agree: same C, and calibration equal at best. If the thesis were evaluated at segment level, a plain cloglog GLM with cluster-robust standard errors would predict as well. Any claim that the GLMM "predicts better" should be removed; the defensible claims are (i) valid inference for β and (ii) a coherent joint model for aggregation.
- **The aggregation is where the random intercept earns its place, and also where it is most exposed.** A model without a person effect yields P(any) as the independence product, which overestimates when segments are positively dependent (background report, Harris inequality). The GLMM corrects this, but the size of the correction is governed entirely by σ̂² and the assumed normal shape, both weakly identified (section 2). So the person risk is exact under the model and fragile outside it. A direct person-level model of "any plaque" does not need σ² at all and is the natural benchmark; if the two disagree in person-level calibration, the GLMM assumptions are the suspect.
- **How large is the b = 0 error likely to be here?** Pavlou's logit numbers (slope 0.948 at ICC 0.05, 0.788 at ICC 0.30) are for individual-level predictions and do not translate to a cloglog P(any-of-9). The error grows with σ and with S_i; it cannot be read off an ICC. It must be computed, not assumed small.
- **Fold-wise σ̂² instability propagates to person-level calibration-in-the-large.** Pavlou did not study plug-in uncertainty in σ̂²; in the thesis's sparse regime it is probably the dominant source of person-level calibration error, larger than coefficient overfitting.
- **Informative cluster size is a live risk.** If the number of evaluable segments per person depends on disease or image quality (non-evaluable or absent segments), Pavlou 2021 shows marginal GLMM predictions degrade and can be worse than a model ignoring clustering. The fix they propose (cluster size as a covariate) is cheap to test.
- **Conditional (empirical-Bayes) predictions are not available** for a held-out person without using that person's outcomes, so the literature's one clear advantage of random-effects models (better calibration and discrimination within known clusters) is structurally unavailable to this thesis.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares, for new clusters, a random-intercept model integrated to a *joint* any-event probability against (a) the independence product from a single-level model and (b) a direct cluster-level model. The thesis's central use of the model is therefore unvalidated in the literature.
- C-statistics in Pavlou 2015 are in supplementary Table S3 and were not read.
- Wynants et al. 2018 and Steyerberg's textbook treatment of clustering were not re-opened.
- No evidence was found on calibration of integrated marginal predictions when σ² is estimated from small sparse clusters.

## 4. Sample size and overfitting with clustered binary outcomes

### Takeaway
The Riley criteria are derived for independent observations and say nothing about clustering; counting persons and person-level events is the conservative and defensible input, while counting segment rows would overstate the information. The one clustered-data simulation opened recommends at least 10 events per variable and found the degree of clustering did not change that, but it did not study clusters of 9 with rare events or the variance parameter that the person risk depends on.

### Cited Findings
- Riley et al. 2019 (Stat Med 38:1276-96), criteria: (i) global shrinkage ≥ 0.9 via n = p / [(S − 1) ln(1 − R²_CS / S)]; (ii) optimism in Nagelkerke R² ≤ 0.05; (iii) overall risk estimated to within ±0.05, n = 1.96² φ(1 − φ)/δ². The paper "does not state or discuss assumptions of independent observations, and it does not address clustered or multilevel data"; Wynants 2015 is cited only in the events-per-parameter debate. The maximum Cox-Snell R² is about 0.33 at 5% and 0.11 at 1% outcome proportion, so low anticipated R²_CS is expected. Jinks' C-to-D conversion used to anticipate R² "needs further evaluation", with error increasing as C rises. The paper's own examples give events per parameter of about 4.8 and 23, so no blanket EPP rule holds. A published correction exists (Stat Med 2019;38:5672) and was not read. **[primary, opened, first 100k characters of 119k]** — [Riley et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519266/)
- Wynants et al. 2015 (J Clin Epidemiol 68:1406-14; multilevel logistic models, ICC 0/5/20%, EPV 5/10/20/50): median calibration slope 0.72 at EPV 5 with ICC 0% and 0.71 with ICC 20%; 0.85 at EPV 10 and 0.96 at EPV 50 (ICC 20%). "The amount of clustering was not meaningfully associated with the models' predictive performance." They recommend "at least 10 EPV", up to 50 with variable selection. **[repository abstract only; number of clusters, cluster sizes and bias in the random-intercept variance not seen]** — [Wynants et al. 2015](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/91572)
- A later dissertation from the same group is summarised as finding severe overfitting at 5 EPV and that mixed models "did not require more demanding sample size guidelines than standard logistic regression". **[search snippet; source and author not identified]** — [researchportal.be record](https://www.researchportal.be/en/node/159735)
- Falconieri et al. 2020: with 5 centres × 50 patients (about 75 events for 8 predictors by the extractor's arithmetic, not a figure in the paper) all models were overfitted; fixed-effects centre models suffered more than random-intercept models when centres were many and small. **[primary, opened; EPV figure is derived, not quoted]** — [Falconieri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7383814)
- Moineddin et al. 2007 recommend, for rare outcomes, that the expected number of events per group exceed one. **[primary, opened]** — [Moineddin et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1955447/)

### Inferences
- **Which n and which events?** Three candidate counts exist: (a) persons and persons-with-any-plaque; (b) segment rows and positive segments; (c) an effective sample size between them. The person risk is the evaluated quantity and Riley's derivation needs independent units, so (a) is the only count the criteria actually license. Using (b) would overstate the budget, and by a lot for person-level covariates (age, sex, SCORE2 factors, LM angle, Finet's ratio, daughter asymmetry), for which nine rows per person are pseudo-replicates and the information is essentially one observation per person.
- **The geometry block is mixed.** Three of the five features (angle, asymmetry, Finet's ratio) are person-level constants carried on pLAD/pLCx rows; two (curvature, taper) vary by segment. The segment-varying ones draw on within-person allocation information (background report: about 94% of the information for a locally coded feature came from allocation), so their effective events are closer to the number of positive segments; the person-level ones are limited by the number of persons with events. A single EPP figure for the block therefore misstates precision in both directions. This should be said explicitly in the budget section.
- **Shrinkage S = 0.9 computed at person level does not describe the fitted model.** The Riley formula predicts the calibration slope of a person-level logistic model with p parameters. The GLMM is fitted to segment rows with extra parameters not in the person-level count (eight segment-position intercepts, log length, σ²). Its person-level calibration slope depends additionally on σ̂² and the frailty shape, neither of which the formula covers. The criteria remain a reasonable outcome-blind way to cap the number of *person-level and geometry* parameters, but the expected shrinkage of the pooled model must be estimated empirically (bootstrap or repeated CV of the whole procedure), not asserted from the formula.
- **Position intercepts with zero or near-zero events** are a concrete separation risk (the background report's own simulation produced β ≈ −15 with a "converged" flag); segment positions with very few events should be checked before the model is locked, since Riley notes sparse categories may need larger samples.
- **The 10/20 events-per-parameter rule currently in `who_where_readiness.py`** is supported only by Wynants 2015 (EPV ≥ 10) for clustered data; Riley's own examples show EPP anywhere from about 5 to 23 can be the true requirement. Switching to the Riley criteria with person-level counts, as the notes already plan, is the more defensible position; neither is validated for this data structure.
- **Anticipated C = 0.70.** Riley flag the C-to-R² conversion as the weak link, with larger error at higher C. Presenting the budget at 0.65, 0.70, 0.75, as planned, is the appropriate hedge. With a low person-level event fraction the implied R²_CS is small and criterion (i) will dominate.

### Gaps
- No published extension of the Riley criteria to clustered or multilevel development data was found in this round (the paper itself is silent). Whether one exists by now (for example in later Riley/Ensor/Pavlou work) was not established within the search budget; **unverified absence**.
- No source was found giving an effective-sample-size or design-effect formula for binary GLMM prediction models with rare events; the standard Kish design effect 1 + (m − 1)ρ is recalled from background knowledge, applies to means on the observed scale, and was not sourced here.
- Wynants 2015 full text not opened: cluster counts, cluster sizes and any results on the random-intercept variance are unknown, so its "clustering does not matter for EPV" conclusion cannot be assumed to hold for 9-unit clusters.
- van Smeden et al. (2016, 2019) on EPV and prediction error were not opened.
- No sample-size guidance was found for the precision of σ̂², which governs the aggregated person risk.

## 5. Independence of segments given the person (no spatial correlation, no segment-specific heterogeneity)

### Takeaway
A single shared intercept imposes exchangeable dependence among all segments of a person; coronary segments are nested in vessels and ordered along them, so extra same-vessel or adjacent-segment dependence is plausible, and no source was found that quantifies it. If present, it leaves point estimates roughly usable but makes the model-based LR test and standard errors too optimistic for vessel-level covariates and biases the aggregated person risk upward.

### Cited Findings
- Clustered data are common in interventional cardiology; ignoring within-patient correlation "leads to a too strong statistical inference" with "misleadingly small standard errors"; of 17 OCT studies reviewed, 7 of the 15 that made inferences clearly did not account for clustering. The worked example uses two levels only (patient and cross-section); vessel-level nesting is not modelled and no ICCs for coronary data are reported. **[primary, opened]** — [Lingsma et al. 2013, EuroIntervention](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/tools-amp-techniques-analysis-of-clustered-data-in-interventional-cardiology-current-practice-and-methodological-advice)
- An RSNA 2007 abstract on coronary CTA accuracy states that analyses at artery and segment level must account for clustering because arteries are nested within patients and segments within arteries, and that ignoring even modest within-patient correlation gives confidence limits that are too narrow. **[search snippet; conference abstract, not opened]** — [RSNA 2007 abstract](https://archive.rsna.org/2007/5000491.html)
- ICONIC handled within-patient dependence of lesions with a shared (patient-level) frailty only. **[background report]** — [Han et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8792800)
- With extra positive dependence beyond the shared intercept, a one-intercept model overestimates P(any) for the same marginal segment risks. **[background report, project derivation]** — no external source.
- Informative cluster size and confounding by cluster degrade marginal GLMM predictions. **[primary, opened]** — [Pavlou, Ambler & Omar 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8254921/)

### Inferences
- **Why extra dependence is plausible.** Plaque in a proximal segment and its distal neighbour share local haemodynamics, and a single lesion can straddle a segment boundary and be scored in both; segments of the same artery share vessel calibre and dominance-related flow. Both produce correlation between neighbours that exceeds correlation between, say, distal RCA and distal LCx. An exchangeable random intercept cannot represent that.
- **Consequences, by quantity (severity in brackets):**
  - *Geometry coefficients for segment-varying features (low to moderate).* Estimates remain approximately right if the mean model is right, but conditional-on-b interpretation blurs.
  - *LR test and SEs for covariates constant within a vessel or within the LM bifurcation (moderate to high).* The left-main angle, asymmetry and Finet's ratio are carried on the pLAD and pLCx rows only. Those two rows are adjacent at the bifurcation; if their outcomes are more correlated than the shared intercept allows, the two rows are counted as more independent evidence than they are and the LR statistic for the block is inflated. This is the specific place where the independence assumption can bias the primary test.
  - *σ̂² (moderate).* Local dependence is absorbed into the person variance, inflating σ̂² relative to the true between-person heterogeneity, which then feeds the person-risk integral.
  - *Person risk (moderate).* By the project's Harris-inequality argument, unmodelled extra positive dependence means P(any) is over-predicted at given marginal segment risks; partly offset by the inflated σ̂². Net direction is not determinable without simulation; person-level calibration is the empirical check.
- **No segment-specific heterogeneity.** A Bernoulli outcome has no free dispersion, so unmodelled segment-level heterogeneity cannot appear as overdispersion; it instead attenuates conditional coefficients toward zero (the usual omitted-heterogeneity scaling in nonlinear models) and is otherwise undetectable in the fit. It also means goodness-of-fit at segment level has little power to reveal this misspecification.
- **A cheap diagnostic exists that does not require a new model:** compare the observed within-person co-occurrence of plaque in adjacent versus non-adjacent segment pairs with that implied by the fitted model (simulated from it), and compare model-based SEs of the geometry block with a person-level cluster bootstrap. Agreement supports the assumption; a clear excess of adjacent pairs falsifies it. (A vessel-within-person intercept is the structural alternative but is outside this note's scope.)
- **Overall defensibility.** At this sample size the model is defensible for its primary inferential purpose (a prespecified 5-df LR test) if it is fitted by adaptive quadrature, if σ̂ is shown to be stable across starts and folds, and if the block test is corroborated by a method that does not rely on the working dependence structure. It is weakly defensible as a *prediction* model: no cited evidence shows a random-intercept model predicting better for new clusters, and its person-level risk rests on σ̂² and a normal frailty shape that sparse clusters of nine cannot pin down. The claims in the thesis should be sized accordingly.

### Gaps

- No simulation was found on the effect of unmodelled nested or serial dependence on LR tests or on aggregated any-event predictions in binary GLMMs.
- The claim that neglected segment-level heterogeneity attenuates conditional coefficients is a standard result recalled from background knowledge (Neuhaus & Jewell; Gail et al. 1984) and was not sourced in this round; **unverified**.
- The RSNA abstract is a conference abstract seen only as a search snippet.
