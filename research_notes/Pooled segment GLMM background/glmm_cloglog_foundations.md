## 1. GLM recap, why cloglog, coefficients as log hazard ratios, log exposure as offset or covariate

### Takeaway
The cloglog link, P(Y=1) = 1 − exp(−exp(η)), is what you get when an event occurs at least once in a Poisson / proportional-hazards process with cumulative hazard exp(η) over the exposure window. Its coefficients are therefore log hazard ratios (not log odds ratios), and log exposure (time, length) enters additively: as an offset if hazard is assumed proportional to exposure, or as a covariate whose free coefficient estimates the power-law scaling of risk with exposure.

### Cited Findings
- The cloglog GLMM is defined as Pr(y_ij = 1 | x_ij, u_j) = H(x_ij β + z_ij u_j) with inverse link H(v) = 1 − exp{−exp(v)}; random effects are multivariate normal with mean 0 (verified) — [Stata ME manual, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)
- Equivalent latent-variable form: y*_ij = x_ij β + z_ij u_j + ε_ij, y_ij = I(y*_ij > 0), with ε_ij i.i.d. extreme-value (Gumbel), mean equal to Euler's constant and variance π²/6; this "nonsymmetric error distribution is an alternative to the symmetric error distribution underlying logistic and probit analysis and is usually used when the positive (or negative) outcome is rare" (verified, quoted) — [Stata ME manual, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)
- Prentice & Gloeckler (1978), "Regression analysis of grouped survival data with application to breast cancer data", Biometrics 34:57–67, propose the grouped-data version of Cox's proportional hazards model for estimating the relative-risk parameter (verified from abstract listing; DOI 10.2307/2529588 given by the listing, not confirmed on JSTOR) — [Synapse listing](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1994670953)
- The Prentice–Gloeckler model gives a cloglog relationship between the interval hazard and covariates, log(−log(1 − h_j(x))) = γ_j + β'x, and is commonly fitted as a binomial GLM on person-period data (verified, secondary source) — [MetricGate, Prentice–Gloeckler discrete PH](https://metricgate.com/docs/prentice-gloeckler-discrete-ph/)
- A frailty model is equivalent to a mixed-effects model with random intercepts and a complementary-log-log conditional link (verified, secondary statement in a frailty paper) — [Marginalizable frailty model, arXiv 1403.6744](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.6744)

### Inferences (derivations; standard, not quoted from a fetched source)
- **GLM structure** (Agresti, *Foundations of Linear and Generalized Linear Models*, 2015, ch. 4–5; standard / not fetched): Y ~ Bernoulli(p), g(p) = η = x'β. Common links: logit g(p) = log{p/(1−p)}; probit Φ⁻¹(p); cloglog log{−log(1−p)}. Logit and probit are symmetric around p = 0.5 (g(p) = −g(1−p)); cloglog is not: it approaches 1 sharply and 0 slowly. For small p, 1 − exp(−e^η) ≈ e^η, so cloglog ≈ log link ≈ logit, and the three give similar fits when outcomes are rare; they diverge when p is large.
- **Derivation from a Poisson / hazard process.** Let the number of events in a unit (segment) over the window be N ~ Poisson(Λ), with cumulative hazard Λ = ∫₀ᵗ λ(s) ds. Only "at least one event" is observed: P(Y = 1) = P(N ≥ 1) = 1 − exp(−Λ). With proportional hazards λ(s | x) = λ₀(s) exp(x'β), Λ = Λ₀(t) exp(x'β), so log(−log(1 − p)) = log Λ₀(t) + x'β. The cloglog intercept absorbs log Λ₀(t); each β_k is a **log hazard ratio**, exp(β_k) a hazard ratio, invariant to the length of the window as long as PH holds. Under logit, by contrast, exp(β) is an odds ratio whose value depends on the baseline risk/window when the data are generated by a hazard process (non-collapsibility of the OR across window length).
- **Current-status interpretation.** With a single follow-up inspection at time t_i and only "event by t_i" observed, the data are current-status (case-I interval-censored) data; the cloglog with log t in the predictor is the PH model for such data with a Weibull-type baseline Λ₀(t) = c·t^κ (κ = coefficient on log t). If follow-up times are nearly constant across units, the coefficient on log t is weakly identified and mostly absorbed by the intercept.
- **Offset vs free coefficient on log exposure.** If hazard per unit exposure is homogeneous, Λ = λ·E (E = time or length), so cloglog(p) = log λ + log E: log E enters as an **offset** (coefficient fixed at 1). If log E enters as a covariate with free coefficient γ, then Λ ∝ E^γ: γ = 1 means risk scales proportionally with exposure (e.g., twice the segment length = twice the hazard, as if plaque arose independently per mm); γ < 1 means less-than-proportional scaling (e.g., risk concentrated at specific sites such as ostia/bifurcations, or detection not proportional to length); γ > 1 more-than-proportional. A free γ is also a hedge against the PH/homogeneity assumption being wrong, and against length being a proxy for other properties (anatomical position, vessel size). Testing H₀: γ = 1 is a test of the "independent per unit length" model. Caveat: if position effects are also in the model and length differs mainly between positions, γ is identified from within-position length variation only.
- **Cloglog and summation over sub-units.** If units j = 1..n share b_i and are conditionally independent, P(no event in any unit | b_i) = Π_j exp(−e^{η_ij + b_i}) = exp(−e^{b_i} Σ_j e^{η_ij}). The cluster-level outcome "any event" is again a cloglog model, with linear predictor log Σ_j e^{η_ij} + b_i. This closure under aggregation is a property of cloglog (of Poisson superposition) and does not hold for logit.

## 2. GLMM formulation: random intercept, conditional vs marginal effects, attenuation, latent-scale ICC

### Takeaway
In η_ij = x_ij'β + b_i the coefficients are **conditional (subject-specific)**: effects for a fixed person. Averaging over b_i gives population-averaged probabilities that, for both logit and cloglog with normal b_i, are no longer of the same link form and imply effects attenuated toward zero; for cloglog this is the familiar frailty result that marginal hazard ratios are diluted. On the latent scale the residual variance is π²/6 for cloglog (Gumbel) vs π²/3 for logit, giving ICC = σ²/(σ² + π²/6) for a cloglog random intercept, but this "ICC" is a latent-scale quantity, not the correlation of the binary outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- Random-intercept GLMM: z_ij = 1, random effects are realisations from N(0, Σ) "not directly estimated as model parameters but ... summarized according to the unique elements of Σ, known as variance components" (verified) — [Stata ME manual, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)
- Latent cloglog residual: Gumbel with variance σ²_ε = π²/6, independent of u_j (verified) — [Stata ME manual, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)
- Nakagawa, Johnson & Schielzeth (2017), "The coefficient of determination R² and intra-class correlation coefficient from generalized linear mixed-effects models revisited and expanded", J. R. Soc. Interface 14:20170213, doi:10.1098/rsif.2017.0213; derives the GLMM residual term on the latent scale and discusses special considerations for binary and proportion data (verified from abstract/metadata). The full text (which I expect lists distribution-specific variances π²/3 logit, 1 probit, π²/6 cloglog) could not be fetched (403/captcha) — [PubMed 28904005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28904005/); [PMC5636267](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5636267)
- Neuhaus, Kalbfleisch & Hauck (1991), "A comparison of cluster-specific and population-averaged approaches for analyzing correlated binary data", Int. Statist. Rev. 59:25–35 (verified citation from a reference list; no DOI retrieved; JSTOR is the route) — [reference list in arXiv 1104.3395](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.3395)
- Cluster-specific models include random effects for each cluster, whereas population-averaged models target how the average response across clusters changes with covariates (verified, secondary) — [UCT thesis, Techniques for handling clustered binary data](https://open.uct.ac.za/items/a1ed2948-02aa-4ad6-9083-255db5c38622/full)
- With normal random effects the marginal model requires numerical integration (e.g. Gaussian quadrature); "marginally interpretable" GLMMs are a separate line of work that chooses random-effect distributions/links so that the marginal model retains a closed form (verified, secondary) — [Marginally interpretable GLMMs, arXiv 1610.01526](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01526)
- Gamma frailties are popular "mainly for inference simplicity due to conjugacy between Gamma distribution and the complementary-log-log link"; positive stable frailties have also been used for shared frailty (verified, secondary) — [Marginalizable frailty model, arXiv 1403.6744](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.6744)
- Under a cloglog random intercept from Hougaard's power-variance-function family, the marginal likelihood is available in closed form (verified, secondary) — [search summary of arXiv 2406.04518 / related](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.04518)

### Inferences (derivations and standard results; flag approximations)
- **Conditional vs marginal.** Conditional: P(y_ij = 1 | b_i) = 1 − exp(−e^{x_ij'β + b_i}). Marginal: p̄_ij = 1 − E_b[exp(−e^{x_ij'β} e^{b})] = 1 − 𝓛(e^{x_ij'β}), where 𝓛(s) = E[exp(−s e^b)] is the Laplace transform of the lognormal "frailty" e^b. There is no closed form for lognormal; it is evaluated by Gauss–Hermite quadrature.
- **Attenuation (logit).** Standard approximation (Zeger, Liang & Albert 1988, Biometrics 44:1049–1060; standard / not fetched): β_M ≈ β_C / √(1 + c²σ²), c = 16√3/(15π) ≈ 0.588, i.e. roughly β_C / √(1 + 0.346σ²). This is an approximation (it uses the logistic ≈ scaled-probit trick), exact only for probit with normal b: β_M = β_C/√(1 + σ²). Flag: verify the constant in the original paper before citing.
- **Attenuation (cloglog).** No equally simple formula exists with normal b. Qualitatively (standard frailty result, e.g. Hougaard, *Analysis of Multivariate Survival Data*, 2000; not fetched): (a) the marginal model is not cloglog-linear in x; (b) the implied marginal hazard ratio is attenuated toward 1 and is **not constant**: it is close to the conditional HR when the marginal risk is low and shrinks toward 1 as risk accumulates (high-risk people "leave the risk set" first). For small e^{η} (rare outcome), 1 − 𝓛(s) ≈ s·E[e^b] = e^{η + σ²/2}, so at low risk the marginal model is approximately cloglog/log-linear with the **same slopes** and an intercept shifted by σ²/2: attenuation of cloglog effects is therefore small for rare outcomes and grows with risk. With a gamma frailty (mean 1, variance θ) the marginal is closed-form: p̄ = 1 − (1 + θe^{η})^{−1/θ}. With a positive-stable frailty of index α ∈ (0,1], E[exp(−s W)] = exp(−s^α), so the marginal is again exactly cloglog with all coefficients multiplied by α (Hougaard's result; this is the cloglog analogue of the "bridge" distribution for logit, Wang & Louis 2003, Biometrika 90:765–775; not fetched). Positive-stable frailty has infinite mean, which is why it is rarely used in practice.
- **Which effect does the question want?** Subject-specific β answers "how does risk change for a given person if x changed"; marginal effects answer "how does average risk differ between subpopulations". For person-level prediction of a new person (b unknown) the correct quantity is the marginal (integrated) probability, computed from the conditional model by integrating over b; plugging in b = 0 gives the risk of a person with median frailty, which is not the mean risk (Jensen's inequality). This mirrors the Neuhaus–Kalbfleisch–Hauck comparison.
- **Latent-scale ICC.** From the latent form, Var(y*_ij | x) = σ² + π²/6 (cloglog) or σ² + π²/3 ≈ σ² + 3.29 (logit), so ICC_latent = σ²/(σ² + π²/6) for cloglog and σ²/(σ² + π²/3) for logit. Caveats (be explicit in the thesis): (i) the same numeric σ² does not mean the same clustering across links, because β and σ are on link-specific scales; (ii) the latent ICC is not the correlation between two binary outcomes in the same cluster, which depends on the fixed-effect predictor and is typically much smaller; (iii) the Gumbel residual is skewed, so the "latent normal + residual" decomposition is less natural than for probit; (iv) Nakagawa et al. (2017) discuss that for binary data an observation-level random effect is not identifiable (expected to be in the full text; unverified here). An interpretable alternative is the **median hazard ratio**, MHR = exp(√(2σ²)·Φ⁻¹(0.75)) ≈ exp(0.954σ), the cloglog analogue of the median odds ratio (Larsen & Merlo 2005, Am J Epidemiol 161:81–88, for MOR; not fetched; the MHR formula is the same algebra on the log-hazard scale).

## 3. Likelihood: integration over b_i, Laplace vs adaptive Gauss–Hermite, software, Laplace bias

### Takeaway
The marginal likelihood is a product over persons of one-dimensional integrals over b_i, with no closed form under a normal random intercept. Adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature (AGQ) centred at the conditional mode is accurate and the preferred method for a single scalar random intercept; the Laplace approximation (AGQ with one node) can bias estimates for binary data, mostly the variance component, and the bias falls as cluster size grows. In R, lme4::glmer does AGQ (nAGQ > 1) only for a single scalar random effect; glmmTMB uses the Laplace approximation.

### Cited Findings
- Pinheiro & Bates (1995), "Approximations to the log-likelihood function in the nonlinear mixed-effects model", J. Comput. Graph. Stat. 4:12–35, DOI 10.1080/10618600.1995.10474663 (DOI from CRAN pages, not confirmed on the publisher): Laplace and Gaussian quadrature centred at the conditional modes of the random effects are "quite accurate and computationally efficient"; quadrature centred at the expected value was less accurate; importance sampling accurate but slow (verified, via search summary) — [GLMMadaptive CRAN page](https://rdrr.io/cran/GLMMadaptive); [Synapse listing](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1967495697)
- GLMMadaptive fits GLMMs for a single grouping factor by ML, approximating the random-effects integrals with an adaptive Gaussian quadrature rule (verified) — [GLMMadaptive CRAN](https://rdrr.io/cran/GLMMadaptive)
- lme4 glmer: adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature is described as the most reliable approximation but is available only for models with a single scalar random effect; nAGQ default = 1 (Laplace); up to 25 points per scalar integral is suggested as a reasonable upper range; the code accepts up to 100 (verified) — [glmer help, R documentation](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/lme4/html/glmer.html); [glmer.Rd 2.0](https://codegraph.jelmer.uk/lme4/2.0-6-1/man/glmer.Rd)
- glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017, R Journal 9(2):378–400, DOI 10.32614/RJ-2017-066) estimates by maximum likelihood with the Laplace approximation via TMB, using lme4-style formula syntax (verified) — [R Journal RJ-2017-066](https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2017-066/RJ-2017-066.pdf)
- Stata: default integration is mean–variance adaptive Gauss–Hermite with 7 points; Laplace "is equivalent to mode-curvature adaptive Gaussian quadrature with one integration point"; "The Laplacian approximation has been known to produce biased parameter estimates; however, the bias tends to be more prominent in the estimates of the variance components rather than in the estimates of the fixed effects"; more points = more accurate log-likelihood; cost grows as points^(dimension of random effects) (verified, quoted) — [Stata ME manual, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)
- Joe (2008), "Accuracy of Laplace approximation for discrete response mixed models", Comput. Stat. Data Anal. 52(12):5066–5074: the bias of Laplace-based estimators depends mainly on the discreteness of the response, binary/ordinal worse than counts; bias decreases as cluster size increases; Laplace is often adequate and useful for quick model comparison (verified from abstract) — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/csdana/v52y2008i12p5066-5074.html)
- Capanu, Gönen & Begg (2013), "An assessment of estimation methods for generalized linear mixed models with binary outcomes", Stat. Med.: Laplace very accurate for moderate-to-large numbers of observations per random effect and surprisingly good even for small binary clusters (verified from abstract) — [MSKCC working paper](https://biostats.bepress.com/mskccbiostat/paper22); [Synapse](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/46584)
- Breslow & Clayton (1993) found PQL (a Laplace-type approximation) biased for binary data with small clusters (verified, as cited secondarily) — [arXiv 2202.07864](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.07864)

### Inferences
- **Likelihood.** L(β, σ) = Π_i ∫ Π_j p_ij(b)^{y_ij} {1 − p_ij(b)}^{1−y_ij} φ(b; 0, σ²) db, p_ij(b) = 1 − exp(−e^{x_ij'β + b}).
- **Laplace:** expand log of the integrand to second order around its mode b̂_i; ∫ ≈ √(2π) |−h''(b̂_i)|^{−1/2} exp{h(b̂_i)}. Exact if the integrand is Gaussian in b, which it is not for a cluster of a few binary observations; accuracy improves with cluster size because the integrand becomes more Gaussian.
- **AGQ:** substitute b = b̂_i + √2 ŝ_i z, with ŝ_i the curvature-based scale, and apply Q-node Gauss–Hermite: ∫ ≈ √2 ŝ_i Σ_q w_q e^{z_q²} f(b̂_i + √2 ŝ_i z_q). Q = 1 reproduces Laplace. Practical check: refit with more nodes; estimates and log-likelihood should be stable.
- **Small binary clusters (n_i ≤ 9):** these are the setting where the literature flags possible Laplace bias, chiefly downward bias in σ̂² and, through it, attenuated β̂ (Breslow & Lin 1995, Biometrika 82:81–91, not fetched). Evidence is mixed (Joe 2008 vs Capanu et al. 2013), so AGQ with a node-doubling check is the defensible default for a single scalar intercept. The magnitude of bias depends on σ² (larger σ² worse) and on prevalence (rarer outcome = fewer informative clusters).
- **Software summary:** glmer(..., family = binomial(link = "cloglog"), nAGQ = Q) for AGQ with one person intercept; glmmTMB (Laplace only, as far as retrieved); GLMMadaptive (AGQ, single grouping factor; also returns marginal coefficients); Stata mecloglog (AGQ default). SAS PROC NLMIXED also uses AGQ (Pinheiro & Bates 1995 is its primary reference).

## 4. Within- vs between-cluster effects, person-level covariates, Mundlak / cluster-mean centring

### Takeaway
A standard random-intercept GLMM assumes b_i is independent of all covariates. For a covariate that varies within persons (segment geometry, length), the single β then blends within-person and between-person associations; if b_i is correlated with the person mean of the covariate, β is biased for the within effect. Adding the person mean x̄_i (Mundlak) or splitting x_ij into (x_ij − x̄_i) and x̄_i separates the two. Person-level covariates (risk factors) carry only between-person information and are identified only through the random-effects (not the conditional/fixed-effects) likelihood.

### Cited Findings
- Mundlak (1978), "On the pooling of time series and cross section data", Econometrica 46(1):69–85 (JSTOR 1913646, DOI 10.2307/1913646): once dependence between regressors and effects is modelled explicitly, the random-effects and fixed-effects approaches give the same slope estimate, the "within" estimate; any matrix combination of within and between estimates is generally biased (verified from abstract, linear model) — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1978/01/01/pooling-time-series-and-cross-section-data)
- Neuhaus & Kalbfleisch (1998), "Between- and within-cluster covariate effects in the analysis of clustered data", Biometrics 54:638–645, propose separating covariate effects into within- and between-cluster components (verified from citation and a secondary description; full text not retrieved) — [Goetgeluk & Vansteelandt abstract](https://www.jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/IBC2006/Abstracts/pdf/666.pdf); [reference list, arXiv 1001.3011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1001.3011)
- Neuhaus & McCulloch (2006), JRSS-B 68(5):859–872, extend the within/between partitioning in GLMMs (verified that the paper exists in this line of work; content not retrieved) — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssb/v68y2006i5p859-872.html)

### Inferences
- **Decomposition:** η_ij = β_W (x_ij − x̄_i) + β_B x̄_i + z_i'α + b_i. β_W is the within-person (segment-vs-segment) effect; β_B the between-person effect of the person-average level; the conventional model forces β_W = β_B. A Wald/LR test of β_W = β_B is the GLMM analogue of the Hausman test. Equivalent Mundlak form: η = β x_ij + δ x̄_i + ..., with β = β_W, δ = β_B − β_W.
- **Nonlinear-model caveat (flag):** in linear models the Mundlak device recovers the fixed-effects within estimator exactly. In logit/cloglog GLMMs it is only approximate: it relies on E[b_i | x_i] being linear in x̄_i (a "correlated random effects" assumption, Chamberlain-type) and on b_i | x̄_i staying normal with constant variance. Brumback and colleagues showed that cluster-mean adjustment in GLMMs can still leave bias when this fails (Brumback et al., 2010, Stat Med; not fetched; cite cautiously). It is also sensitive to informative cluster size (number of evaluable segments) — Neuhaus & McCulloch line of work.
- **Conditional likelihood as alternative.** For logit, conditioning on the cluster total (conditional logistic regression, stratified by person) eliminates b_i entirely and estimates only within effects, without any distributional assumption on b_i. Person-level covariates drop out. No exact analogue exists for cloglog (no sufficient statistic for b_i), so within-person effects in a cloglog model rely on the random-effects assumptions or on Mundlak terms.
- **Person-level covariates in a GLMM:** they are estimated from between-person contrasts, and their coefficients are conditional on b_i (subject-specific log HRs). Their interpretation as "the effect for a given person" is a model-based extrapolation since the covariate does not vary within person; the marginal/population-averaged effect on person-level risk is obtained by integrating out b_i.
- **Implication for a segment-level model with person-level predictors:** a person-level geometry summary (e.g. a ratio defined once per person) is a between-person covariate, while segment geometry varying within person mixes within and between information unless decomposed.

## 5. Likelihood-ratio tests of fixed effects; testing variance components on the boundary

### Takeaway
Nested fixed-effect blocks in a GLMM are tested with LR = 2(ℓ₁ − ℓ₀) ~ χ²_df, using ML log-likelihoods computed with the same accurate integration (AGQ, same number of nodes) and the same data. Testing σ² = 0 puts the parameter on the boundary, so the null distribution is a 50:50 mixture of χ²₀ and χ²₁ (halve the naive p-value), a result from Self & Liang (1987) that is asymptotic and derived under i.i.d.-cluster assumptions.

### Cited Findings
- Self & Liang (1987), "Asymptotic properties of maximum likelihood estimators and likelihood ratio tests under nonstandard conditions", JASA 82(398):605–610 (JSTOR 2289471): when the true parameter is on the boundary, the limiting distribution of the LR statistic is a mixture of χ² distributions; derived by Chernoff's cone approximation, generalising Moran (1971) and Chant (1974) (verified) — [PDF copy, UW–Madison](https://pages.stat.wisc.edu/~larget/Stat998/Fall2015/Self-Liang-1987.pdf); [review, arXiv 2206.15178](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.15178)
- Two special cases in Self & Liang were later challenged by Kopylev & Sinha (2011) (verified, secondary) — [arXiv 2509.00223](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.00223v1)
- Stram & Lee (1994), "Variance components testing in the longitudinal mixed effects model", Biometrics 50(4):1171–1177, with a correction in Biometrics 51(3):1196 (1995); assumes the response can be split into many i.i.d. subvectors; later work found alternatives (e.g. Crainiceanu & Ruppert 2004 for LMMs, parametric bootstrap) can perform better when that fails (verified, secondary) — [arXiv 2404.15060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.15060); [varTestnlme, arXiv 2007.04791](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.04791)
- Using Laplace instead of AGQ can bias variance components more than fixed effects (verified) — [Stata ME manual](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Fixed effects:** H₀: β_G = 0 for a block of k coefficients; LR = 2{ℓ(β̂, σ̂) − ℓ(β̂₀, σ̂₀)} → χ²_k under H₀, σ² re-estimated under each model (σ² is a nuisance parameter in the interior if σ² > 0). Requirements: same rows (no listwise-deletion differences), same link, same integration method and node count, ML (GLMMs have no REML in glmer). The χ² approximation needs the number of clusters, not observations, to be large; with few clusters LR tests of fixed effects are anticonservative (documented for LMMs by Pinheiro & Bates, *Mixed-Effects Models in S and S-PLUS*, 2000, §2.4; not fetched). Laplace log-likelihoods are approximations; differences between nested models usually behave better than levels (Joe 2008 notes Laplace remains useful for model comparison).
- **Variance component:** H₀: σ² = 0 vs σ² > 0, one parameter on the boundary, no nuisance parameters on the boundary: LR → ½χ²₀ + ½χ²₁, so p = ½ P(χ²₁ > LR). The naive χ²₁ is conservative. For q correlated random effects the mixture weights are more complex (Stram & Lee). For GLMMs the result rests on the same regularity conditions and is asymptotic; with small clusters and binary data, parametric bootstrap is the safer check.
- **Practical thesis relevance (method level only):** a prespecified single LR test of a fixed-effects block is a standard χ²_k test; the variance component is a nuisance parameter that is not tested, so the boundary issue matters mainly if σ̂² ≈ 0 (then the GLMM reduces to an ordinary cloglog GLM, which is a valid fit, not a failure).
