# Model choice for new-plaque prediction in Herlev-Osterbro (review of 01_model_features.tex, sections 1.7 to 1.12)

Date: 2026-09-28. Scope: are Model 1 (logistic regression, mean curvature) and Model 2 (1D-CNN on kappa(s), late fusion) the right pair? Section numbers below refer to the subsections of 01_model_features.tex in reading order: 1.7 Two models, 1.8 One pair per artery domain, 1.9 Age/sex/dominance, 1.10 Curvature profile, 1.11 Events per variable, 1.12 Correlated features.

What the document proposes (read in full): outcome = new plaque at second scan (about 10 years later) in an artery domain (LAD, LCx, RCA) that was plaque-free, in participants whose whole tree was plaque-free at baseline. One pair of models per domain. Model 1 = logistic regression, 12 weights (LAD, LCx) or 5 (RCA). Model 2 = small CNN on kappa(s) with the same scalars fused late, plus a control network given mean curvature. Sample size rule = about 20 events per weight (Ogundimu 2016), ridge on side-branch features if events fall short. Bootstrap/cross-validation is mentioned only as "cross-validation" in 1.12. No mention of within-patient clustering, calibration, TRIPOD+AI, a clinical-only baseline or calcium score. ImageCAS-X (800 trees) supplies only the correlation/reference distribution.

## Q1. Are the two proposed models sensible, and what is the strongest defensible primary model plus one comparator?

### Takeaway
Model 1 (penalized logistic regression) is the right primary model, but it should be pre-specified as ridge-penalized (or Firth) with a nested clinical-only baseline, not plain maximum likelihood. Model 2 as designed (a CNN in a few hundred events at best) is very unlikely to be estimable or to beat logistic regression, and the model pair confounds "profile vs mean" with "nonlinear network vs linear"; a low-dimensional functional summary of kappa(s) inside a penalized logistic model is the better comparator, with the CNN demoted to an exploratory sensitivity analysis.

### Cited Findings
- Riley et al. BMJ 2020 (368:m441) give the current sample-size method for developing prediction models. For binary outcomes it targets four things: shrinkage factor at least 0.9, small optimism in Nagelkerke R2, precise overall risk, and precise calibration-in-the-large. Inputs are prevalence, anticipated C-statistic or Cox-Snell R2, and the number of candidate predictor parameters including categorical expansions and transformations — [PMC primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/); original paper indexed at [BMJ 2020 citation via search](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/sim.9025) (related Riley paper); binary/time-to-event method: [Riley 2019 Stat Med Part II](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30357870/).
- In the primer's worked example (3% prevalence, 25 candidate predictors) the required sample was 9,658 (290 events); cutting to 15 predictors gave 5,795 (174 events) — [PMC primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/). The required size scales with number of parameters, not with a fixed EPV.
- The "10 events per variable" rule has weak evidence behind it: only one of three earlier simulation studies supported it, and the authors call the evidence for EPV rules weak — [van Smeden et al. 2016, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12874-016-0267-3). A follow-up shows EPV is not a good sample-size criterion for prediction models — [van Smeden et al. 2019, SMMR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0962280218784726).
- The document's EPV source (Ogundimu, Altman, Collins 2016, J Clin Epidemiol 76:175-82) is described in its abstract as a resampling study of **Cox regression** with **low-prevalence binary predictors** in a 2-million-record general practice dataset — [Oxford ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:8da45333-9351-451c-91f5-7259c2a83ac7); [Northumbria record](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/adequate-sample-size-for-developing-prediction-models-is-not-simp/). The document (1.11) cites it for logistic regression with mostly continuous predictors.
- A systematic review of 71 studies found no performance benefit of machine learning over logistic regression for clinical prediction models with binary outcomes, in studies with a median of eight events per predictor — [Christodoulou et al. 2019, J Clin Epidemiol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30763612/).
- Penalized regression is effective when EPV is low (below 10) for low-dimensional binary outcomes; the review compared ridge, lasso, Firth and Bayesian shrinkage — [Pavlou et al. 2015 BMJ / 2016 Stat Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26514699/). The document already cites Pavlou for ridge.
- Penalization is not a guarantee: tuning parameters are estimated with large uncertainty when effective sample size is small and Cox-Snell R2 is low, so penalized models can still be unreliable — [Riley et al. 2021, J Clin Epidemiol 132:88-96](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8026952/); a simulation reaches the same conclusion, that shrinkage methods do not guarantee improved performance — [Van Calster et al. 2020, SMMR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0962280220921415).
- Functional logistic regression exists in three main forms: dimension reduction by functional PCA, penalized (spline) functional regression, and wavelet expansions with lasso. FPCA scores as predictors address collinearity but give a non-smooth, hard to interpret coefficient function, which penalized spline versions fix — [Penalized spline functional logit, TEST](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11749-012-0307-1); [robust scalar-on-function logistic regression](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.02508).
- Machine-learning plaque-progression studies with serial CCTA are small: for example 97 patients and 127 plaques with logistic regression, k-nearest neighbours and random forest — [Prediction of plaque progression, PMC11872547](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11872547/). I found no published model using vessel-level centerline curvature profiles to predict new plaque from a plaque-free baseline.

### Inferences
- Illustrative Riley criterion 1 calculation (my own arithmetic from the published shrinkage formula n = p / ((S-1) ln(1 - R2cs/S)), S = 0.9, not from a source page; check with the `pmsampsize` package): p = 12 needs about 5,340 participants at R2cs 0.02, 2,100 at 0.05, 1,020 at 0.10; p = 5 needs 2,225, 875, 425. Geometry-only models for a 10-year outcome will probably sit at the low end of R2cs, so the document's "240 events" (20 x 12) is likely optimistic, and the true requirement depends on the cohort size, which is unknown until access (the document flags this). Report the calculation before opening outcomes, per Riley.
- Because Model 2 has convolutional filters plus a head, its effective parameter count is far larger than 12. Under Riley criteria it is very probably underpowered; any "Model 2 vs Model 1" gap would more likely reflect noise or overfit than kappa(s) information.
- The current design's control network (same architecture, mean curvature) does isolate "position of bends" from "nonlinearity", but it inherits the same estimation problem; a null result would be uninformative rather than negative.
- Strongest defensible primary: ridge (or Firth-penalized, if events are very few) logistic regression, pre-specified parameter set, standardized inputs, penalty chosen by nested cross-validation (with the caveat from Riley 2021 that tuning is itself noisy), reported with bootstrap optimism-corrected calibration and discrimination.
- Strongest defensible comparator: the same penalized logistic model with mean curvature replaced (or supplemented) by 3 to 5 functional-PCA scores (or a few pre-specified segment means, e.g. curvature over proximal, mid, distal thirds) of kappa(s). This tests "position matters" with 3 to 5 extra parameters, is interpretable (FPCA loadings plotted as bend location), and keeps the comparison a nested likelihood-ratio test. Keep the CNN as a clearly labelled exploratory analysis.
- Clinical-only nested baseline is needed (see Q3). It should be Model 0: age, sex, dominance, plus standard risk factors and calcium score if available in the cohort (calcium is zero by construction only in the plaque-free-baseline sample, so its variance may be small; check).

### Gaps
- Herlev-Osterbro repeat-scan sample size, event count per domain, scan interval and available covariates are not known (the document says so). No sample-size conclusion can be final.
- I did not open the Ogundimu paper full text; only its abstract-level description was found, so whether its 20-EPV result carries to logistic regression is unverified.
- I found no published study of CNN vs logistic regression on centerline curvature for incident plaque; the Tello Ayala 2026 study cited in 1.10 was not re-checked here.
- Riley 2020 BMJ page itself was not fetched; details taken from the PMC primer and Stat Med Part II indexing.

## Q2. How should within-patient correlation across artery domains be handled?

### Takeaway
The three artery domains of one patient share age, sex, dominance, LM geometry, risk factors and unmeasured biology, so the three per-domain models are fitted on the same 1 patient x 3 outcomes structure and their errors are correlated. Because the models are fitted separately per domain (1.8), each single-domain fit has one row per patient and no clustering inside a fit; clustering enters when pooling domains, when reporting overall performance across domains, and in resampling. Sections 1.8 and 1.11 do not state this.

### Cited Findings
- Two standard ways to account for clustered data are GEE (population-averaged, robust sandwich standard errors) and multilevel/mixed-effects models (cluster-specific random effects) — [Neurology methods review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11469681/).
- For bootstrap inference, resampling whole clusters is the appropriate approach for clustered data, and cluster bootstrap is preferred over GEE/mixed models when residual covariance assumptions are doubtful (evidence from continuous-outcome settings) — [cluster bootstrap summary, PMC5965657](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5965657/); [Sherman/le Cessie bootstrapping clustered data](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4772364_Bootstrapping_clustered_data).
- Harrell's optimism-corrected bootstrap is the conventional internal validation; Steyerberg and Harrell concluded bootstrapping is recommended for the internal validity of a logistic prediction model — [Steyerberg/Harrell summary via review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12874-020-01201-w); [Harrell on bootstrap](https://www.fharrell.com/post/bootcal/).

### Inferences
- Keep the per-domain separate fits as the primary analysis (no clustering within a fit), but (a) in every bootstrap and cross-validation resample by patient, never by artery or domain, so that a patient's three domains and any repeated rows never straddle train and test; (b) if a pooled model across domains is offered as a sensitivity analysis, use a domain-indicator model with either GEE (exchangeable or unstructured working correlation) or a patient random intercept; the pooled model would also let the thesis test "does curvature act the same in all arteries" via a domain-by-curvature interaction, which the current three-way comparison does not do formally; (c) report the three domain results as correlated, since they are not independent replicates of the curvature test; the RCA pair as "cleanest test" (1.8) is fine, but "the comparison is made three times" (1.8) should not be read as three independent confirmations.
- Because domain outcomes are correlated, a multivariate or "any new plaque" secondary outcome and the joint distribution across domains (e.g. how many participants have events in more than one domain) should be tabulated, since events per domain and events per patient differ. Riley-type sample size is in participants, not arteries.
- Choose GEE if the aim is population-averaged prediction (coefficients comparable to Model 1); choose a random intercept only if patient-specific predictions are needed. For a small number of correlated units per cluster (3), cluster-robust variance is fine, but with few events a penalized fit with patient-level bootstrap CIs is more reliable.

### Gaps
- I found no source specific to prediction-model development with 3 correlated outcomes per patient (domain-level rather than lesion-level); the guidance above is general clustered-data methodology transferred by inference. Riley has clustered-data sample-size work, but I could not locate and verify it in this session.

## Q3. What would a reviewer criticise, and what should change?

### Takeaway
The likeliest criticisms are: (1) the CNN is unsupported by the event count and adds nothing over penalized regression in the base literature; (2) no clinical-only baseline or incremental value test, so "geometry predicts plaque" is not separated from "age, sex and calcium predict plaque"; (3) a prediction-model claim with no calibration, no optimism-corrected validation, no TRIPOD+AI reporting plan; (4) an unverified EPV source and no Riley calculation; (5) fixed 10-year binary outcome with no treatment of loss to follow-up or varying interval; (6) clustering unaddressed.

### Cited Findings
- TRIPOD+AI (BMJ 2024) is the current reporting standard for prediction models using regression or machine learning; it supersedes TRIPOD 2015 and covers development, validation, and combined studies — [TRIPOD+AI, PMC11025451](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11025451/).
- Calibration is the weak spot of prediction studies; poorly calibrated models can mislead, and calibration should be assessed at validation and managed by balancing model complexity against sample size — [Van Calster et al. 2019, BMC Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-019-1466-7).
- Uncertainty in sample size and performance matters: sample size criteria should account for variability in performance — [arXiv 2509.14028](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14028) (preprint, not peer-reviewed as far as I could see).
- The `pmsampsize` package (CRAN, v1.1.3) implements Riley criteria for binary and time-to-event outcomes — [pmsampsize CRAN](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pmsampsize/pmsampsize.pdf).
- Serial-CCTA plaque progression ML studies exist but with small samples and non-geometric features (pericoronary fat radiomics) — [PMC11872547](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11872547/).

### Inferences
Concrete changes, keyed to document sections:

1. Section 1.7 (two models). Rename the comparison. Primary = Model 1, penalized logistic with mean curvature. Comparator = same model with a low-dimensional functional summary of kappa(s) (FPCA scores, or thirds). Add Model 0 = clinical-only (age, sex, dominance, plus risk factors and calcium score if the cohort has them). Report the nested sequence 0, 1, 1+profile with likelihood-ratio tests, delta C-statistic, and delta calibration. Keep the CNN plus its control as exploratory and say it is likely underpowered. State the outcome definition once, including that participants are plaque-free at baseline, so "risk factors adding to geometry" is judged inside a low-risk group.
2. Section 1.7 outcome. The text says "about ten years later"; if intervals vary, a fixed-horizon logistic model is misspecified. Options: include scan interval as a covariate/offset in a complementary log-log model (discrete-time or interval-censored survival, since plaque appearance is only known between scans), or restrict to a narrow interval window. Plain Cox is not directly suited because event time is interval-censored between two scans; if the interval is near-constant, logistic is defensible. This point rests on my reasoning; I did not find a source specific to it. Also decide how death or loss between scans is handled (selection: only those returning for a second scan are analyzed), which is a source of bias to report.
3. Section 1.8 (domains). Add the clustering statement and resample by patient (Q2). Add a domain interaction test in a pooled sensitivity model. Note that the left-dominant reassignment of PDA/PLA to LCx makes domain definitions depend on dominance, which is also a predictor, so the outcome and predictor share information; consider reporting results within dominance stratum or at least checking the outcome rate by dominance.
4. Section 1.9 (age, sex, dominance). Fine as adjustment. Nonlinearity of age (splines with 3 knots costs 2 more parameters) and an age x curvature interaction should be pre-specified or explicitly excluded because parameters count against the Riley budget. Clinical covariates beyond age, sex, dominance (lipids, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, calcium score) are absent; a reviewer will say geometry may proxy for them. If available in CGPS, add them to Model 0 and to all models.
5. Section 1.10 (profile). The Tello Ayala evidence is angiographic 2D, different outcome (existing CAD) and no significance test, as the document already admits; it does not license a CNN in 3D CCTA with a few hundred events. Rely on the profile for a hypothesis about location; test it first with the FPCA comparator. Also specify how profiles of different length, and arteries with variable branching, are aligned: the document uses a fixed length L from the origin, so shorter arteries need a stated padding rule; FPCA requires a common domain or registration.
6. Section 1.11 (EPV). Replace the 20-EPV rule with a Riley et al. calculation (pmsampsize) done before outcome access, using anticipated prevalence and R2cs; report the shrinkage factor target of 0.9 and the number of participants and events available. Verify Ogundimu (Cox, low-prevalence binary predictors) before citing it for logistic regression, or drop it; van Smeden shows EPV alone is not a valid criterion. Cite Riley 2021 and Van Calster 2020 to admit that ridge tuning is itself noisy with few events. Consider Firth or a Bayesian shrinkage prior (weakly informative on standardized coefficients, and the document's pre-specified signs in Tables give natural informative priors) as alternatives to ridge; Pavlou et al. compare these.
7. Section 1.12 (correlated features). The plan (ridge or drop a family, report VIF, family-level LR tests) is sound for prediction. Add: predictive performance is judged with optimism-corrected bootstrap resampling patients, calibration slope and intercept and a calibration plot, Brier score, and decision curve only if a decision threshold is argued. The sentence about "cross-validation" should state which validation, whether nested for the penalty. Ridge with ImageCAS-X correlation matrix is fine as a normative reference, but the ImageCAS-X cohort has no outcomes and different (patient, disease, scanner) mix; it must not be used to select coefficients or shrinkage, only to define feature reference distributions and correlation families, and to check pipeline stability. The document states this in 1.12; keep it explicit in the model section too. Keep external validity in scope: no external validation cohort exists, so TRIPOD+AI item on internal-only validation and its limits should be written.
8. Add a reporting plan: TRIPOD+AI checklist as an appendix; pre-registration of the analysis (primary outcome, parameter list, penalty, validation) before opening outcomes; a data-flow (STARD-like) diagram for exclusions (baseline plaque, missing second scan, failed segmentation, absent branches); handling of missing features (D1/OM1 absent is coded by indicator, already good).
9. Tree-based alternatives (random forest, gradient boosting) are not recommended as primary with these events: no evidence they beat logistic regression in low-signal small-event settings (Christodoulou). They could serve as a nonlinear sanity check on the scalars (fitted on the same folds), replacing part of the CNN's role of testing for nonlinearity at lower complexity.
