# Scalar (patient/vessel-level average) torsion as a CAD-prediction feature

## Does spatial averaging fix torsion's noise problem, or is the problem systematic bias that averaging cannot fix?

### Takeaway
Torsion's error has two distinct components documented in the literature: a random, point-to-point noise component (reducible by averaging, in the ordinary statistical sense) and a systematic component tied to the discretization/smoothing pipeline itself (window size, spline order, centerline resolution) that does **not** average out, because it is a property of the estimator/pipeline, not of the sample. No study was found that directly decomposes coronary-centerline torsion error into these two components at multiple resolutions; the case for "some noise averages out, some bias does not" is assembled from adjacent numerical-differentiation and centerline-geometry reliability studies rather than from one paper that measures both.

### Cited Findings
- Torsion depends on the third derivative of the centerline curve; estimating it from real (even low-noise) discretized data "can be very unstable," and this instability is described as intrinsic to the Frenet-Serret formulation's third-order derivative structure, not merely a function of how much raw noise is present — [Curvature and Torsion estimation of 3D functional data (arXiv 2203.02398)](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02398)
- A 2026 sensitivity-analysis paper (Curvature Sensitivity Index / Torsion Sensitivity Index, published in *Axioms*) derives the Torsion Sensitivity Index as "a first-order variance bound under the delta method framework to characterize noise amplification in torsion estimation," and compares cubic-spline vs. cubic-Hermite reconstruction, finding cubic-spline reconstruction gives lower Monte Carlo RMSE and reduced empirical variance for torsion estimates — [Spline-Based Smoothing of Noisy Discrete Curves in the Frenet–Serret Framework (MDPI Axioms 15(5):365, 2026)](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1680/15/5/365). Note: full text of this paper could not be fetched directly (blocked/403 on two attempts); the above is reconstructed from search-result summaries only, not verified against the primary text, so treat the exact quantitative relationship between smoothing bandwidth and bias vs. variance as unconfirmed.
- A 2026 blood-vessel morphology classification paper (Zhong et al., arXiv 2607.14195) computes torsion via a constrained least-squares derivative approximation "with adjustable window size and noise robustness," explicitly trading window size against derivative stability — the classic bias/variance tradeoff signature (larger window suppresses noise variance but smooths away real local structure, i.e. introduces bias) — [A Hybrid Framework for Blood Vessel Morphology Classification (arXiv 2607.14195)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14195)
- In a carotid-bifurcation bend-landmarking comparative study, centerline **resolution** (not raw coordinate noise) was found to have the largest impact on results: the number of detected bends ranged from 3 to 33 depending on resolution choice alone — [Automated landmarking of bends in vascular structures (BioMedical Engineering OnLine, Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-021-00957-6)
- Traditional finite-difference torsion estimators "use a fixed window size, which may cause large errors and amplify noise, especially when computing third-order derivatives," motivating adjustable/discrete-geometry estimators as a fix — [A Hybrid Framework for Blood Vessel Morphology Classification (arXiv 2607.14195)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14195)

### Inferences
- The resolution-dependence finding (3 to 33 bends depending on centerline resolution) is about curvature-extrema landmarking, not torsion directly, and is offered here only by analogy: it demonstrates that a differential-geometry quantity's *systematic* value can shift wholesale with a discretization choice, independent of any particular noisy sample. If coronary torsion behaves similarly (plausible, since torsion is a higher-order and more noise-sensitive quantity than curvature), then a single pipeline's chosen smoothing/window/spacing sets a fixed bias in every torsion value it produces — averaging many points from *that same pipeline* would shrink the scatter around that pipeline's biased answer, but would not move the answer toward a resolution-independent "true" torsion. Two different pipelines (different smoothing bandwidths) could each report a highly reproducible (low-variance) but different scalar mean torsion for the same vessel.
- Given this, spatial/vessel-level averaging is expected to genuinely help with the random component of torsion noise (consistent with elementary statistics: averaging many roughly-unbiased, weakly-correlated point estimates reduces the variance of the mean), but it is not a substitute for validating that the chosen centerline-extraction and smoothing pipeline itself does not introduce a resolution- or bandwidth-dependent systematic offset. Averaging converts "noisy and unusable" into "smoother but still pipeline-dependent," not into "unbiased."

### Gaps
- No study was found that computes coronary (or any single vascular bed's) torsion at multiple explicit spatial scales/resolutions and reports whether the *scalar mean* torsion is stable across those scales (a direct test of "does averaging fix it"). This is the most direct evidence that would resolve the question, and it does not appear to exist in the published literature located here.
- The Axioms 2026 CSI/TSI paper is the closest published attempt at a formal noise-sensitivity analysis for torsion estimation, but its full text was inaccessible in this session (403 errors from both the DOI redirect and the MDPI page); its conclusions above should be re-verified from the primary source before being relied on for the thesis.

## Does the profile-beats-mean pattern found for tortuosity/curvature (Tello Ayala et al. 2026, PMC13308244, AUROC 0.67 profile vs 0.60 mean) generalize to torsion, and if so does that argue against even a scalar torsion feature?

### Takeaway
No study anywhere was found that compares a per-point torsion profile against a scalar mean torsion for any prediction task, in CAD or otherwise — the profile-vs-mean question that Tello Ayala et al. answered for tortuosity/curvature has simply never been asked for torsion. This makes the extrapolation from curvature's profile-beats-mean result to torsion an inference, not a finding, and it is a weaker inference than it looks: the studies located here that do use torsion at all skip the profile representation entirely and go straight to scalar/aggregate torsion, for reasons that appear to be practical (noise-sensitivity of the per-point signal) rather than a considered scalar-vs-profile comparison.
- Note: the project's own prior conclusion (excluding a per-point torsion channel from Model 2) is treated here as established context; it is not re-litigated in this note, only used to check consistency of what the current literature adds.

### Cited Findings
- Kashyap, Gharleghi et al. 2022 tested four coronary tortuosity/curvature metrics against low-TAWSS in the left main bifurcation and branches (n=127 patients without CAD): Tortuosity Index, Average Absolute-Curvature, RMS-Curvature, and Average Squared-Derivative-Curvature. All four are scalar/averaged measures over the vessel; **no profile-based (per-point) metric and no torsion metric at all** were included in the comparison. Average absolute-curvature performed best (LMCA R²=0.196, p<0.001; LAD R²=0.232, p<0.001; bifurcation R²=0.112, p=0.001), while the classical tortuosity index (also scalar) showed no significant relationship (p=0.86 at LMCA) — [Accuracy of vascular tortuosity measures using computational modelling (Scientific Reports 12:865, 2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- Zhu/Pao et al. 2008 catalogued torsion in 67 angiographically-normal coronary arteries (52 patients) using three *segment-averaged scalars per proximal/middle/distal segment* — mean torsion (MeT), maximum torsion (MaT), and standard deviation of torsion (SDT) — not a continuous per-point profile and not a single whole-vessel scalar either, but an intermediate segment-level aggregation. No profile representation of torsion was used or compared — [Cataloguing the geometry of the human coronary arteries (Int J Cardiol 135(1):43-52, 2008)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)
- Zhong et al. 2026 (vessel morphology classification) use only aggregated torsion features — Average Absolute Torsion, Total Absolute Torsion, and an Average Curvature/Torsion ratio — never a per-point torsion profile, while for curvature they retain more granular features. Ablation showed removing the aggregated torsion features cost more in a three-class (ternary) morphology task (Macro-F1 dropped 0.8626→0.8492) than in a binary task, and torsion carried roughly 18-25% of feature importance weight depending on task — [A Hybrid Framework for Blood Vessel Morphology Classification (arXiv 2607.14195)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14195). This is a vessel-morphology classification task, not a CAD-outcome prediction task, and it is unclear from the available text whether "morphology class" corresponds to a clinical category; treat this as an indirect analogue, not CAD evidence.

### Inferences
- Every study located that uses torsion at all (Zhu/Pao 2008, Zhong et al. 2026) collapses it to a scalar or segment-level aggregate before using it; none tests a torsion profile. This is consistent with, but does not prove, the idea that torsion's per-point signal is considered too unreliable to use directly (matching the project's existing rationale for excluding it from Model 2), rather than that a profile was tried and shown to underperform a mean.
- Because the profile-beats-mean finding for curvature/tortuosity in Tello Ayala et al. rests on curvature's third-derivative-free (lower-order, less noise-amplified) computation, and torsion has no equivalent evidence in either direction, extrapolating "profile beats mean, so scalar torsion is doomed too" is not supported by direct evidence. The more defensible reading is the opposite: since no torsion profile has ever been shown to add information over a torsion mean, there is no existing result to be "beaten" — a scalar torsion feature is not undercut by an established profile that outperforms it, unlike curvature/tortuosity.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head profile-vs-mean torsion comparison exists in the literature located here, in CAD or any other outcome. This absence should be reported as a gap, not filled with an assumption either way.
- Whether Zhong et al.'s "morphology classification" task has any relationship to CAD, stenosis, or plaque risk categories could not be determined from the fetched summary; the underlying paper would need to be read in full to know if it is even a relevant analogue.

## Is there a published study, in coronary arteries or any other vascular bed, using a scalar/average torsion value as a predictor of a clinical or hemodynamic outcome?

### Takeaway
No study was found, in coronary arteries or any other vascular bed, that uses a scalar/average **geometric torsion** value as a predictor of a clinical outcome (CAD, stenosis, aneurysm) or a hemodynamic outcome (WSS, helicity) with a reported effect size. The closest positive precedents in the literature use scalar aggregates of *related but distinct* quantities — scalar average curvature predicting low-WSS area, and scalar maximum flow helicity predicting plaque progression — but in both cases the paper explicitly measured curvature or helicity, not torsion. This absence is itself the main finding for this question.

### Cited Findings
- Kashyap, Gharleghi et al. 2022 explicitly did **not include torsion** among the four tortuosity/curvature metrics tested against WSS in coronary left-main bifurcations (n=127); their best scalar predictor of low-TAWSS was average absolute-curvature (R²=0.196-0.232, p<0.001 across LMCA/LAD), and they recommend it over the classical tortuosity index — [Accuracy of vascular tortuosity measures using computational modelling (Scientific Reports 12:865, 2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- Zhu/Pao et al. 2008 report scalar (segment-averaged) mean/max/SD torsion descriptively for 67 coronary arteries but explicitly do **not** correlate it with any disease presence, severity, plaque burden, or clinical outcome; the paper frames this as a "long-term goal" for future work using epidemiological data, not something the paper itself accomplishes — [Cataloguing the geometry of the human coronary arteries (Int J Cardiol 135(1):43-52, 2008)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)
- A related paper, "Geodesic pattern of coronary arteries as a predictor for cardiovascular risk," states this geometric information (including torsion) "may conceivably possess an adjunctive merit in estimating cardiovascular risk" — phrased as speculation ("may conceivably"), not a demonstrated result, and no effect size or outcome correlation was surfaced for it — [Geodesic pattern of coronary arteries as a predictor for cardiovascular risk (PubMed 19108911)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19108911)
- A closely-related, non-geometric scalar did show a significant outcome association: normalized **maximum flow helicity** (a CFD-derived hemodynamic scalar, aggregated as the maximum value within a stenotic lesion segment, not a per-point profile) predicted coronary plaque progression (diameter stenosis increase ≥5% over ~2 years) with OR=1.44 (95% CI 1.07-1.93, p=0.016) in a cohort of 22 patients / 34 lesions / 209 lesion segments. This study did not use geometric torsion at all — [Hemodynamic Study of Plaque Progression and Regression Based on Coronary CTA Imaging using CFD (Lv S. et al., J Cardiovasc Transl Res 19(1):3, 2026)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12858539/)

### Inferences
- The plaque-progression helicity result is the strongest available precedent for "a scalar, aggregated, derived geometric/hemodynamic descriptor of vessel shape can carry real outcome signal," which weakly supports the general strategy of using scalar summaries of complex 3D vessel behavior as CAD-model covariates. But it is evidence for scalar *helicity*, not scalar *torsion*, and helicity is computed from the flow field (a CFD output), not from the centerline geometry alone the way torsion is — so this does not transfer as direct torsion evidence, only as a structural analogy one step removed.
- Combined with Kashyap et al.'s explicit exclusion of torsion from their WSS-prediction comparison (despite testing multiple other coronary shape metrics) and Zhu/Pao et al.'s explicit non-pursuit of a torsion-outcome correlation despite having the data to attempt it, the literature reads less like "torsion was tried and failed" and more like "torsion has been consistently set aside as a covariate," even in papers built specifically to test scalar shape descriptors against outcomes.

### Gaps
- As instructed: no study anywhere (coronary or otherwise) was found using scalar/average torsion as a predictor of a clinical or hemodynamic outcome with a reported effect size, correlation, or significance test. This absence should be reported plainly as a finding, not inferred away.

## Does the contested torsion-helicity-WSS pathway provide a plausible mechanistic case for scalar torsion, even without direct outcome evidence?

### Takeaway
The mechanistic chain (torsion → helical flow intensity → favorable/unfavorable WSS → CAD risk) requires two separate links to hold, and the literature located here casts doubt on the first link specifically for coronary arteries: a 2025 study that directly tested geometric torsion against helicity intensity in coronary arteries found the correlation was **not statistically significant** after multiple-comparison correction. Combined with the second link being explicitly "double-edged" (helicity reduces low-WSS area but increases high-WSS/oscillatory area linked to plaque vulnerability, per the same paper), the case for scalar torsion as a CAD covariate through this pathway is mechanistically plausible in outline but empirically weak at both links, not just contested at the second link as the prior project note framed it.

### Cited Findings
- Shen, Zhang, Keramati, Ferreira de Almeida, Beier (2025), "High-intensity helical flow: a double-edged sword in coronary artery haemodynamics" (Royal Society Open Science, DOI 10.1098/rsos.242184): tested correlations between geometric factors (including torsion) and helical-flow-based factors, and found "no statistically significant correlations were found between geometrical and HF-based factors" after Bonferroni-Holm correction, though some torsion-helicity correlations reached p<0.05 before correction in diagonal-branch arteries — [High-intensity helical flow: a double-edged sword in coronary artery haemodynamics (PMC12324882)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324882/)
- The same paper reports the "double-edged" hemodynamic result directly: in stenosed segments, a helicity-related metric (h2) was positively correlated with high time-averaged endothelial shear stress area (ρ=0.87, p=0.0004) and negatively correlated with low time-averaged endothelial shear stress area (ρ=-0.89, p=0.0001) — i.e. helicity simultaneously suppresses one adverse WSS regime and promotes another, confirming the "double-edged sword" characterization rather than a simple protective effect — [same source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324882/)
- Torsion and curvature are independently described elsewhere in the literature as "well-known strong helicity initiators" in general vascular flow, which is the standing mechanistic premise the double-edged-sword paper's torsion-helicity test was checking — [search-result summary citing helical flow / geometry literature; see also Gallo, Steinman, Bijari, Morbiducci, "Helical flow in carotid bifurcation as surrogate marker of exposure to disturbed shear," J Biomech 2012]
- The project's own prior note cites a torsion-helicity correlation of r=0.64 and a helicity-to-unfavorable-WSS anti-correlation of r=-0.91 from an earlier source; this session could not independently verify either figure — the presumed source, "Improved prediction of disturbed flow via hemodynamically-inspired geometric variables" (PMC3371282, Gallo/Morbiducci/Steinman-adjacent group, carotid bifurcation), was blocked by a CAPTCHA wall on fetch. A related search snippet for helicity-disturbed-flow models in that literature area reports "adjusted R² in the range 0.4-0.7 (P<0.0001)," which is broadly the same order of magnitude as r≈0.64 (r²≈0.41) but is not a confirmed match to the specific figure.

### Inferences
- Treating the mechanistic case honestly: it now rests on (a) an unverified, single-source, possibly carotid-not-coronary r=0.64 torsion-helicity correlation from an older study that this session could not access, versus (b) a 2025 coronary-specific, purpose-built test of the same relationship that found no significant correlation after correcting for multiple comparisons. Where these conflict, the more recent, coronary-specific, and correction-aware result should be weighted more heavily; it directly contradicts the premise that torsion reliably drives helicity intensity in coronary arteries specifically.
- Even granting the torsion-helicity link, the helicity-WSS link is now explicitly bidirectional/double-edged rather than uniformly protective, so a monotonic story ("more torsion → more helicity → more protective WSS → lower CAD risk") is not defensible even in principle; the honest mechanistic statement is that torsion could plausibly push WSS in either a favorable or unfavorable direction depending on the local flow regime, which is a wash for feature-engineering purposes.
- Given both links are now individually contested (one non-significant after correction, one explicitly two-directional), the mechanistic argument does not rescue scalar torsion in the absence of direct outcome evidence. It is best described as a plausible-sounding but currently empirically unsupported hypothesis rather than an evidence-backed rationale.

### Gaps
- The primary carotid-geometry source for the project's r=0.64 / r=-0.91 figures (PMC3371282) could not be accessed in this session (CAPTCHA-blocked); this should be re-checked directly rather than relied on secondhand before it is used to justify any modeling decision.
- No coronary-specific study was found that tests the full three-link chain (torsion → helicity → WSS → clinical outcome) in one cohort; the evidence used above is stitched together from separate studies of adjacent links, which is explicitly a chain of inference, not a single confirmatory result.
