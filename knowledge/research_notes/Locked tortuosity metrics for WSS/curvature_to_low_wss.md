# Curvature/Bending Geometry Links to Low Wall Shear Stress and Plaque Localization

Scope note: this covers curvature and bending geometry specifically (mean/local/peak curvature,
radius of curvature, bend angle, bifurcation/branch angle) and their links to low WSS and plaque
location. Torsion/non-planarity/helical flow is out of scope per assignment (covered by another
researcher) except where a source ties curvature and torsion together inseparably (none found in
this pass).

## What is the classic evidence that low/oscillating/disturbed WSS localizes at curved-segment
inner walls, bifurcation flow-divider outer walls, or high-curvature zones generally, and what is
the specific mechanism?

### Takeaway
The foundational Zarins/Ku/Giddens/Glagov/Malek body of work establishes that atherosclerotic
plaque co-localizes with low and oscillating WSS at the **outer, non-divider walls of vessel
bifurcations**, where flow separates and recirculates; the **flow-divider/inner-branch wall stays
comparatively disease-free at higher, more unidirectional shear**. This is a distinct geometric
picture from simple curved-tube (single-vessel bend) CFD studies, which instead put the low-WSS
zone on the **inner (concave) wall of the bend** because the velocity profile skews toward the
outer wall. Both patterns are real and repeatedly reported, but they describe two different
geometric features (branch-point flow division vs. simple bend curvature) and the "inner/outer"
label does not transfer between them without care.

### Cited Findings
- Malek, Alper, Izumo, "Hemodynamic Shear Stress and Its Role in Atherosclerosis," *JAMA*
  1999;282(21):2035-2042 (primary text read in full from PDF, not abstract-only):
  - "Despite the systemic nature of its associated risk factors, atherosclerosis is a
    geometrically focal disease that has a propensity to involve the outer edges of blood vessel
    bifurcations... In these susceptible areas, blood flow is slow and changes direction with the
    cardiac cycle, resulting in a weak net hemodynamic shear stress." — [Malek et al. 1999 PDF](https://veryanmed.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Malek-JAMA.pdf)
  - "Atherogenesis preferentially involves the outer walls of vessel bifurcations and points of
    blood flow recirculation and stasis. In these geometrically predisposed locations, fluid shear
    stress on the vessel wall is significantly lower in magnitude and exhibits directional changes
    and flow separation... Direct measurements and fluid mechanical models of these susceptible
    regions have revealed shear values on the order of ±4 dyne/cm2 compared with greater than 12
    dyne/cm2 in the protected areas." — [Malek et al. 1999](https://veryanmed.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Malek-JAMA.pdf)
  - "High-speed cinematography and microparticle flow analysis in postmortem coronary arterial
    trees have correlated subintimal thickening with the low wall shear stress of bifurcations; in
    contrast, pathologic lesions were absent from the flow-dividers and inner wall where shear is
    higher." (citing Asakura & Karino 1990, *Circ Res* 66:1045-1066, on human coronary arteries
    specifically) — [Malek et al. 1999](https://veryanmed.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Malek-JAMA.pdf)
  - En-face endothelial studies of human thoracic aortas show "leukocyte adhesion, accumulation of
    subendothelial macrophages and lymphocytes, irregular endothelial morphology with denuded
    regions covered with platelets, and dilated intercellular clefts in the outer walls but not in
    the inner walls or flow divider of bifurcations." — [Malek et al. 1999](https://veryanmed.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Malek-JAMA.pdf), citing Kolpakov et al. 1996
  - Quantitative shear thresholds given directly in the paper: physiologic/atheroprotective arterial
    shear is ">15 dyne/cm2"; atherogenic low shear is "<4 dyne/cm2" (stated as "0 to ±4 dyne/cm2" in
    several places); normal arterial range is "10 to 70 dyne/cm2." — [Malek et al. 1999](https://veryanmed.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Malek-JAMA.pdf)
  - Two contradictory 1970s hypotheses are noted and resolved in favor of the low-shear side: Fry
    (1969) proposed direct endothelial injury from supraphysiologic high shear (400 dyne/cm2); Caro
    et al. (1971) proposed low shear as the atherogenic driver; "subsequent observations and studies
    made in the last 3 decades have validated the low-shear hypothesis of atherosclerosis." —
    [Malek et al. 1999](https://veryanmed.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Malek-JAMA.pdf)
  - The mechanism stated is recirculation/stasis and flow separation at the outer bifurcation wall,
    plus oscillatory (direction-reversing) shear there; this is explicitly contrasted with steady,
    higher-magnitude, unidirectional flow at the flow divider. No mention in the verified text of a
    "curved segment inner wall" mechanism as distinct from the bifurcation case — the paper's own
    geometric vocabulary is bifurcation-centric (outer bifurcation wall vs. flow divider), not
    single-vessel-bend-centric.
- Ku, Giddens, Zarins, Glagov, "Pulsatile flow and atherosclerosis in the human carotid
  bifurcation. Positive correlation between plaque location and low oscillating shear stress,"
  *Arteriosclerosis* 1985;5:293-302: measured flow velocities by laser Doppler velocimetry in a
  scale model of the human carotid bifurcation under pulsatile flow and directly compared wall
  shear stress at multiple axial/circumferential positions with intimal plaque thickness in
  matched cadaver carotid bifurcations, establishing the positive correlation between plaque
  location and low, oscillating shear stress. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3994585/); [full text PDF](http://atvb.ahajournals.org/content/5/3/293.full.pdf)
  - Ku et al. also developed the oscillatory shear index (OSI) in this study; per a secondary
    summary (not independently verified against the primary PDF in this pass), "the correlation
    between the OSI and intimal thickness was positive and strong at the outer and inner walls of
    the carotid sinus" — flagged as sourced from a search-engine synthesis, not a direct read of the
    paper's text, so treat as lower-confidence pending verification.
- Zarins, Giddens, Bharadvaj, Sottiurai, Mabon, Glagov, "Carotid bifurcation atherosclerosis:
  quantitative correlation of plaque localization with flow velocity profiles and wall shear
  stress," *Circ Res* 1983;53:502-514 — the earlier, direct antecedent quantitatively correlating
  plaque location with velocity/WSS profiles at the carotid bifurcation; cited as reference 5
  inside Malek et al. 1999 (verified: this citation appears repeatedly in the primary text I read).
- Chiu & Chien, "Effects of Disturbed Flow on Vascular Endothelium: Pathophysiological Basis and
  Clinical Perspectives," *Physiological Reviews* 2011;91:327-387: "In branches and curvatures,
  blood flow is disturbed with nonuniform and irregular distribution of low wall shear stress...
  the disturbed flow pattern in branch points and curvatures causes the preferential localization
  of atherosclerotic lesions." This review explicitly groups "curvatures" together with "branch
  points" as the two canonical disturbed-flow geometric features, rather than treating simple
  bends and bifurcations as mechanistically identical — the flagged nuance above (different
  inner/outer conventions) is consistent with the review discussing both as separate but analogous
  categories. — [Physiological Reviews, full text](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/physrev.00047.2009); this summary was obtained via a search synthesis, not a direct verified read of the full PDF, so treat the exact wording as abstract/summary-level pending full-text confirmation.
- Johnston & Johnston (2007; commonly referenced as the RCA curvature/WSS paper), *Phys Med Biol*
  52(9): "There is a strong correlation between WSS and reciprocal radius and a weaker correlation
  between high curvature and extremes of WSS, with high WSS on the 'inside' of a bend and low WSS
  on the 'outside' of a bend" for the overall dataset, but "for each artery, a region proximal to
  the acute margin is identified where low WSS is found and where WSS is lower on the 'inner' wall
  of the RCA than on the 'outer' wall" — [IOPscience](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0031-9155/52/9/013). These two statements from the same abstract-level fetch appear to
  point in opposite directions for inner vs. outer wall; this is flagged explicitly as an internal
  tension in the source that could not be resolved without the full paper text (only the abstract
  was accessible in this pass — full-text access was blocked). A plausible reading is that the
  general/bulk correlation (WSS vs. 1/R) behaves one way while the specific near-acute-margin
  region behaves the opposite way, i.e. curvature sign/location-dependence matters, but this is
  inference, not a verified statement from the source.
- Mechanisms named consistently across these sources for why curved/branching geometry produces
  low WSS: **flow separation**, **recirculation/stasis zones**, and (for curved tubes specifically)
  **secondary flow / skewed velocity profile toward the outer wall** (Dean-type secondary flow).
  The skewed-profile mechanism for simple curved tubes is stated directly in a search synthesis:
  "Laminar fluid flow in curved vessels is typified by the skewing of the velocity profile toward
  the outer wall, creating a relatively low wall shear stress at the inner wall" — this is a
  search-engine synthesis of multiple curved-tube CFD sources, not a single verified primary quote,
  flagged accordingly.

### Inferences
- The classic, most rigorously verified evidence (Malek et al. 1999, read in full) is about
  **bifurcation geometry** (outer bifurcation wall = low WSS/atherogenic; flow-divider/inner-branch
  wall = high WSS/protected), not about simple single-vessel curvature. If the report's argument
  needs a citation specifically for "inner wall of a bend has low WSS," the curved-tube CFD
  literature (Johnston, and general Dean-flow secondary-flow arguments) is the right citation, not
  Malek et al., whose own geometric vocabulary is bifurcation-centric.
- Because bend geometry and bifurcation geometry produce low WSS on geometrically opposite sides
  (concave inner wall of a simple bend vs. convex outer, non-divider wall of a bifurcation), any
  report combining both mechanisms must be explicit about which geometric feature (a smooth bend
  vs. a branch point) each cited low-WSS/plaque finding refers to, to avoid an apparent
  contradiction.

### Gaps
- Full-text access to Chiu & Chien 2011 and to Johnston & Johnston 2007 was blocked (paywall/only
  abstract visible); the quotes above from these two sources are at abstract/search-synthesis
  confidence, not full-primary-source-verified confidence like the Malek et al. 1999 PDF.
- Could not independently verify the Ku et al. 1985 OSI/intimal-thickness "positive and strong"
  correlation claim against the primary PDF within the tool budget for this task; flagged as
  lower-confidence.

## Within a curved tube/vessel bend, is there a quantitative or well-established relationship
between local curvature (1/radius of curvature) magnitude and the magnitude/extent of the
resulting low-WSS region on the inner wall — and is it robust/generalizable or case-specific?

### Takeaway
There is a well-established *qualitative* relationship (higher curvature -> lower inner-wall WSS,
via secondary-flow/velocity-profile skewing), and at least one patient-specific coronary study
reports a *strong* quantitative correlation between WSS and reciprocal radius; but multiple sources
note the curvature-to-WSS relationship is sensitive to which curvature metric and which vessel
segment is used, so it is not straightforwardly generalizable across all bend geometries and
calibers without qualification.

### Cited Findings
- Johnston & Johnston (Phys Med Biol 2007), four human right coronary arteries reconstructed from
  bi-plane angiograms, generalized power-law viscosity model: "There is a strong correlation
  between WSS and reciprocal radius and a weaker correlation between high curvature and extremes of
  WSS." — [IOPscience abstract](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0031-9155/52/9/013)
  (abstract-level access only; full text not verified in this pass).
- Kashyap, Gharleghi, et al., "Accuracy of vascular tortuosity measures using computational
  modelling," *Scientific Reports* 2022 (12:xxxxx), 127-patient CTCA cohort without coronary artery
  disease, four tortuosity/curvature metrics tested against CFD-derived TAWSS (tortuosity index,
  average absolute-curvature, RMS curvature, average squared-derivative-curvature): "The
  absolute-curvature showed a significant correlation to the percentage vessel area coverage of low
  TAWSS in the LMCA and LAD segments, and a similar trend for the LCx. In contrast, for the
  commonly used tortuosity index, no correlation is observed overall (p > 0.05)." —
  [Nature Scientific Reports, DOI 10.1038/s41598-022-04796-w](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-04796-w) (this exact wording is from a search-engine synthesis of the paper; full-text
  access was blocked by a login redirect on Nature.com and a CAPTCHA on the PMC mirror, and the
  ResearchGate copy returned HTTP 403, so this quote should be treated as abstract/search-synthesis
  confidence pending a full-text read).
- The same source's overall conclusion, per search synthesis: "average-absolute-curvature is
  recommended as a tortuosity measure" over the conventional tortuosity index, because it
  correlated significantly with low-TAWSS burden where the index did not.
- On generalizability across vessel segments: a separate search synthesis (source paper not
  individually identified/verified) states "there was a strong correlation between tortuosity and
  larger areas of low shear stress only in the left main coronary artery (LMCA) and left arterial
  descending (LAD) artery. However, for tortuosity in the left circumflex (LCx) artery, one study
  showed a weak correlation with low Time Averaged WSS (TAWSS) whereas another showed a strong
  negative correlation." This is an explicit **contradiction across studies for the LCx segment**
  that could not be resolved in this pass — flagged rather than silently picked.
- On robustness across idealized vs. real geometry: the Kashyap et al. abstract-level summary notes
  "an accurate representation of coronary tortuosity must account for all relevant geometric
  aspects, including curvature imposed by the heart shape" and that "differences between idealized
  and patient-specific results" exist, implying the curvature-WSS relationship measured in
  idealized bent-tube models does not fully generalize to real, heart-motion-constrained coronary
  geometry.
- Curved-tube CFD literature generally (search synthesis of several sources, not individually
  verified primary reads): "curved pipe flows generally exhibit larger values of wall shear stress
  along the outer wall due to larger velocity gradients resulting from the outwardly shifted
  velocity profile," and dynamic (time-varying, i.e. cardiac-cycle-driven) curvature further
  increases wall shear rate variation, with "low mean shear rates along the inner wall and very
  high shear rate variations along the outer wall" — [search synthesis citing curved coronary artery CFD studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1114/1.113); flagged as
  synthesis-level, not a verified direct quote from the primary paper.

### Inferences
- The relationship is directionally robust (more curvature -> lower inner-wall WSS is repeatedly
  reported) but its strength as a *quantitative, generalizable* predictor is metric-dependent: a
  physically grounded curvature magnitude (absolute or reciprocal-radius) metric correlates with
  low-WSS burden more consistently than a shape-based "tortuosity index," and even then the
  strength varies by coronary segment (robust in LMCA/LAD, inconsistent/contradictory in LCx across
  different studies).

### Gaps
- No study found in this pass reports a single, cross-study-consistent correlation coefficient
  (e.g., one Pearson r applicable across cohorts) for curvature vs. low-WSS extent; the picture is
  "significant in some segments, contradictory in others," which itself is the most defensible
  characterization of the current evidence, not a single robust number.
- Full-text verification of the Johnston 2007 and Kashyap 2022 papers was not achieved (paywalled,
  CAPTCHA-blocked, or 403); only abstract-level or search-synthesized text was obtained. A
  full-text read (e.g. via institutional access or paper-review skill) would be needed before
  citing exact r/p values with high confidence.

## Is peak/local curvature at a point, versus average/integrated curvature over an extended
region, the more mechanistically relevant predictor of low-WSS location? Any direct comparison
study?

### Takeaway
The one study found that directly compares multiple curvature-family metrics against a CFD outcome
(Kashyap et al. 2022, 127 patients) compares an *averaged* pointwise-curvature-magnitude metric
("average absolute-curvature") and two other averaged/aggregate curvature descriptors against a
*shape-based* tortuosity index — it does not isolate single-point peak curvature against
length-averaged curvature as a head-to-head test, so this specific question is not directly settled
by any source found in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- Kashyap et al. 2022 tested exactly four metrics: tortuosity index, average absolute-curvature,
  RMS curvature, and average squared-derivative-curvature, all against percentage area of low TAWSS,
  and found the average-absolute-curvature metric to be the best correlate and the tortuosity index
  to show no significant overall correlation (p > 0.05) — see quote and caveats in the previous
  section. — [search synthesis of Nature Scientific Reports 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-04796-w)
- Johnston & Johnston 2007's "weaker correlation between high curvature and extremes of WSS"
  (i.e., peak/extreme curvature values correlating only weakly with the most extreme WSS values)
  versus their "strong correlation between WSS and reciprocal radius" (a more continuously
  distributed geometric quantity) is suggestive that a single peak/extreme curvature value is a
  weaker predictor than a more distributed curvature-related quantity, but the source does not
  frame this explicitly as a peak-vs-average comparison — it is an inference on my part, not a
  direct claim in the source. — [IOPscience abstract](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0031-9155/52/9/013)

### Inferences
- None of the metrics found in this pass ("average absolute-curvature," "RMS curvature," "average
  squared-derivative-curvature") is a single-point peak-curvature metric; all four are computed
  over the vessel length in some averaged/aggregated form. This suggests the literature found here
  has already moved past "one point's curvature" toward length-averaged curvature descriptors as
  the practical unit of analysis, but no source explicitly argues that this is because peak
  curvature was tested and rejected as inferior — that comparison, if it exists, was not located.

### Gaps
- No study was found that explicitly and directly pits single-point peak curvature against
  length-averaged/integrated curvature as competing predictors of low-WSS location or extent in the
  same analysis. This is a genuine gap in the evidence surfaced by this research pass, not merely an
  access failure — the search results (varied phrasings tried) did not surface such a study.
- If this comparison is essential to the report's argument, note explicitly that it should be
  stated as an open question / gap rather than asserted either way.

## Is there coronary-artery-specific patient-cohort (not idealized bent-tube) literature directly
correlating a tortuosity/curvature index with CFD-derived low-WSS regions or plaque location, and
what was the reported correlation strength?

### Takeaway
Yes: the clearest patient-cohort example is Kashyap/Gharleghi et al. 2022 (127 CTCA patients
without coronary artery disease), which found a significant correlation between an
average-absolute-curvature metric and the percentage area of low TAWSS in the LMCA and LAD (similar
trend in LCx), while the conventional tortuosity index showed no significant overall correlation.
A second, only snippet-level-verified source describes a CFD study of coronary bifurcation
tortuosity based on in vivo dynamic CT, reporting a low/oscillatory WSS region forming at the inner
wall downstream of a bend when the (bifurcation) angle exceeds roughly 120 degrees.

### Cited Findings
- Kashyap, Gharleghi, et al., *Scientific Reports* 2022, DOI 10.1038/s41598-022-04796-w: 127
  patients, CTCA-derived geometry, CFD-modelled TAWSS; average-absolute-curvature significantly
  correlated with % low-TAWSS area in LMCA and LAD, similar trend in LCx; conventional tortuosity
  index not significantly correlated overall (p > 0.05). Recommended metric: average-absolute-
  curvature. — [Nature Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-04796-w) (search-synthesis confidence; full text blocked by
  login wall, CAPTCHA on PMC mirror at [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/), and HTTP 403 on ResearchGate mirror — could
  not independently verify exact r/p values or the paper's own stated correlation coefficients).
- A CFD steady-state study "based on in vivo dynamic CT measurements" of coronary bifurcation
  tortuosity as a potential local risk factor for atherosclerosis (identified via search only as
  PMC7574594, title: "Tortuosity of Coronary Bifurcation as a Potential Local Risk Factor for
  Atherosclerosis") — full text was blocked by a CAPTCHA wall in this pass and could not be read
  directly. Via search snippet only: "A low and oscillated wall shear stress (WSS) region was
  formed at the inner wall downstream of the bend section when the bend angle was larger than
  120°," and separately, "at peak systolic phase in simulated coronary artery models with different
  LAD-LCx angles, a big region of low wall shear stress magnitude is present at 120° angulation." —
  [PMC7574594](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7574594/) (snippet-level only; this appears to concern the LAD-LCx **bifurcation
  branch angle**, not a simple single-vessel bend, so the "120°" figure should be attributed to
  branch/bifurcation angle rather than generic bend curvature unless the full text confirms
  otherwise — this distinction could not be confirmed without full-text access).
- A distinct, only-title-level-identified paper, "Effects of Vessel Tortuosity on Coronary
  Hemodynamics: An Idealized and Patient-Specific Computational Study" (*Annals of Biomedical
  Engineering*, 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10439-015-1492-3), appears directly on-topic (idealized +
  patient-specific tortuosity/WSS) but access was blocked by a Springer login redirect in this pass;
  content could not be extracted or verified. — [Springer link (paywalled)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-015-1492-3)
- A related but distinct earlier finding, cited only via search synthesis and not verified against
  primary text: "in tortuous vessels, there is recirculation of flow, with higher velocities on the
  outer walls of the vessel bend... lower velocities (and WSS) were observed on the inner walls of
  bends, which correlates clinically with the location where plaque progression is likely to
  occur," attributed in the search summary to coronary-specific tortuosity/CFD literature generally
  rather than one identified paper.

### Inferences
- Of the patient-cohort evidence surfaced, the Kashyap et al. 2022 study (n=127) is the strongest
  candidate for the report to cite as "coronary-specific, patient-cohort, CFD-derived correlation
  between a curvature index and low-WSS burden," but the report should flag that its exact reported
  statistic (a p-value threshold, "p > 0.05" for the null result on tortuosity index, and an
  unspecified "significant correlation" for average-absolute-curvature) was obtained via search
  synthesis, not by this researcher reading the primary paper's results tables/figures directly.
  Given this is thesis-adjacent work, a full-text verification pass (paper-review skill) is
  recommended before this number is used as a load-bearing citation.

### Gaps
- Exact correlation coefficients (r values) and p-values for the Kashyap et al. 2022 study were not
  independently verified from primary text; only descriptive language ("significant correlation,"
  "no correlation... p > 0.05") was obtained via search synthesis after three separate full-text
  access attempts failed (Nature.com login wall, PMC CAPTCHA, ResearchGate 403).
- The PMC7574594 bifurcation-tortuosity CFD paper's exact tortuosity/curvature metric, sample
  size, and full mechanism description could not be extracted; only a search-snippet-level "120°"
  finding was obtained, and even that may concern bifurcation branch angle rather than simple bend
  curvature — this needs full-text confirmation before use.
- The 2015 Annals of Biomedical Engineering idealized + patient-specific tortuosity study was
  identified as on-topic but its content is entirely unverified (paywalled) in this pass.
- No coronary-specific plaque-location (as opposed to WSS-surrogate) correlation study with a
  curvature/tortuosity index was found and verified in this pass; the evidence located ties
  curvature to low-WSS area, which is one step removed from directly measured plaque location.
