# Torsion, Non-Planarity, Helical Flow, and Branching Topology in Relation to Wall Shear Stress

## KQ1: Does established literature (Caro, Stonebridge, related) argue that non-planar/torsional vessel geometry induces helical/swirling flow that reduces flow separation and low/oscillatory WSS, compared to an idealized planar curve of the same curvature? What is the mechanism and how strong is the evidence?

### Takeaway
There is a genuine, decades-old line of work (Caro and colleagues; Stonebridge and colleagues) arguing that the normal, non-planar/helical geometry of arteries generates swirling/helical flow that suppresses flow separation and stagnation, and that this is protective. The mechanistic story is well developed and has some idealized-CFD, in-vitro, and small patient-specific support, but much of the strongest "3D geometry directly lowers WSS extent" material only surfaced as search-engine synthesis rather than text I could verify against the primary paper, so it is flagged accordingly below.

### Cited Findings
- Caro CG and colleagues' seminal study "Non-Planar Curvature and Branching of Arteries and Non-Planar-Type Flow" (Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A, 1996, 452:185-197) examined human and rabbit aortic geometry via casts and used MRI in healthy human subjects; it reported that the aortic arch's curvature is approximately helical (non-planar) and that this non-planar geometry produces a swirling type of flow that improves clearance of material from the flow system — [ResearchGate record of the paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259069086_Non-Planar_Curvature_and_Branching_of_Arteries_and_Non-Planar-Type_Flow). This is search-engine-synthesized from the paper's abstract/record, not a directly fetched full text — flag as **abstract-level, not primary-verified**.
- A related SpringerLink book chapter, "Non-Planar Geometry and Non-Planar-Type Flow at Sites of Arterial Curvature and Branching: Implications for Arterial Biology and Disease" — [SpringerLink](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4757-9471-7_5) — extends the same Caro-associated argument that non-planar geometry and flow are implicated in arterial biology/disease; not fetched in full, listed at title level only.
- Stonebridge and colleagues are associated with the claim that swirling flow suppresses recirculation-zone formation and hinders separation/turbulence, producing a more favorable hemodynamic environment — this appears in aggregated web-search summaries citing "Stonebridge et al. (2004)" but I could not retrieve or verify the primary paper's text directly; **flag as search-summary level, unverified against primary source**.
- Engineering application of the same mechanism: "Fluid-Dynamic Optimal Design of Helical Vascular Graft for Stenotic Disturbed Flow" (PLOS ONE) explicitly designs helical/spiral graft conduits to induce swirling flow and reduce disturbed flow at stenotic anastomoses, i.e. treats induced helicity as a design tool against low/disturbed WSS — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0111047) / [PMC4215892](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4215892/). Title/abstract-level only, not fetched in full.
- In-vitro mechanistic support (non-arterial anatomical model, but directly testing the proposed mechanism): "Swirling flow created in a glass tube suppressed platelet adhesion to the surface of the tube," Thrombosis Research — [ScienceDirect](https://www.thrombosisresearch.com/article/S0049-3848(09)00120-0/fulltext) — an idealized in-vitro (not CFD, not animal, not patient) experiment supporting that swirling flow reduces a thrombogenic/atherogenic surface process. Title/abstract-level only.
- Experimental (PIV) support in a graft/anastomosis geometry: "Advantageous swirling flow in 45° end-to-side anastomosis," Experiments in Fluids — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00348-014-1861-y) — reports swirling flow as advantageous in an idealized anastomosis model; title/abstract-level only, not fetched in full.
- A search-engine synthesis (not a verified single primary source) claimed that "research examining the effect of arterial three-dimensionality shows that under physiological flow conditions, three-dimensionality can reduce both the range and extent of low wall shear stress regions and substantially increase oxygen flux through the walls," and that in non-planar anastomosis models there was "a marked reduction of peak wall shear stress at certain anastomosis sites and a greatly increased flux ... into occluded regions, with wall shear stress in the occluded region being higher with the non-planar model than the planar model." This tracks closely with "Oxygen mass transfer in a model three-dimensional artery" — [PMC2607427](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2607427/) — but I was not able to fetch and directly verify that full text; **flag as search-summary level, not independently confirmed against the primary paper**.
- Directly relevant to torsion specifically: Gallo et al. (see KQ4 below) report that vascular torsion is itself correlated with the degree of helical-flow feature intensity in human coronary arteries (r = 0.64, p < 0.001), providing a patient-specific-CFD-level (not merely idealized) link from geometric torsion → helicity. This is the strongest direct evidence tying the geometric quantity "torsion" (not just qualitative non-planarity) to a flow mechanism.

### Inferences
- The "non-planarity/torsion → helical flow → reduced separation/low-WSS" causal chain is coherent and has multiple independent lines of support (idealized CFD, in-vitro/PIV, and patient-specific correlation of torsion with helicity), but very little of what I found is a *direct, quantified, primary-source-verified* measurement of WSS-area-reduction attributable specifically to torsion (as opposed to helicity generally, which is downstream of torsion but also has other drivers such as pulsatility and inlet secondary flow).
- The Caro/Stonebridge tradition treats non-planarity as a design feature deliberately used in vascular graft/anastomosis engineering, which is itself indirect evidence the mechanism is taken seriously in applied biomechanics.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch full text for the Liu et al. review "Physiological Significance of Helical Flow in the Arterial System and its Potential Clinical Applications" (Ann Biomed Eng, 2015) — [Springer link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-014-1097-2) — due to an authentication redirect; this review would likely be the single best synthesis source and its absence is a real gap.
- Could not independently verify the exact Stonebridge primary citation (year/journal) behind the "swirling flow suppresses recirculation" claim; only search-engine synthesis was obtained.
- No idealized CFD study was found and verified that directly compares an artery reconstructed with vs. without torsion (holding curvature fixed) and reports a WSS-area or OSI number — the closest candidate (see KQ4) could not be fetched due to a paywall.

## KQ2: Is there literature suggesting torsion/non-planarity is instead associated with WORSE (more disturbed/lower/more oscillatory) WSS — i.e. is the directionality genuinely contested?

### Takeaway
Yes — the literature is genuinely mixed, and the clearest, most rigorously verified evidence (a 2025 patient-specific CFD study on 39 coronary trees) explicitly shows helical flow acts as a "double-edged sword": it reduces adversely low WSS in some regions but simultaneously increases adversely high WSS in others, so the single-directional "torsion/helicity is protective" narrative does not hold universally even within the same patient cohort.

### Cited Findings
- "High-intensity helical flow: a double-edged sword in coronary artery haemodynamics," Royal Society Open Science, 2025 (also posted as [arXiv:2502.06161](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06161)) — patient-specific CFD on **39 left coronary artery trees from the ASOCA dataset (20 non-stenosed, 19 stenosed)**. Findings (from search-engine synthesis of the abstract/PMC record, full text blocked by a bot-check page at [PMC12324882](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324882/)):
  - In stenosed cases, the vessel area exposed to adversely **low** time-averaged endothelial shear stress (TAESS < 0.5 Pa) was **reduced** (p = 0.0001) where helical flow intensity was higher — the "protective" direction.
  - At the same time, areas of adversely **high** TAESS (> 4.71 Pa) **increased** (p < 0.05), coinciding spatially with regions of high helical-flow intensity — high TAESS is linked in the same literature to increased plaque vulnerability and acute events, i.e. the "harmful" direction.
  - Explicit conclusion (as reported): "helical flow in coronaries is not always protective, as previously thought, because it not only mitigates low time-averaged endothelial shear stress ... but also exacerbates adversely high time-averaged endothelial shear stress linked to increased plaque vulnerability and even acute events."
  - **Verification status:** this is drawn from the abstract/PMC summary and search-engine synthesis of it; I could not open the full body text (blocked by a reCAPTCHA interstitial on both the NCBI mirror and PMC), so treat the exact p-values/thresholds as abstract-level, not full-text-verified, though they were consistently repeated across independent search snippets.
- "Breaking symmetry in non-planar bifurcations: distribution of flow and wall shear stress" (Lu Y, Lu X, Zhuang L, Wang W; Biorheology 2002;39(3-4):431-6) — idealized-geometry CFD (steady-state Newtonian, finite element solution of 3D Navier-Stokes in a hypothetical non-planar bifurcation) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12122263/), [SAGE](https://doi.org/10.1177/0006355x2002039003004021). Reported finding: **non-planarity in one of the two daughter vessels deflects flow from the inner wall to the outer wall and changes the WSS distribution**; the paper frames this as supporting the view that "non-planarity in blood vessels is a factor with important haemodynamic significance and may play a key role in vascular biology and pathophysiology" — a neutral-to-cautionary framing, not an explicit "protective" claim, and it does not state that the change in WSS distribution is beneficial. **Verification status:** could not fetch primary abstract text directly (PubMed reCAPTCHA-blocked); relayed via search-engine synthesis of the title/abstract, so treat as abstract-level.
- By contrast, the Gallo et al. atheroprotective-helicity paper (see KQ1/KQ4) reports a strong *negative* correlation between "unfavorable WSS" and helicity intensity (r = -0.91, p < 0.001) — i.e. the opposite conclusion (more helicity → less unfavorable WSS), in patient-specific coronary CFD.

### Inferences
- Taken together, the 2025 ASOCA-based "double-edged sword" study and the earlier Gallo et al. correlational study are not strictly contradictory (one measures area of low WSS, the other an aggregate "unfavorable WSS" score that may weight low-WSS more heavily), but they do show that framing helicity/torsion as unconditionally protective is an oversimplification once high-WSS-driven plaque vulnerability is also considered. A report should state the directionality question as **contested/context-dependent**, not settled, with the most recent and most rigorous patient-specific evidence (39-case ASOCA CFD) explicitly rejecting the simple protective-only story.
- The Lu et al. non-planar bifurcation study is best read as establishing that non-planarity has a *large effect* on WSS distribution (redistributing high/low WSS location), without itself asserting a protective or harmful net direction — it is evidence for "non-planarity matters mechanistically" more than for either directional claim.

### Gaps
- I could not obtain the full text of either the Lu et al. 2002 paper or the 2025 ASOCA double-edged-sword paper, only search-engine-synthesized abstract content; a report-writer citing exact p-values/thresholds from the latter should note they are abstract-level.
- No animal-model study directly testing torsion (as opposed to helicity/swirl generally) as harmful was found in this search pass.

## KQ3: What is known about WSS at arterial bifurcations/branch points independent of parent-vessel curvature or torsion — classic flow-divider vs. outer/lateral-wall pattern — and is bifurcation angle itself quantitatively linked to the severity/extent of low-WSS zones?

### Takeaway
The classic pattern is well established and directly verified from a primary review: the flow-divider (carina) apex experiences high endothelial shear stress while the lateral/outer walls of the main vessel and branches experience low, oscillatory shear stress and correspondingly higher plaque prevalence. Bifurcation angle is quantitatively linked to this pattern in multiple independent studies, but the direction of the angle→WSS relationship differs between idealized/healthy-geometry studies (wider angle → lower WSS, higher OSI) and patient-specific stenosed-vessel studies (wider angle → higher WSS specifically at the stenotic site) — a heterogeneity that should be flagged rather than collapsed into one rule.

### Cited Findings
- **Directly fetched and verified**, EuroIntervention review "Impact of local flow haemodynamics on atherosclerosis in coronary artery bifurcations" (2015) — [link](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/impact-of-local-flow-haemodynamics-on-atherosclerosis-in-coronary-artery-bifurcations):
  - "Along the lateral walls of the main vessel and side branches, a distinct flow pattern is observed with local low and oscillatory ESS, while high ESS develops at the flow divider (carina)."
  - "Early intimal thickening occurs in areas of low and oscillatory ESS within coronary bifurcations ... the lateral walls (low ESS areas) have a significantly higher prevalence of plaque compared to the flow divider regions."
  - Mechanism stated: "Arterial bifurcations are known for their distinct flow velocity patterns resulting in flow separation, recirculation and secondary flow patterns leading to local low and oscillatory ESS along the lateral walls."
  - Quantitative angle relationship stated directly: **"The bifurcation angle and diameter influence the local ESS patterns, i.e., the higher the angle and diameter the lower the ESS."** This is a review-level synthesis of underlying computational/in-vitro studies, not a single new primary measurement, but it is directly verified text from the review itself.
- **Directly fetched and verified**, Sun et al., "An investigation of correlation between left coronary bifurcation angle and hemodynamic changes in coronary stenosis by coronary computed tomography angiography-derived computational fluid dynamics" — [PMC5682403](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5682403/). Patient-specific CCTA-derived CFD, **30 patients** (22 male, mean age 59±6.9y) with calcified LCA plaques:
  - Bifurcation angle threshold of **>80°** best predicted significant stenosis (sensitivity 100%, specificity 78.6% by CCTA/73.3% by ICA, PPV 84.2%/78.9%, NPV 100%).
  - "Wall shear stress was noted to increase in the LAD and LCx models with significant stenosis and wider angulation (>80º), but demonstrated little or no change in most of the coronary models with no significant stenosis and narrower angulation (<80º)."
  - Mean bifurcation angle 83.9±13.6° (CCTA) vs 83.8±13.3° (ICA), not significantly different (p=0.98).
  - Wider angulation also associated with greater pressure drop and increased flow velocity at stenotic sites, with recirculation immediately downstream of plaque promoting further plaque deposition.
- **Directly fetched and verified**, "Prediction of time averaged wall shear stress distribution in coronary arteries' bifurcation varying in morphological features via deep learning" — [PMC11920710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11920710/). Uses **1,800 idealized bifurcation geometries** (varying LM/LAD/LCx diameters and the LM-LAD angle γ and LAD-LCx angle α) with CFD-trained deep-learning surrogate. Key finding, quoted from the paper's own citation of the relevant relationship: **"a wider bifurcation angle is associated with lower wall shear stress and higher oscillatory shear index (OSI), which can contribute to the stimulation of atherosclerosis."** Figure 7 in the paper shows TAWSS increasing with the α angle in their idealized parametric sweep (note: read the direction here as internally consistent with "wider angle associated with lower WSS" as the paper's stated interpretive claim; the deep learning model captured overall trends better than point-level values).
- **Directly fetched and verified**, "Vortical Structures Promote Atheroprotective Wall Shear Stress Distributions in a Carotid Artery Bifurcation Model" — [PMC10525770](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10525770/). Idealized-geometry 3D pulsatile CFD comparing a "healthy" averaged carotid bifurcation model against a "pre-disposed" geometry with an increased ICA angle (45°) and reduced ICA/CCA diameter ratio (0.5):
  - A dominant hairpin vortical structure forms in the internal carotid sinus; "the position of this high-WSS band aligns with the internal position of the main hairpin vortical structure," i.e. the vortex actively raises WSS in what would otherwise be a low-WSS separation zone.
  - In the healthy geometry the hairpin vortex persists through most of the cardiac cycle; in the pre-disposed (altered angle/diameter) geometry it deteriorates prematurely, producing proatherogenic WSS distributions.
  - This directly ties a **geometric change localized at the bifurcation itself** (angle, diameter ratio) — independent of any upstream parent-vessel curvature/torsion — to a mechanistically explained WSS outcome via vortex stability.

### Inferences
- The flow-divider-high/lateral-wall-low WSS pattern at bifurcations is not contested — it is the textbook finding, verified here directly from a review source, and consistent with the classic carotid-bifurcation atherosclerosis literature (e.g. the well-known low-oscillating-shear/plaque-location correlation reported by Ku, Zarins et al., which appeared in this search as ["Pulsatile flow and atherosclerosis in the human carotid bifurcation," PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3994585/) but was not independently fetched/verified here).
- The directionality of the *angle*-WSS relationship is where real heterogeneity appears: idealized/healthy-geometry studies (Frontiers deep-learning paper on 1,800 idealized geometries; carotid vortical-structure study) find **wider angle → lower/worse WSS**, consistent with a straightforward "wider angle promotes more flow separation" mechanism. The one patient-specific stenosed-vessel study (Sun et al., 30 patients) finds **wider angle → higher WSS specifically at the stenotic lesion**, which is plausibly explained by the stenosis-induced jet acceleration dominating local WSS in already-diseased vessels rather than contradicting the idealized-geometry mechanism for healthy/pre-disease vessels. A report should present both findings and flag that they measure different things (pre-disease geometric predisposition vs. WSS at an already-formed stenotic lesion) rather than treating them as a flat contradiction.
- Bifurcation angle is therefore a genuinely evidenced, quantitative geometric predictor of both plaque/stenosis risk and directly-simulated WSS/OSI, independent of any parent-vessel torsion — this is a stronger and more consistently-replicated body of evidence than the torsion/non-planarity literature in KQ1/KQ2.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch full text of the systematic review "Evaluation of the Relationship between Left Coronary Artery Bifurcation Angle and Coronary Artery Disease" ([PMC9457427](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9457427/)) — blocked by a bot-check interstitial on repeated attempts — which would have given a pooled/heterogeneity view across many studies rather than the two individual studies above.
- Could not verify the classic Ku/Zarins carotid bifurcation plaque-location/oscillatory-shear paper beyond its title in search results.
- Did not find a coronary-specific (as opposed to carotid-specific) idealized vortical-structure mechanism paper analogous to PMC10525770; the carotid finding is the best-verified mechanistic bifurcation-vortex evidence found, and its coronary-specific generalizability was not independently confirmed here (a separate coronary-tree literature scan may be warranted if this matters for the thesis's coronary-only scope).

## KQ4: Has any study directly used a torsion metric, a non-planarity/out-of-plane-angle metric, or a bifurcation-angle/branching-topology metric computed from a vessel/tree centerline as a predictor of measured or simulated WSS or of plaque location? What was found?

### Takeaway
Yes for all three metric types, with varying strength of evidence: bifurcation angle has the most directly verified, quantitative predictor role (two fetched primary studies, one idealized parametric with 1,800 geometries, one patient-specific with 30 patients); a geometric torsion metric has documented use as a predictor of helical-flow intensity in patient-specific coronary CFD (Gallo et al.), which itself predicts WSS; and a direct torsion-vs-WSS "torsion-free projected centerline" methodology exists in the literature but could not be verified past a paywalled abstract in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- **Bifurcation angle → WSS (idealized, verified):** the Frontiers/PMC11920710 deep-learning study used the LM-LAD angle (γ) and LAD-LCx angle (α), computed as part of an idealized bifurcation's centerline/branch geometry, as explicit input features to predict time-averaged WSS distribution across 1,800 generated geometries, finding wider α associated with lower TAWSS and higher OSI (see KQ3 for full quote).
- **Bifurcation angle → stenosis/WSS (patient-specific, verified):** Sun et al. ([PMC5682403](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5682403/)) used CCTA-measured bifurcation angle as a predictor of both CFD-derived WSS and of significant stenosis itself, in 30 patients, with the >80° cutoff yielding 100% sensitivity for significant stenosis (see KQ3 for full numbers).
- **Bifurcation angle → CAD, narrower systematic-review corroboration (title-level only):** "Association between the bifurcation angle and the angle of the origin of the left anterior descending with the severity of coronary artery disease" — [PMC13069120](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13069120/) — title suggests a further direct angle-severity link; not fetched/verified in this pass, listed only as corroborating evidence that this is an active, multiply-studied predictor relationship.
- **Torsion → helicity → WSS (patient-specific, partially verified via search synthesis):** Gallo D. et al., "The Atheroprotective Nature of Helical Flow in Coronary Arteries" (Ann Biomed Eng, 2018/2019) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-018-02169-x), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30488307/). Reported (via search-engine synthesis of the abstract, full text blocked by an institutional-login redirect):
  - Coronary hemodynamics is characterized by counter-rotating bi-helical flow structures.
  - **Vascular torsion is correlated with helical-flow feature intensity: r = 0.64, p < 0.001** — i.e. torsion, computed presumably from the reconstructed coronary centerline geometry, is reported as a direct geometric determinant of the degree of helical flow.
  - Helical-flow intensity itself was strongly inversely correlated with "unfavorable WSS conditions" (r = -0.91, p < 0.001) and strongly positively associated with WSS magnitude (p < 0.001).
  - Coronary segments with high baseline helical-flow intensity showed significantly lower subsequent wall-thickness growth (a plaque-progression proxy) than segments with mid or low helical-flow intensity — i.e. a longitudinal/prognostic link from the geometry-derived flow feature to a plaque outcome.
  - **Verification status:** this chain (torsion → helicity → WSS → plaque progression) is exactly the kind of direct evidence the key question asks for, but I obtained it only via search-engine synthesis of the abstract/PubMed record; I was not able to open the full Springer text (redirected to an institutional-login page) to confirm sample size, imaging modality, or the exact torsion computation method. **Flag clearly as abstract/search-summary level, not full-text-verified**, when this is cited in the report.
- **Torsion-free projected-centerline methodology (unverified, paywalled):** "The influence of blood velocity and vessel geometric parameters on wall shear stress" — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1350453324000146) — a search snippet describes the paper's method as explicitly generating a "torsion-free" model by projecting the original vessel centerline onto its averaged plane of curvature, then comparing WSS distributions between the original (torsion-present) and projected (torsion-free) geometries to isolate torsion's causal effect — precisely the controlled-comparison design the key question is looking for. A further snippet states "in helical centerline cases, wall shear stress rises with distance as swirl flow develops, contrasting with straight vessel cases," suggesting the paper's finding is that torsion/helicity increases WSS with downstream distance. **I could not fetch the full text (HTTP 403 Forbidden) and could not confirm the vessel/dataset used (idealized, animal, or patient-specific), sample size, or the precise magnitude/direction of the reported effect.** This should be listed as a promising lead for a report-writer or later researcher with journal access, not as a confirmed finding.
- A tangential, likely-irrelevant hit: "Torque and Shear Stress Distributions in Arterial Wall with Torsion Around the Vessel Axis" (Journal of Elasticity, 2025) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10659-025-10162-4) — appears from its title to concern solid-mechanics torque/shear within the arterial *wall* material under an applied torsional load, not fluid-dynamic WSS from blood flow past a torsional centerline. Flagged as a different physical quantity (structural mechanics vs. hemodynamic WSS) and likely not usable as evidence for this report's question; not fetched in full.

### Inferences
- Among the three candidate metric types, **bifurcation angle** has by far the most directly verified, quantitatively specific, and multiply-replicated evidentiary base as a predictor of WSS/plaque outcomes (two independently fetched primary studies with concrete numbers, plus corroborating titles). **Torsion** has one strong-looking but only search-summary-verified patient-specific result (Gallo et al., r = 0.64 and r = -0.91) and one promising but paywalled methodology paper (torsion-free projected centerline) that could not be confirmed. **Non-planarity/out-of-plane angle** as a named, standalone metric (distinct from torsion) was not found used as a direct WSS/plaque predictor in this pass — the closest is the qualitative "non-planar vs planar" comparison in Lu et al. 2002 and the idealized non-planar-bifurcation CFD literature, which establishes that non-planarity changes WSS distribution but was not found expressed as a scalar out-of-plane-angle metric feeding a predictive/statistical model.
- If the report needs to rank these three candidate "advanced" geometric descriptors by strength of evidenced WSS linkage, based on this research pass the order is: bifurcation angle (strongest, most direct, most replicated) > torsion (real but currently resting on one abstract-level correlational study plus one unverified paywalled paper) > standalone non-planarity/out-of-plane-angle scalar (weakest — evidenced qualitatively but not as a specific predictive metric in what I could find).
