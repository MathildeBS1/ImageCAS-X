# LCx Feasibility: Torsion, Bifurcation Angle, Diameter, and Topological Descriptors for CAD Prediction

## Anatomical branch density along the LCx (obtuse marginals, atrial branches, posterolateral branches) vs the RCA

### Takeaway
The LCx carries a moderate, intermediate number of named branch points, more than the RCA's few named landmarks but far fewer, and less consistently present, than the LAD's diagonal series; bifurcation angle would be sampled at a handful of discrete locations (1-3 obtuse marginals, plus variable atrial/SA-nodal and posterolateral branches) rather than densely along the whole length, so it sits between the RCA "sparse" case and a hypothetically dense case, not equivalent to it.

### Cited Findings
- The LCx runs in the left atrioventricular (coronary) groove and gives off "one to three obtuse marginal branches (OM1, OM2, OM3), with OM1 being the most proximal and typically the largest" — [Left marginal branch / OM anatomy summary](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12236989/)
- Besides the OM branches, the circumflex "also gives off left atrial branches and posterolateral branches, with some individuals having up to three lateral branches called obtuse marginal branches" — [Circumflex artery anatomy summary](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/anatomy/circumflex-artery)
- The sinoatrial (SA) nodal artery arises from the proximal LCx rather than the RCA in roughly 27-42% of individuals (one source cites 38%), and when it does, it can follow a distinctive "S-shaped" course in ~14-18% of those cases — [Anatomical Variations in the Sinoatrial Nodal Artery meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0148331); [MDCT of the S-Shaped Sinoatrial Node Artery](https://www.ajronline.org/doi/10.2214/AJR.07.3127)
- A cadaveric anomaly report notes normal variants can include "absence of the left marginal artery," underscoring that even the OM branch is not a guaranteed constant landmark — [Trifurcation of the RCA with absence of the left marginal artery](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12887354/)
- A large CT angiography anomaly-prevalence study (2,697 patients, 64-detector CT) found the obtuse marginal branch was involved in anomalies in only 0.03% of cases, including one case of an absent OM artery (0.03%) — [Prevalence of Congenital Coronary Artery Anomalies, 64-detector CT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3522371/)
- A dedicated search for a study directly enumerating "typical number of obtuse marginal branches" per patient across a cohort did not surface one; available sources describe the range (1-3) qualitatively rather than reporting a population distribution — (search conducted, no primary distributional study found)

### Inferences
- Named branch points along the LCx cluster into three anatomically distinct types: OM branches (main lateral supply, 1-3 per patient), atrial branches (including the SA-nodal branch when circumflex-origin, present in roughly a third of patients), and posterolateral branches (present variably, more consistently in left-dominant/codominant hearts, see next section). This gives the LCx more candidate angle-sampling points than the RCA (which the prior RCA research characterized as anatomically sparse), but the total is still a handful of discrete locations along the vessel's length, not a continuous or densely repeating branch pattern like the LAD's diagonals/septals.
- Because OM branch count itself varies (1 to 3, sometimes absent), a per-point bifurcation-angle feature for the LCx would be present at a variable number of locations per patient, an inherent between-subject inconsistency that any model architecture (or feature-imputation scheme) would need to handle, similar in kind to the RCA sparsity problem but higher in average branch count.

### Gaps
- No primary study was found that reports a population-level distribution (mean, mode, range with frequencies) of the number of obtuse marginal branches per patient on CTCA; anatomy sources describe the range (1-3) without base rates.
- No source was found that reports a per-branch-type prevalence table analogous to what might exist for RCA branches (e.g., conus branch, acute marginal branches), making a rigorous side-by-side branch-count comparison with the RCA’s "few named branch points" claim only approximate.

## Effect of coronary dominance on LCx length, termination, and branching pattern (confound analogous to RCA)

### Takeaway
Coronary dominance materially changes the LCx's length, terminal point, and branch complement: in left-dominant and codominant hearts the LCx extends further (reaching or crossing the crux and giving off the posterior descending and posterolateral branches), while in right-dominant hearts (the majority, ~70%) it is shorter and terminates before or at the obtuse margin; this is a direct anatomical/geometric confound for any length- or branch-count-based LCx feature, analogous to (and arguably larger in magnitude than) the RCA-dominance confound already identified, because right-dominance is more common than left-dominance so the LCx feature distribution is more often "truncated" than "extended."

### Cited Findings
- "The circumflex artery manifests the greatest variability in terms of length and distribution. In most cases, the circumflex artery terminates between the left margin and the crux of the heart. However, in about 20-30% of cases, it terminates before reaching the left margin. In left-dominant circulation, it reaches the crux of the heart or even goes beyond it." — [Circumflex artery anatomy summary](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/anatomy/circumflex-artery)
- At the crux cordis specifically: "the RCA crossed the crux in 70% and the LCx reached the crux in 20%; both vessels reached the crux in 10%" — [Morphological Study of Coronary Artery Dominance Pattern in Adult Human Hearts](https://healthcare-bulletin.co.uk/article/morphological-study-of-the-coronary-artery-dominance-pattern-in-adult-human-hearts-4758/) (same study reports right dominance 70%, left dominance 20%, codominance 10%, a notably higher left-dominance estimate than other sources)
- Prevalence estimates for left dominance vary by source: "the heart is left dominant (7.5%)" by one convention, vs. "approximately 1 in 10 people" (~10%) by another, vs. 20% in the morphological study above — [Coronary dominance and LCx overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex_branch_of_left_coronary_artery); the spread itself indicates dominance-classification criteria are not fully standardized across studies
- In left-dominant circulation, the LCx "gives off the posterior interventricular branch to supply the diaphragmatic surface of the myocardium," i.e., it takes over the PDA territory normally supplied by the RCA — [Coronary dominance and LCx overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex_branch_of_left_coronary_artery)
- A JAHA review titled "Clinical Significance of Coronary Arterial Dominance: A Review of the Literature" exists and is the most likely comprehensive synthesis of dominance-driven anatomical and outcome differences, but its full text could not be retrieved (HTTP 403 on ScienceDirect/AHA site, reCAPTCHA block on the PubMed abstract mirror) despite two access attempts — [Clinical Significance of Coronary Arterial Dominance](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.123.032851) [access blocked, not verified beyond title/search snippet]

### Inferences
- Because right dominance (~70%) is markedly more common than left dominance (~10-20% depending on source) or codominance (~10%), the LCx-length confound is asymmetric: most subjects will have a "short, OM-terminating" LCx, and a dominance-dependent minority will have a substantially longer LCx reaching the crux and carrying PDA-territory branches. This is structurally the same type of confound flagged for the RCA (where dominance changes whether the RCA carries the PDA), but here it manifests as added length and additional distal branch points rather than (as for the RCA) as the presence/absence of the single dominant distal continuation, so an LCx model would need dominance as a covariate or the sampled profile length would encode dominance status implicitly, likely more strongly than a raw RCA profile length does, since the RCA's proximal/mid anatomy is comparatively dominance-invariant while the LCx's whole distal one-third to one-half of the profile is dominance-dependent.
- The disagreement between the ~7.5%, ~10%, and ~20% left-dominance estimates across sources suggests the ImageCAS-X cohort's own dominance labelling (if available via Descriptors.xlsx per the project's CLAUDE.md) should be used directly rather than assuming a textbook prevalence, since study-to-study variation this large likely reflects differing classification thresholds (e.g., whether "codominant" is a separate category) rather than true population differences.

### Gaps
- The JAHA dominance review (the most directly relevant single source for this question) could not be accessed in full; its specific quantitative claims about LCx length/branch-count differences by dominance category are therefore not verified here beyond the anatomy-textbook-level sources above.
- No source quantifies the average length difference (in mm, or as % of total path length) between a right-dominant LCx and a left-dominant/codominant LCx profile; the qualitative "reaches/crosses the crux" description was the most specific finding available.
- No source reports how commonly a left-dominant LCx's added PDA-territory segment introduces additional bifurcation points beyond the already-noted posterior interventricular/posterolateral branches (i.e., whether the added length is itself branch-point-dense).

## LCx-specific tortuosity/curvature/torsion studies and association with CAD, stenosis, or plaque burden

### Takeaway
The LCx is the coronary vessel most frequently reported as tortuous across at least two independent invasive-angiography cohorts, but the two available LCx-specific tortuosity studies give directly conflicting conclusions about its relationship to atherosclerotic burden (one finds tortuous LCx segments have *less* significant stenosis, the other finds *no* significant association), and no LCx-specific curvature/torsion (Frenet-frame) study on CT centerlines analogous to the RCA machine-learning tortuosity work was found; the RCA-side conclusion that torsion is "noise-sensitive with no coronary outcome evidence" appears to extend to the LCx by absence of counter-evidence, but the LCx additionally carries actively conflicting tortuosity-outcome findings that the RCA literature does not.

### Cited Findings
- In a cohort of 737 patients, the LCx showed the highest tortuosity burden of the three major vessels: "54.1% had no tortuosity, 23.6% mild, 11.3% moderate, and 11% severe," and among vessels with "significant coronary tortuosity" (SCT, defined as ≥3 consecutive 90-180° curvatures or ≥2 consecutive ≥180° curvatures in a vessel ≥2mm diameter), the LCx-SCT group had *lower* rates of ≥50% stenosis than non-SCT LCx (17.7% vs 31.1%, P=0.001) and lower Gensini scores (2.1±3.4 vs 5.2±9.5, P=0.01), while also showing slower flow (TIMI frame count 17.1±4.4 vs 12.7±4.4, P<0.001) — [The significant coronary tortuosity and atherosclerotic CAD; what is the relation?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6335987/)
- In a separate cohort of 1,010 patients (mean age 64±11, 544 male, 2-4 year follow-up), tortuosity (defined as ≥3 bends of ≥45° change in direction, present in both systole and diastole) had the highest incidence in the LCx (26.9%), more than LAD (21.1%) and far more than RCA (1.4%); this study found "no significant difference between LCX(RCA) tortuosity and LCX(RCA) atherosclerosis," in contrast to the LAD, where tortuosity was negatively associated with atherosclerosis — [PLOS ONE: Clinical Implication of Coronary Tortuosity in Patients with CAD](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0024232)
- A third source (different tortuosity definition again) reports vessel-level tortuosity absence rates of "LAD 70.6% none, LCX 54.1% none, RCA 82.6% none," and significant tortuosity most common in the circumflex (34.0%), then LAD (14.4%), then RCA (2.8%); this source also reports LCx foreshortening/masking of proximal stenosis on standard angiographic views in tortuous/angulated LCx cases, mitigated by AP-caudal or LAO-caudal projections — [search-aggregated result, consistent with the PMC6335987 study above but possibly a different cohort/definition; not independently re-verified as a distinct paper, cross-check recommended]
- A CT-derived tortuosity/curvature machine-learning approach exists that computes Frenet curvature (bending) and Frenet torsion (non-planarity) directly from 3D centerline coordinates, but the described implementations (skeletonized U-Net segmentation, 38,691-angiogram cohort) are explicitly built and validated on the right coronary artery, not the LCx — [A Machine Learning Driven Approach to Quantifying Coronary Artery Tortuosity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)

### Inferences
- The two LCx-specific stenosis-association findings directly conflict (protective/negative association in the 737-patient study vs. null association in the 1,010-patient study), which is a materially different evidentiary situation than the RCA's "no outcome evidence" gap: for the RCA there is simply an absence of data, whereas for the LCx there is present but contradictory data. A curvature-based LCx feature therefore cannot currently be justified by a clean literature consensus either for or against association with disease; if anything, the balance of two studies leans toward "no positive association with stenosis severity," which argues against using raw curvature/tortuosity as a strong stand-alone predictive channel for LCx CAD status, though it could still carry information jointly with other features (flow-rate proxies, since both studies found slower flow in tortuous segments).
- Since the LCx is consistently reported as the single most tortuous major coronary vessel across all three tortuosity studies found (in contrast to RCA, the least tortuous in two of the three), a curvature/torsion feature channel would have the most "signal" (numerically) on the LCx, even though what that signal means clinically is unresolved. This raises a specific risk for the CAD-prediction network: a naive curvature channel could function as an implicit "which vessel is this" indicator (since LCx segments are geometrically distinguishable from RCA/LAD by curvature magnitude alone) rather than a genuine disease marker, and this risk is more acute for LCx than it apparently was for RCA.
- No torsion-specific (as opposed to planar curvature/tortuosity) LCx literature was found at all; the existing torsion critique from the RCA research (noise-sensitivity, no coronary outcome evidence) transfers to the LCx without modification, and if anything is reinforced, since even simpler planar-tortuosity metrics on the LCx already show unresolved/conflicting outcome associations.

### Gaps
- No study computing 3D Frenet torsion (non-planarity) specifically on LCx centerlines, on CT or otherwise, was found; all torsion-adjacent methodology found is RCA-specific.
- The third tortuosity-prevalence source (34.0%/14.4%/2.8% by vessel) could not be traced to a distinct, independently verifiable primary article within the tool budget; it should be treated as unverified/possibly duplicative of the PMC6335987 findings rather than a fully independent third data point.
- No study was found that stratifies LCx tortuosity-outcome association by coronary dominance, despite the dominance-driven length/branch differences documented above; whether the "no association" or "protective association" findings hold within right-dominant vs. left-dominant/codominant LCx subgroups is unknown.
- No study quantifying plaque burden (as opposed to binary stenosis ≥50%) specifically against LCx tortuosity/curvature was found beyond the Gensini-score result already cited.

## LCx luminal diameter: tapering, typical range, dominance-related variability, and use as predictor/normalizer

### Takeaway
LCx proximal diameter is reported in the 3.4-4.2 mm range (smaller than a typical RCA/LAD proximal caliber), and vessel morphology parameters built on LCx diameter and length (lumen volume, V/L ratio, vessel length, bifurcation angle) have documented, quantified associations with CT-derived FFR specifically in the LCx, giving the LCx comparable (if less extensively replicated) diameter-based outcome evidence to the RCA's FFR-CT study; dominance-related diameter variability specifically for the LCx (i.e., whether a dominant LCx is measurably wider) was not directly evidenced in the sources found, which is a gap.

### Cited Findings
- "For the left circumflex, proximal diameter varied between 3.4±0.5 and 4.2±0.6 mm" — [Lumen Diameter of Normal Human Coronary Arteries](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/01.CIR.86.1.232)
- In a CT-FFR vessel-morphology study, "the change in CT-derived FFR (ΔFFRCT) significantly correlated with bifurcation angle and vessel length in the left circumflex artery (LCX angle, r = 0.26, p = 0.02; LCX vessel length, r = 0.49, p < 0.0001)," and separately, for the LCx, "non-calcified plaque volume (threshold > 130 mm³) was identified as an independent predictor of positive FFR at multivariable analysis" — [search-aggregated result from CT-FFR vessel morphology literature, primary article not individually pinned down; treat as moderately-sourced, recommend direct verification against the original PMC9889442 (bifurcation angle and CT-FFR) or the RCA-analogous vessel-morphology paper before citing a specific r-value in the thesis]
- The RCA-specific analogous study ("Impact of vessel morphology on CT-derived fractional-flow-reserve in non-obstructive CAD in right coronary artery") exists as the RCA benchmark the current research already relies on; a directly parallel, single dedicated LCx-only version of this exact study design was not found, though LCx cases and LCx-specific coefficients do appear within multi-vessel CT-FFR vessel-morphology cohorts (as in the bifurcation-angle/vessel-length finding above) — [Impact of vessel morphology on CT-derived FFR in RCA](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10873436/)
- A bicentric outcome study on LCx/obtuse-marginal physiology (432 patients) found μFR (an angiography-derived flow-ratio surrogate) correlates well with invasive FFR in the LCx/OM territory (R=0.782, p<0.001), but only 5.1% of intermediate LCx/OM lesions were FFR-positive, and revascularization of physiologically non-significant LCx/OM lesions did not improve outcomes — this is a functional/flow study rather than a pure-diameter study, but establishes that LCx/OM lesions are disproportionately physiologically non-significant relative to their anatomic appearance, a relevant caveat for interpreting any diameter-based severity proxy in the LCx — [Physiological assessment of LCx, bicentric μFR outcome study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41242207/)
- General FFR-CT tapering literature (not LCx-specific) notes that "FFR-CT in the distal right coronary artery was lower than invasive FFR measurements, most likely owing to increased resistance of a long, tapering vessel," illustrating that vessel length/tapering interacts with any diameter-based feature's meaning; no equivalent LCx-specific tapering-discrepancy statement was found — [FFR-CT vessel morphology overview](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00330-023-09972-8)

### Inferences
- Diameter-as-outcome-predictor evidence for the LCx is present but more indirect and dispersed than the RCA's single dedicated FFR-CT vessel-morphology study: the LCx evidence comes from (a) a basic anatomic-diameter reference range, (b) vessel-morphology correlates (length, bifurcation angle, plaque volume) within CT-FFR studies that include LCx cases and report an LCx-specific coefficient, and (c) a physiology/outcome study on LCx/OM lesions using a flow surrogate rather than raw diameter. Taken together, this is enough to say diameter/vessel-morphology has documented outcome relevance for the LCx, similar in kind to the RCA finding, but the reporting is less clean, and one specific numeric claim (the r=0.26/r=0.49 correlations) could not be pinned to a single primary source with full confidence within the tool budget, so it should be re-verified before being used as a load-bearing citation.
- The μFR/LCx-OM outcome study's finding that most anatomically intermediate LCx/OM lesions are physiologically insignificant argues for caution in using anatomic diameter alone (without an accompanying physiology/flow signal) as an LCx severity proxy; this nuance was not present in the RCA diameter discussion and may be worth flagging as an LCx-specific caveat.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares LCx diameter between right-dominant and left-dominant/codominant hearts; dominance-related diameter variability for the LCx specifically (as distinct from the well-evidenced length/branch differences above) is unverified.
- The exact primary source and full methodology behind the ΔFFRCT/bifurcation-angle/vessel-length LCx correlation coefficients could not be conclusively isolated from the RCA-focused and general vessel-morphology search results; this should be re-checked with a targeted follow-up search (e.g., against PMC9889442 directly) before use.
- No LCx-specific single-study equivalent of the RCA's dedicated "Impact of vessel morphology on CT-derived FFR ... in non-obstructive CAD in right coronary artery" paper (i.e., one paper, one vessel, purpose-built) was found; the LCx evidence is assembled from multi-vessel studies rather than an LCx-dedicated design.

## Is the LCx under-studied relative to LAD/RCA in coronary geometry/tortuosity literature?

### Takeaway
There is a documented and explicit evidence gap for the LCx in the closest analogous imaging-difficulty literature found (transthoracic echocardiography, where the LCx is explicitly stated to lack normative nomogram data because of poor acoustic-window visibility), and indirect signals (foreshortening on standard invasive-angiography projections, the disease-specific Kawasaki-CTA paper's explicit statement that LCx literature "remains limited" relative to LM/LAD/RCA) support that the LCx is comparatively under-characterized; however, no source was found making this claim specifically and quantitatively for CT-derived centerline/tortuosity/geometry research (as opposed to echocardiography or general angiographic difficulty), so the CT-specific version of this gap claim is inferred rather than directly evidenced.

### Cited Findings
- "Existing literature on coronary artery lesions in Kawasaki disease has mainly focused on involvement of the left main, left anterior descending, and right coronary arteries, with information about the left circumflex coronary artery remaining limited," explicitly naming the LCx as the most under-studied of the four major named coronary segments in that disease-specific literature — [The value of CT coronary angiography for comprehensive assessment of LCx in Kawasaki disease](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lansea/article/PIIS2772-3682(24)00121-5/fulltext)
- "Assessment of the left circumflex coronary artery is difficult on transthoracic echocardiography due to its anatomy and limited accessibility to an appropriate acoustic window, and due to these reasons, normative data for the left circumflex have not been given in most published nomograms" — [same source](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lansea/article/PIIS2772-3682(24)00121-5/fulltext)
- On invasive angiography specifically (not echo, not CT), tortuous or sharply angulated LCx segments are subject to foreshortening on standard views, "which can... mask proximal stenosis," requiring alternative AP-caudal or LAO-caudal projections to adequately visualize proximal LCx angulation/tortuosity — [tortuosity/angiographic-projection literature, aggregated search result, primary source not individually re-verified]
- CT angiography's overall diagnostic detection performance is markedly better than invasive angiography for congenital coronary anomalies generally (100% vs 53% detection in one comparative series), which is a general imaging-modality point (not LCx-specific) but is consistent with, and likely part of the reason for, why CT rather than invasive angiography is increasingly used for LCx assessment specifically in imaging-difficult contexts — [general CTA-vs-invasive detection comparison, aggregated search result]

### Inferences
- The under-study claim is well evidenced for two adjacent domains (pediatric/Kawasaki-disease CTA literature explicitly, and echocardiography explicitly) and plausibly extends to adult CT-derived coronary geometry/tortuosity research by the same underlying anatomical cause (the LCx's posterior, atrioventricular-groove course makes it harder to visualize end-to-end without foreshortening or acoustic dropout on several modalities), but this extension is an inference, not a directly cited fact for CT centerline/geometry research specifically.
- Paradoxically, the tortuosity-specific findings above suggest the LCx is not under-studied in absolute terms for basic tortuosity prevalence (it appears as the top-line result, often the most-tortuous vessel, in multiple invasive-angiography cohort studies of several hundred to over a thousand patients), so the "under-studied" claim is more specifically about (a) diameter/nomogram reference data and (b) imaging modality-specific visualization difficulty, rather than about tortuosity-outcome research volume per se, where the LCx if anything appears prominently. This is an important nuance for the thesis: the evidence gap for LCx is not "nobody studies LCx tortuosity" but "LCx-specific findings conflict, normative geometric/diameter reference data are thinner, and visualization is harder," which is a different and more specific gap than a blanket under-study claim.

### Gaps
- No source was found stating explicitly, for CT-derived centerline/curvature/tortuosity research (as distinct from echocardiography or Kawasaki-disease literature), that the LCx receives less research attention than LAD or RCA; this specific claim remains an inference from adjacent-domain evidence, not a directly cited fact, and should be flagged as such if used in the thesis.
- No study was found directly comparing publication counts or cohort sizes for LCx-specific vs. RCA-specific vs. LAD-specific geometric/tortuosity feature research, which would be the most rigorous way to substantiate an under-study claim quantitatively.
