# Robust "normal" definitions and ML feature engineering for tree/curve-shaped geometric measurements

## What is functional PCA (fPCA) applied to a curve/curvature/torsion profile, and how are its scores used as ML features in clinical prediction outside cardiology?

### Takeaway
fPCA treats an entire shape function (e.g. a curvature-vs-arc-length profile) as one functional observation and decomposes it into an orthonormal basis of "eigenfunctions" plus a small vector of subject-specific scores (basis coefficients); those scores, rather than a single scalar summary index, are the features fed downstream. This is an established feature-extraction step in longitudinal/biomarker clinical prediction (oncology, transplant, cardiology, Alzheimer's, mobile health), but the specific case of fPCA applied directly to a Frenet-Serret curvature/torsion profile of a 3D curve is a much newer and thinner literature, and no direct clinical-risk-model application of the latter was found.

### Cited Findings
- Functional data analysis (FDA), specifically FPCA, has been "widely adopted in the literature for extracting features from time-varying biomarker trajectories" and has been used to abstract trends in intensive longitudinal data (ILD) collected by mobile health devices, though FDA/fPCA has "not been used extensively in mobile health research" to date — [Predicting health outcomes with intensive longitudinal data / fPCA regression](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10944610/)
- "Supervised FPCA" is a documented refinement: it determines functional principal components so as to optimize the association between the time-varying marker and a time-to-event outcome (rather than just explaining variance in the marker itself), and it "accommodates irregularly spaced and sparse longitudinal data"; simulation studies show supervised FPCA "retain[s] better discrimination and calibration performance than unsupervised FPCA" — [Dynamic prediction with time-dependent marker using supervised FPCA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10025984/)
- A supervised-FPCA method has been used to "extract image features associated with Alzheimer's disease risk while accounting for right censoring", i.e. fPCA scores computed directly from imaging-derived functional profiles are used as risk-model covariates — [Supervised FPCA under a mixture cure rate model, Alzheimer's application](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11760660/)
- fPCA has been used to extract features from longitudinal cholesterol profiles for landmark analysis of coronary heart disease risk, and jointly to model multiple transplant outcomes via a competing-risks model — [FPCA landmark analysis, cholesterol/CHD](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33155338/); [FPCA competing-risk transplant outcomes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9754024)
- On the geometric side: a Frenet-Serret-based functional-data framework has been developed specifically to estimate curvature and torsion of 3D curves ("3D functional data"), motivated by the fact that "classical [FDA] approaches that rely on linear approximations" cannot capture the geometric features of multi-dimensional curves. It defines a "mean curvature," "mean torsion," and a "mean shape" via a mean vector field, and fits these via a penalized-regression formulation with an accompanying algorithm, validated on simulated and real data — [Curvature and Torsion estimation of 3D functional data (Frenet-Serret), arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02398)
- That same Frenet-Serret paper's abstract/available content does not explicitly describe applying fPCA (eigen-decomposition into scores) to the estimated curvature/torsion functions — it stops at estimating the mean curvature/torsion/shape, not at a dimension-reduced score vector — [arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02398) (checked directly, not just abstract, via fetch; no fPCA/eigenfunction decomposition section found in the retrieved content)
- Curvature and torsion of a 3D curve are formally defined as, respectively, the rate of change of the unit tangent vector (intensity of in-plane bending) and the rate of change of the osculating plane (out-of-plane deformation); arc-length parameterization is the standard way to make such profiles comparable across subjects by removing sampling/velocity variability — [Shape descriptors and arc-length parameterization, general FDA/shape-analysis search summary](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02398) and related morphometrics work — [Reframing 3D Morphometrics through FDA, arXiv:2509.00650](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.00650)

### Inferences
- The pattern in the clinical-prediction literature is: (1) parameterize the raw curve/trajectory over a common domain (age, time, or arc length), (2) run fPCA (or a supervised variant) to get a K-dimensional score vector per subject, (3) feed those K scores into a standard risk model (Cox, landmark, competing-risks, or a generic ML classifier) instead of feeding either the raw curve or a single hand-picked scalar index. This generalizes directly to a curvature-vs-arc-length or torsion-vs-arc-length profile along a vessel/branch: each subject's profile becomes a curve, fPCA yields eigen-shape functions common to the population, and each subject's coordinates on those eigen-shapes are the ML features.
- Because "supervised FPCA" explicitly optimizes the components against the outcome rather than against variance explained, it is the more defensible choice when the end goal is a risk-prediction model (as opposed to purely descriptive population characterization), at the cost of needing the outcome label at feature-construction time (i.e., it is not usable for building an unsupervised "normal" reference alone).
- The Frenet-Serret fPCA line of work is the closest primary-literature match to "fPCA applied to a curvature/torsion profile" the user asked about, but it appears to stop short of the score-based dimension reduction that is standard in the clinical-outcome fPCA literature — suggesting that extending that framework with an eigenfunction decomposition of the estimated mean-curvature/torsion residual field is itself a research contribution rather than an off-the-shelf method.

### Gaps
- No source was found that applies fPCA (with explicit eigenfunction/score decomposition) directly to a curvature or torsion profile and then uses the resulting scores in a downstream ML risk-prediction model; the Frenet-Serret curvature/torsion estimation paper (arXiv:2203.02398) was the closest match but its retrieved content (abstract + main text summary) does not describe a score-based ML feature step.

## What are the standard robust statistics methods (LMS, GAMLSS, quantile regression, MCD/robust Mahalanobis) for defining a population "normal range" from skewed/heterogeneous data, and how is each more robust than naive mean ± 2 SD?

### Takeaway
Four families are standard, in increasing order of flexibility: LMS (a single Box-Cox transform to normality, then Gaussian ± quantiles), GAMLSS (LMS generalized to broader distribution families and smooth covariate-dependence of all distribution parameters), quantile regression (nonparametric on the error distribution, models each target quantile directly), and MCD/robust Mahalanobis distance (robustifies the multivariate center-and-spread estimate itself against contamination, replacing the plug-in sample mean/covariance used by classical Mahalanobis distance). Each addresses a distinct failure mode of naive mean ± 2 SD: skewness, non-Gaussian kurtosis, covariate-dependence, and outlier-contaminated covariance estimation, respectively.

### Cited Findings
- **LMS**: The method estimates three smooth curves (in a covariate such as age): M(t), the median (50th centile) curve; S(t), the coefficient of variation (fractional SD); and L(t), a Box-Cox skewness/power parameter. The z-score transform is `Z(t) = ((Y(t)/M(t))^{L(t)} - 1) / (L(t) S(t))`, with the limiting form `Z = ln(Y/M(t))/S(t)` when L(t) = 0 (lognormal case) — [WHO growth-chart computation of centiles/z-scores](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/child-growth/growth-reference-5-19-years/computation.pdf?sfvrsn=c2ff6a95_4); LMS is the international standard for age-specific reference centiles and underlies the WHO, CDC, and UK90 growth charts — [MetricGate LMS/Cole-Green docs](https://metricgate.com/docs/growth-chart-lms-method/)
- LMS is formally a special case of GAMLSS: LMS corresponds to fitting the Box-Cox Cole-Green (BCCG) distribution family within the GAMLSS framework — [European Journal of Human Genetics, LMSz method](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41431-025-01947-1)
- **GAMLSS**: a class of regression models for a univariate response where "the distribution for the response variable... can be selected from a very general family of distributions including highly skew or kurtotic continuous and discrete distributions," and *all* of the distribution's parameters (not just the mean) — location, scale, and shape — can each be modelled as linear, nonlinear, or smooth nonparametric functions of covariates — [CRAN gamlss package docs](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/gamlss/gamlss.pdf); [Wikipedia, GAMLSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_additive_model_for_location,_scale_and_shape)
- The WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study Group specifically recommended GAMLSS with the Box-Cox Power Exponential (BCPE) distribution family for constructing the WHO Child Growth Standards — [GAMLSS/BCPE search summary](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/gamlss/refman/gamlss.html)
- **BCPE**: a 4-parameter GAMLSS family with parameters (μ, σ, ν, τ): μ is the median, σ is approximately the coefficient of variation (for small σ, moderate ν>0), ν controls skewness, and τ controls kurtosis, with ranges μ>0, σ>0, ν ∈ (−∞, ∞), τ>0 — [gamlss.dist BCPE documentation](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/gamlss.dist/versions/6.1-1/topics/BCPE); foundational references are Rigby & Stasinopoulos (2004), *Statistics in Medicine* 23:3053-3076 ("Smooth centile curves... using the BCPE distribution") and Rigby & Stasinopoulos (2005), *Applied Statistics* 54(3):507-554 ("Generalized additive models for location, scale and shape") — [ResearchGate GAMLSS reference list](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26538604_Generalized_Additive_Models_for_Location_Scale_and_Shape_GAMLSS_inR)
- **Quantile regression**: extends regression to directly optimize conditional quantiles (e.g. the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles) of the response, and "makes no distributional assumption about the error term"; a 95% reference interval is obtained by fitting two separate quantile regressions at τ=0.025 and τ=0.975, optionally with covariates such as age — [Estimating reference intervals via quantile regression, BMC Medical Research Methodology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12874-024-02378-0)
- Quoted minimum sample-size guidance: a 95% reference interval (0.025/0.975 quantiles) needs a minimum n of 39; a 90% confidence interval around that 95% reference interval needs a minimum n of 119 — [Quantile-Quantile Toolbox for Reference Intervals, J. Applied Laboratory Medicine](https://academic.oup.com/jalm/article/9/2/357/7514598)
- **MCD (Minimum Covariance Determinant)**: looks for the h-observation subset (n/2 ≤ h ≤ n) whose sample covariance matrix has the smallest determinant (i.e., occupies the smallest possible volume/ellipsoid); the sample mean and covariance of that subset become the robust location/scatter estimate, and Mahalanobis distances computed from those robust estimates (rather than the plug-in full-sample mean/covariance) flag outliers — [ScienceDirect, MCD estimator in practice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1572312708000853); [scikit-learn robust covariance documentation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/covariance/plot_mahalanobis_distances.html)
- The FAST-MCD algorithm (Rousseeuw & Van Driessen, 1999) approximates the exhaustive MCD search using iterative "C-steps": from an initial h-subset, compute mean/covariance, compute Mahalanobis distances of all points against that mean/covariance, then re-select the h points with the smallest distances as the new subset; each C-step is guaranteed not to increase the determinant, and iterating to convergence gives a fast approximate MCD solution — [search summary of Rousseeuw & Van Driessen FAST-MCD](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/robustbase/versions/0.93-3/topics/covMcd)
- MCD's breakdown point rises to roughly (n − h)/n ≈ 50% for the default h, versus the classical (non-robust) sample covariance matrix, whose breakdown point is effectively 0% (a single extreme outlier can arbitrarily distort it) — [High-Breakdown Robust Multivariate Methods, arXiv:0808.0657](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0808.0657)
- The disadvantage motivating MCD explicitly: classical Mahalanobis distance uses "the multivariate sample mean and covariance matrix that are particularly sensitive to outliers," and "multivariate outliers can severely distort the estimation of population parameters" when using the naive plug-in estimates — [Detecting multivariate outliers: robust variant of Mahalanobis distance](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103117302123)

### Inferences
- The four methods form a natural escalation for a tree/curve-geometry "normal range" pipeline: if a single scalar feature (e.g. one tortuosity index) is skewed but its distribution is roughly stable across the population, LMS/BCCG (1 covariate curve set) suffices; if it depends smoothly on a covariate (age, vessel caliber, body size) and has non-trivial kurtosis as well as skew, GAMLSS/BCPE is the more general tool since it estimates all four moments as smooth covariate functions rather than assuming a fixed Box-Cox family; if no parametric family fits well, quantile regression sidesteps the distributional assumption entirely at the cost of not sharing information across quantiles (each quantile is fit somewhat independently, so extreme quantiles need larger samples, per the size guidance above); and once the feature vector is multivariate (several shape descriptors at once, e.g. several fPCA scores), none of LMS/GAMLSS/quantile regression is inherently multivariate, and MCD-based robust Mahalanobis distance is the standard way to define a single "distance from normal" while resisting contamination by a minority of outlier subjects in the reference population itself.
- All three univariate methods (LMS, GAMLSS, quantile regression) are more robust than naive mean ± 2 SD specifically because mean ± 2 SD implicitly assumes symmetry and Gaussianity; LMS/GAMLSS instead estimate the actual shape (skew via L or ν, kurtosis via τ) so that, e.g., a right-skewed measurement gets an asymmetric reference band instead of a symmetric one that would put a lower bound below zero or an upper bound too tight; quantile regression avoids the distributional assumption altogether. MCD is a different axis of robustness: it protects the *estimation of the reference model itself* against outlier contamination in the training/reference sample, a failure mode none of LMS/GAMLSS/quantile regression directly addresses (they can still be fit on a contaminated sample, e.g. via a robust GAMLSS variant, but that is a further refinement, not the base method).

### Gaps
- No source specifically discussing "robust GAMLSS" (i.e., robustified fitting of GAMLSS itself against outlying observations in the reference sample, distinct from GAMLSS's inherent skew/kurtosis flexibility) was retrieved; this may exist in the GAMLSS literature but was not found in this search pass.
- Exact equations for the BCPE density function (beyond parameter interpretation) were not retrieved in closed form; only the parameter roles (μ median, σ ≈ CV, ν skewness, τ kurtosis) were confirmed from the R documentation and blog secondary source. A primary-source check of Rigby & Stasinopoulos (2004/2005) would be needed to quote the density formula itself.

## What anomaly/novelty detection methods (one-class SVM, isolation forest, Mahalanobis-distance scoring, autoencoder reconstruction error) score a new sample's "distance from normal," and have any been applied to geometric/anatomical shape features from imaging?

### Takeaway
Four standard families exist — Mahalanobis/robust-Mahalanobis distance, one-class SVM, isolation forest, and autoencoder reconstruction error — and all four have documented applications to imaging-derived features, with Mahalanobis-distance scoring specifically documented on anatomical (white-matter tract) shape/diffusion features and autoencoder reconstruction error broadly documented for anatomical/medical images; isolation forest and one-class SVM are documented for imaging-adjacent quality/texture features (e.g., CT quality assessment) but a direct citation of either applied to *geometric tree/curve shape descriptors specifically* was not found in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- **Mahalanobis distance** is "one of the most common ways to detect anomalies from a given distribution," with "robust variants based on the Minimum Covariance Determinant proposed to tackle sensitivity to outliers" — [Why is the Mahalanobis Distance Effective for Anomaly Detection?, arXiv:2003.00402](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.00402)
- Direct anatomical/imaging application: Mahalanobis Distance Tractometry (MaD-Tract) computes the Mahalanobis distance of diffusion-tensor eigenvalue profiles *along white-matter tracts* between an individual TBI patient and a normative healthy-control sample, for personalized white-matter anomaly detection — [MaD-Tract, PMC9531540](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9531540/). This is a directly relevant precedent: it applies Mahalanobis-distance-based normative comparison to a profile measured *along* an anatomical curve (a white-matter tract), structurally analogous to a curvature/tortuosity profile along a vessel centerline.
- Mahalanobis distance's practical advantages are stated as "low computational requirements and high interpretability," since it quantifies deviation via just the mean vector and covariance matrix of the reference/training set — [search summary](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8121071/)
- **One-class SVM**: trained via unsupervised learning to fit a frontier (hyperplane in a kernel-induced feature space) enclosing most of the training (assumed-normal) data on one side; new points falling outside the frontier are scored as anomalies — [search summary of one-class SVM anomaly detection](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395912929_ANOMALY_DETECTION_IN_MNIST_DATASET_USING_ONE-CLASS_SVM)
- **Isolation forest**: isolates anomalies by recursively partitioning the feature space with random splits, exploiting the fact that rare/anomalous samples require fewer partitions to isolate than typical samples; comparative evaluations report it scales better than one-class SVM and other classical methods — [search summary, Isolation Forest vs SVM/DBSCAN](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388323510_Evaluating_the_Performance_of_SVM_Isolation_Forest_and_DBSCAN_for_Anomaly_Detection)
- Imaging application of isolation forest: a multi-modal framework integrating isolation forest and DBSCAN was used for automated CT image quality assessment using a 22-dimensional engineered feature vector (statistical + texture features) — [search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.10802) (imaging-quality application, not a geometric/anatomical shape descriptor application specifically)
- **Autoencoder reconstruction error**: the standard unsupervised-anomaly-detection logic is "if only normal samples are used to train the autoencoder, it will be able to reconstruct similar normal samples accurately, and the reconstruction results for abnormal samples will be poor," so reconstruction error (typically MSE) serves as the anomaly score — [Autoencoders for Detecting Anomalies in Medical Imaging, University of Twente thesis](https://essay.utwente.nl/fileshare/file/98159/Reve_BA_EEMCS.pdf)
- Explicit medical-imaging framing: "In medical diagnosis, an encoder can be trained on a set of normal medical images. When a new medical image is acquired, the encoder can be used to reconstruct the image. If the reconstruction error is high, then the image may contain a medical anomaly" — [same source, Twente thesis](https://essay.utwente.nl/fileshare/file/98159/Reve_BA_EEMCS.pdf); a related deep-generative approach explicitly learns "manifolds of normal anatomical variability," scoring anomalies via the image-to-latent-space mapping rather than pixelwise reconstruction error alone — [search summary, generative anatomical-variability anomaly scoring](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8121071/)
- An attempted direct fetch of "Unsupervised Shape Normality Metric for Severity Quantification" (arXiv:2007.09307), which by title appears to propose exactly a shape-based normality/anomaly score, failed: the WebFetch tool returned only corrupted/binary PDF content and could not extract the methodology. This paper's actual method (whether Mahalanobis-, autoencoder-, or PCA-based; what anatomy/dataset) could not be verified in this pass and should be treated as an open lead, not a confirmed finding.

### Inferences
- The MaD-Tract precedent (Mahalanobis distance over a profile measured along an anatomical curve, compared against a normative population) is the closest structural analogue found to "compute a normal range for a curvature/tortuosity profile along a vessel and score a new patient's deviation from it," and suggests that pairing fPCA-derived scores (question 1) with MCD/robust-Mahalanobis scoring (question 2) is not a novel combination in principle. The same combination (functional feature extraction -> multivariate distance-based anomaly scoring) already exists for a different anatomical curve type (white-matter tracts vs. diffusion metrics).
- No source found in this pass reports one-class SVM or isolation forest applied specifically to hand-crafted geometric/tree-shape descriptors (curvature, torsion, branching angle) as opposed to intensity/texture features; this may reflect a genuine gap in the literature rather than a search failure, since anatomical shape anomaly detection appears to more often use either explicit statistical distance (Mahalanobis/MCD) or generative/autoencoder reconstruction error rather than isolation forest or one-class SVM. This should be flagged as a gap rather than asserted as fact.

- Could not verify the method used in "Unsupervised Shape Normality Metric for Severity Quantification" (arXiv:2007.09307) due to a PDF-fetch failure; a follow-up with a different retrieval method (e.g. Read tool on a downloaded copy, or the paper's HTML abstract page) is needed before citing its specific method.

## What do methodological papers on clinical/biomedical risk-prediction recommend about feature engineering from raw high-dimensional shape data (hand-crafted vs. learned embeddings), and how are the two compared for interpretability vs. predictive power at small-to-moderate sample size?

### Takeaway
The radiomics methodology literature converges on: hand-crafted features are preferred for interpretability and for small-to-moderate clinical sample sizes (they don't need a large dataset to avoid overfitting and can be individually validated against clinical reasoning), deep/learned features tend to need larger datasets and are less interpretable, and the most consistently recommended practical compromise is a hybrid model combining both, which several sources report yields the best predictive performance.

### Cited Findings
- "Deep feature extraction requires a large database to avoid overfitting issues" whereas hand-crafted radiomic feature extraction "has shown advantages over deep feature extraction, making it possible to perform accurate feature extraction on moderate sample sizes" — [Handcrafted versus deep learning radiomics for prediction of cancer therapy response, The Lancet Digital Health](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(19)30062-7/fulltext)
- "Deep learning approaches assume a large enough dataset which can be problematic for small medical datasets, whereas hand-crafted features have no such constraint and are easily interpretable" — [search summary of hand-crafted vs deep radiomics literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10606594/) (title: "Deep Features from Pretrained Networks Do Not Outperform Hand-Crafted Features in Radiomics" — note the title itself is a direct, primary finding, not just a summary claim)
- "Hand-crafted radiomic features offer intelligibility, with shallow learning and explainable methods providing insights into the features driving their decisions, allowing clinicians to validate the reasoning"; by contrast "latent deep features... are less interpretable than radiomics" — [Image biomarkers and explainable AI: handcrafted vs deep learned features, European Radiology Experimental](https://eurradiolexp.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41747-024-00529-y)
- "A hybrid approach including both hand-crafted and deep learning features often yields the highest performance" — [search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593328/), though this specific sentence is a search-engine synthesis rather than a directly quoted/verified primary-source sentence and should be treated as moderately, not fully, confirmed pending direct-source check.
- A caution specific to small samples: "challenges of traditional radiomics approaches such as lack of reproducibility and interpretability as well as over-fitting on small datasets will only be amplified in deep learning-driven prediction models of cancer outcome" — [search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11576747/), i.e. deep learning does not just fail to help in small samples, it is reported to actively amplify the reproducibility/overfitting problems radiomics already has.

### Inferences
- For a thesis-scale or moderate-N clinical cohort (the kind implied by "small-to-moderate sample size clinical setting" in the prompt), the literature consensus favors starting from domain-informed hand-crafted shape descriptors (e.g. fPCA scores of a curvature profile, tortuosity indices, branching angles) rather than an end-to-end learned shape embedding (autoencoder or GNN latent space), specifically because: (a) it avoids the large-N requirement of deep feature learning, (b) it preserves clinician-checkable interpretability, and (c) the "amplified overfitting" caution suggests learned embeddings are riskier, not just less interpretable, at small N. A learned embedding is more defensible as a secondary/exploratory comparison arm (per the hybrid-approach finding) than as the primary feature set when N is limited.

### Gaps
- The "hybrid approach yields highest performance" claim was only found via search-engine synthesis, not a directly fetched primary sentence; it should be verified against the original PMC11593328 text before being stated with full confidence in a downstream report.
- No paper specifically addressing feature engineering from *tree-structured* (as opposed to 2D image/texture) shape data was found in this sub-question; the findings here are from the general radiomics (image texture) literature, which may not transfer exactly to branching-curve geometric features. This is a genuine gap: the closest tree-specific material is covered under question 5 below (GNN/graph literature), not this radiomics-methodology literature.

## Are GNNs or other tree/graph-structured learning methods used to learn a shape embedding directly from a branching tree's geometry without collapsing to hand-crafted scalar indices, and how do they handle varying topology across samples?

### Takeaway
Yes: GNN- and recursive-neural-network-based methods exist specifically for vascular/coronary tree geometry (node/edge features = positions, radii, curvature; edges = vessel connectivity), and they handle varying topology in one of two ways — either (a) generic permutation-invariant graph pooling/readout (sum/mean/max, or learned attention-based readout such as Set2Set) that can ingest graphs of any size and structure, or (b) recursive/hierarchical architectures that explicitly traverse the tree structure with weight-tied branch/merge operations, producing a fixed-size latent code regardless of branch count. Coronary-artery-specific GNN work exists mainly for anatomical labeling/segmentation rather than for a risk-prediction shape embedding per se.

### Cited Findings
- Graph neural networks have been applied for automatic extraction and anatomical labeling of the coronary artery tree in CT angiography, using graph convolutional networks that combine geometric and image-intensity information per arterial segment/node — [GNNs for coronary artery tree labeling, PMC11095121](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095121/)
- EAGMN (Edge Attention Graph Matching Network) converts coronary artery semantic segmentation into a graph-node similarity task, using graph attention modules for feature embedding/aggregation across arterial segment nodes, explicitly to handle bifurcation/branching junctions — [EAGMN, arXiv:2305.12327](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12327)
- TreeLab-Net (a graph/point-cloud-based method) reports AUC > 97% for main coronary branch classification and > 90% for major side-branch classification, using morphological distance fields to resolve branch identity at junctions — [search summary of coronary GNN labeling literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.00386)
- SIRE is a graph neural network (a gauge-equivariant mesh CNN, GEM-CNN) that estimates local vessel orientation directly from image intensities on nested spherical meshes around each vessel point; it is explicitly scale-invariant (via weight sharing plus a symmetric max-aggregation across scales) and SO(3) rotation-equivariant (via gauge-equivariant convolution), and was validated on the Vascular Model Repository, the ASOCA coronary artery dataset, and an in-house abdominal aortic aneurysm dataset — [SIRE, arXiv:2311.05400](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05400)
- A Recursive variational Neural Network (RvNN) "fully exploits the hierarchical organization of vessels and learns a low-dimensional manifold encoding branch connectivity along with geometry features," for 3D blood-vessel generative modeling, and its latent space can be sampled to generate new vessel geometries — [Recursive VAE for vessel generative modeling, ScienceDirect](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1361841525002506); direct fetch of the full article was blocked (HTTP 403), so architectural detail beyond this abstract-level description (e.g. exact node/branch feature set, how variable branch count maps to a fixed latent size) could not be verified in this pass.
- General GNN topology-handling mechanics: for graph classification, a permutation-invariant "READOUT" function (sum, mean, max, or a more sophisticated pooling function) aggregates node-level representations into one graph-level representation, which by construction handles a variable number of nodes and is invariant to node ordering — [Advanced Graph Pooling and Readout](https://apxml.com/courses/graph-neural-networks-gnns/chapter-4-advanced-gnn-tasks-techniques/graph-pooling-readout); "Set2Set" is cited as a standard attention-based readout mechanism for producing a permutation-invariant graph-level embedding from a variable-size node set — [search summary, GNN readout mechanisms](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/7caf9d251b546bc78078b35b4a6f3b7e-Paper-Conference.pdf)
- On tree-specific recursive architectures more generally (outside the vascular domain, e.g. NLP TreeNets): "TreeNets can have different numbers of inputs in the branch nodes... branch nodes of the same type have tied weights," which is exactly the mechanism that lets a single fixed set of learned weights process trees of varying branching factor and depth — [search summary, TreeNets/recursive neural networks](https://www.kdnuggets.com/2016/06/recursive-neural-networks-tensorflow.html)

### Inferences
- Two structurally distinct solutions to "varying topology" recur across this literature: (1) message-passing GNN + permutation-invariant global pooling (sum/mean/max/attention), which is topology-agnostic almost by construction because the aggregation function does not care how many nodes/edges it receives; and (2) recursive/hierarchical tree-autoencoders with weight-tying across branch nodes, which instead explicitly walk the tree structure and are architecturally tree-native rather than merely tolerant of variable graph size. The RvNN vessel-generative-modeling work is the closest existing precedent for producing a *fixed-length* shape embedding from a branching vascular tree with a variable number of branches without first collapsing to scalar indices, which directly answers the "without first collapsing to hand-crafted scalar indices" framing of the prompt's key question.
- The bulk of GNN work specifically on coronary/vascular trees found here targets anatomical labeling/segmentation/orientation estimation, not risk-prediction embeddings; using such a GNN's learned per-node or graph-level representation as a downstream risk-model feature (rather than for the labeling task it was trained for) would be a repurposing not directly documented in the retrieved sources, and should be flagged as an inference rather than a demonstrated precedent.
