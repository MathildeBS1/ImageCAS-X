# Curve-shape and tree-shape analysis methods from differential geometry, computer graphics, computational topology, and robotics/motion-planning

Scope note: this catalogue stays in the mathematical/technical-methods domain. Where a method is already known to have been applied to blood vessels or other anatomical trees, that is flagged explicitly as a note, not as the primary content.

## What is the Frenet-Serret frame and how do curvature kappa(s) and torsion tau(s) combine into scalar/spectral shape descriptors? What are the standard summary statistics and how are they discretized for a polyline?

### Takeaway
The Frenet-Serret apparatus gives an arc-length-indexed orthonormal frame (T, N, B) whose evolution equations define curvature kappa(s) and torsion tau(s) as the two local scalar invariants of a 3D curve; the standard scalar summaries (total curvature/turning, mean curvature, curvature/torsion "energy") are line integrals of kappa and tau (or their squares) over arc length, and on a polyline these are approximated either via turning angles between consecutive edges (discrete curvature) or via circumradius/Menger-curvature of consecutive point triples, with torsion requiring a third-order (four-point) discrete estimate.

### Cited Findings
- The continuous Frenet-Serret equations are dT(s)/ds = kappa(s) N(s), dN(s)/ds = -kappa(s) T(s) + tau(s) B(s), dB(s)/ds = -tau(s) N(s), where kappa(s) and tau(s) are the curvature and torsion respectively, s being arc length. — [Space Curves, Frenet Frames, and Torsion](https://www.math.umd.edu/~jmr/241/curves2.htm); consistent with [Frenet-Serret and the Estimation of Curvature and Torsion (BIRS workshop notes, Kim)](https://www.birs.ca/workshops/2011/11w5018/files/kim.pdf)
- A common discrete Frenet frame construction on a polyline of points c_i defines the unit tangent T_i = (c_{i+1} - c_i)/|c_{i+1} - c_i|, then the discrete normal N_i = (T_i - T_{i-1})/|T_i - T_{i-1}|, and the discrete binormal B_i = T_i x N_i (cross product), from which discrete curvature and torsion angles are read off as the turning of T and of the (N,B) plane respectively. — [Frenet-Serret and the Estimation of Curvature and Torsion](https://www.birs.ca/workshops/2011/11w5018/files/kim.pdf)
- For discrete chains (this literature comes from protein backbone geometry, a non-anatomical/non-vascular discrete-curve setting), curvature is represented by the bond angle kappa_i between successive tangent segments and torsion by the dihedral/torsion angle tau_i between successive (T,N) or equivalent local planes, replacing the continuous kappa(s), tau(s) — i.e., the discrete analogue of Frenet-Serret is a sequence of local turning angles rather than continuous rates. — [The Discrete Frenet Frame, Inflection Point Solitons And Curve Visualization with Applications to Folded Proteins](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1102.5658)
- Standard scalar summary statistics built from kappa(s) and tau(s) over an arc-length interval [0, L] are integrals: total curvature (total turning) = integral_0^L kappa(s) ds (the total angle through which the unit tangent vector turns); "curvature energy" / bending energy uses integral_0^L kappa(s)^2 ds (related to the Willmore/elastic bending energy of a curve); an analogous "torsional energy" uses integral_0^L tau(s)^2 ds. — [On the Torsional Energy of Deformed Curves and Knots](https://doi.org/10.3390/axioms13100661); [Fáry–Milnor theorem discussion, total curvature as supremal total curvature of inscribed polygons](http://people.reed.edu/~ormsbyk/milnor-total-curvature.pdf)
- Fenchel's theorem: the total curvature (total turning, integral of kappa(s) ds) of any simple closed curve in 3-space is >= 2*pi, with equality if and only if the curve is a planar convex curve. — [Six proofs of the Fáry–Milnor theorem (arXiv, states Fenchel's theorem as background)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.15137); [Fáry–Milnor theorem summary](https://lean-lang.org/eval/problems/fary_milnor/)
- Fáry–Milnor theorem: a smooth (or tame polygonal) closed curve in R^3 with total curvature <= 4*pi must be unknotted; equivalently, any nontrivially knotted closed curve in R^3 has total curvature strictly greater than 4*pi. Milnor's original definition of total curvature for an arbitrary (possibly non-smooth/polygonal) closed curve is the supremum, over all inscribed polygons, of the sum of exterior turning angles at the polygon's vertices — this is exactly the natural discretization of integral kappa(s) ds for a polyline (sum of turning angles at vertices). — [J.W. Milnor, "On the Total Curvature of Knots," Annals of Mathematics (1950), as hosted](http://people.reed.edu/~ormsbyk/milnor-total-curvature.pdf); [Six proofs of the Fáry–Milnor theorem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.15137)
- A widely used discrete curvature at an interior polyline vertex from three consecutive points is the Menger curvature: for points x1, x2, x3 forming a triangle of area Delta and side lengths a, b, c, the Menger curvature is c(x1,x2,x3) = 4*Delta/(a*b*c) = 1/R, where R = a*b*c/(4*Delta) is the circumradius of the triangle through the three points — i.e., discrete curvature at a point is the reciprocal of the radius of the osculating (circumscribed) circle through that point and its two neighbors. — [Menger curvature (Wikipedia, summarizing standard definition)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menger_curvature); [Curvature, circumradius, and circumcenter formulas for any three points](https://hratliff.com/posts/2019/02/curvature-of-three-points/)
- An equivalent vector form of three-point (Menger) discrete curvature using consecutive edge-displacement vectors u = x2-x1, v = x3-x2 is c = 2*sqrt(1 - cos_sim(u,v)^2) / |x3 - x1| (i.e., 2*sin(angle between u,v)) / (distance between the two outer points)), giving a closed-form, coordinate-free discretization directly usable on a 3D polyline. — [Notes on Menger Curvature / three-point curvature discussion](https://doc.gold.ac.uk/~ffl/Notes/CurvSurf/MengerCurv/index.html)
- Dedicated 3D discrete curvature and torsion *estimators* (as opposed to the naive three/four-point finite-difference formulas above) exist in the digital-geometry / discrete differential geometry literature under the name "Curvature and Torsion Estimators for 3D Curves," addressing multigrid convergence (estimator accuracy as sampling resolution increases) rather than only pointwise finite differences. — [Curvature and Torsion Estimators for 3D Curves (Springer chapter, title/abstract verified via search; full equations not independently confirmed from primary text in this session)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-89639-5_66)

### Inferences
- Because total curvature (sum of turning angles for a polyline) is exactly the quantity bounded below by Fenchel's and Fáry–Milnor's theorems, "total curvature" is not an arbitrary tortuosity statistic but a quantity with hard topological floors (>= 2*pi for any closed curve, > 4*pi if knotted); for an *open* curve/tree branch there is no such closed-curve theorem, but the same discretization (sum of vertex turning angles) is the natural, resolution-consistent way to accumulate "total bending" along a branch, and it is additive along a path, which matters when later summarizing a whole tree of branches (Key Question 4) by aggregating per-edge total-curvature contributions.
- The three-point Menger-curvature discretization and the four-point discrete torsion/turning-angle discretization are the direct polyline analogues of kappa(s) and tau(s); because they only use raw point positions (no explicit derivative estimation), they are simple to implement but, unlike the multiscale methods below, commit to a single "scale" set by the point spacing of the polyline — motivating Key Questions 2 and 5.

## What is curvature scale space (CSS) and multiscale curvature via Gaussian smoothing, and is there an analogous wavelet-based multiscale curvature/torsion decomposition for 3D curves?

### Takeaway
CSS represents a planar curve's shape as a two-parameter function kappa(u, sigma) of arc-length position u and Gaussian smoothing scale sigma, by convolving the curve's coordinate functions with Gaussians of increasing width and recomputing curvature at every scale, so that shape features (inflection points, extrema) are tracked continuously across scale rather than fixed at one smoothing choice; this has been extended to 3D space curves via torsion-based and combined torsion-curvature scale-space descriptors, and a distinct multiscale route replaces the Gaussian scale parameter with a wavelet (B-spline) decomposition level, giving fast multiresolution curvature estimates with the same "curvature as a function of scale" property.

### Cited Findings
- CSS convolves an arc-length (path-based) parametric representation of a planar curve with a 1D Gaussian of increasing width sigma, producing smoothed coordinate functions X(u,sigma), Y(u,sigma); the standard formula is X(u,sigma) = x(u) * g(u,sigma) and Y(u,sigma) = y(u) * g(u,sigma), where g(u,sigma) = (1/(sigma*sqrt(2*pi))) * exp(-u^2/(2*sigma^2)) and * denotes convolution. — [A Theory of Multiscale, Curvature-Based Shape Representation for Planar Curves, Mokhtarian & Mackworth, IEEE TPAMI, Aug 1992](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/34.149591); Gaussian convolution formula corroborated by search-derived summary — [Curvature Scale Space overview](https://home.iitk.ac.in/~amit/courses/768/99/gunjan/)
- Curvature at scale sigma is then computed from the smoothed coordinate functions by the standard planar-curve curvature formula applied to the smoothed curve: kappa(u,sigma) = [X'(u,sigma) Y''(u,sigma) - X''(u,sigma) Y'(u,sigma)] / [X'(u,sigma)^2 + Y'(u,sigma)^2]^(3/2), where primes denote derivatives with respect to the parameter u. — [Curvature Scale Space overview, restating Mokhtarian & Mackworth's formula](https://home.iitk.ac.in/~amit/courses/768/99/gunjan/)
- As sigma increases from a small to a large value, the curve is progressively smoothed and the number of curvature zero-crossings (inflection points) monotonically decreases; the "CSS image" is the plot, over the (arc-length, sigma) plane, of the set of (u, sigma) locations where kappa(u,sigma) = 0, forming arch-shaped contours, each corresponding to a concavity or convexity of the original curve, that shrink and eventually vanish as sigma grows — this is what makes CSS an explicit function-of-scale representation (a whole family of curvature zero-crossing maps) rather than a single smoothing choice. — [A Theory of Multiscale, Curvature-Based Shape Representation for Planar Curves](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/34.149591)
- The CSS representation is invariant to rotation, uniform scaling, and translation of the curve, and (for closed planar curves) maintains a essentially one-to-one correspondence between a curve and its CSS image; it was adopted as an MPEG-7 standard visual shape descriptor. — [Curvature Scale Space Representation: Theory, Applications, and MPEG-7 Standardization, Mokhtarian & Bober](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232203535_Curvature_Scale_Space_Representation_Theory_Applications_and_MPEG-7_Standardization)
- CSS has an affine-invariant variant using affine-length parametrization instead of Euclidean arc length. — [Curvature Scale Space with Affine Length Parametrisation](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-48236-9_39)
- A 3D extension exists as "A Theory of Multiscale, Torsion-Based Shape Representation for Space Curves" (Mokhtarian, 1997), which builds an analogous scale-space image but tracks torsion zero-crossings of a 3D space curve under Gaussian smoothing, rather than planar curvature zero-crossings. — [A Theory of Multiscale, Torsion-Based Shape Representation for Space Curves, Computer Vision and Image Understanding (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1077314297905440)
- A further generalization, the "Generalized Torsion-Curvature Scale Space (GTCSS) Descriptor for 3-Dimensional Curves," computes both curvature and torsion measures of a 3D curve at multiple Gaussian smoothing scales jointly (rather than torsion alone) and reports the descriptor is invariant under rigid transformations; it has been evaluated for 3D pose estimation from space curves extracted from 3D objects. I could not retrieve the exact governing equations from primary text in this session — this is an abstract/summary-level finding only. — [Generalized Torsion-Curvature Scale Space Descriptor for 3-Dimensional Curves (ResearchGate, abstract-level)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368743689_Generalized_Torsion-Curvature_Scale_Space_Descriptor_for_3-Dimensional_Curves)
- A related multi-scale representation for free-form space curves, the "Torsion Scale Space Image," is described as tracking torsion-based features of a 3D curve across scale, in the same spirit as CSS. — [Multi-Scale Representations for Free-Form Space Curves: The Torsion Scale Space Image and Its Properties](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-017-0343-7_10)
- A wavelet-based alternative to Gaussian CSS exists: "Multiscale curvature-based shape representation using B-spline wavelets" computes multiscale curvature functions efficiently via B-spline wavelet transforms, with curve evolution implemented directly in a B-spline scale-space; this is reported to have computational advantages over classical Gaussian scale-space, including the availability of fast (wavelet-transform) algorithms, and is presented as usable for curve data compression as well as shape representation. — [Multiscale curvature-based shape representation using B-spline wavelets (PubMed listing)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18267433/)
- A more recent variant, "An almost complete curvature scale space representation: Euclidean case," revisits completeness properties of the Euclidean CSS construction (i.e., under what conditions the CSS image uniquely determines the curve up to the invariances). — [An almost complete curvature scale space representation: Euclidean case](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0923596518306921)

### Inferences
- CSS and its 3D descendants (torsion scale space, GTCSS) directly satisfy the objective's multiscale requirement (a): they report the whole zero-crossing structure across a continuum of sigma, rather than picking one sigma, so a curve's shape is described by which features (inflections/zero-torsion points) persist across a wide scale range versus which are scale-fragile — this is a principled, non-arbitrary alternative to reporting a single tortuosity number at one fixed smoothing.
- The B-spline wavelet route to multiscale curvature is a genuinely distinct multiresolution mechanism (dyadic wavelet decomposition levels rather than a continuous Gaussian sigma sweep) and answers the objective's explicit sub-question about a "wavelet-based multiscale curvature/torsion decomposition for 3D curves" — although the retrieved evidence for a full joint curvature+torsion wavelet decomposition in 3D (as opposed to a 2D/curvature-only or torsion-only construction) is thinner than for Gaussian CSS and should be checked against primary text before being relied on for exact equations.

### Gaps
- I was not able to retrieve the exact GTCSS or wavelet-B-spline formulas (the precise multiscale torsion definition, or the wavelet basis/decomposition formula for 3D curvature and torsion jointly) as primary-source-verified equations within this session's tool budget; the findings above for GTCSS and the B-spline wavelet method are abstract/title-level only and should be flagged to the report writer as needing a follow-up full-text read if exact equations are required.
- I found no direct evidence of a CSS-style method that jointly outputs curvature and torsion zero-crossing surfaces (as two coupled scale-space images with a stated joint invariant), only: pure curvature CSS (2D), pure torsion scale space (3D), and a "generalized" combined descriptor whose exact joint formulation I could not verify from primary text.

## What is SRVF/elastic shape analysis, how does it define a metric/distance invariant to parametrization, translation, rotation, and (optionally) scale, and has it (or a similar Riemannian shape-space approach) been used to define a mean shape / normal range for a population of curves or curve-trees?

### Takeaway
The square-root velocity function (SRVF) re-parametrizes a curve by its normalized velocity, q(t) = beta_dot(t)/sqrt(|beta_dot(t)|), which turns the reparametrization-invariant "elastic metric" (a metric that separately accounts for bending and stretching) into the ordinary L2 metric on SRVFs, so that geodesic distance between two curves' shapes becomes a tractable optimization (over rotation and reparametrization) of an L2 distance between q-functions, and the same Riemannian-manifold machinery lets one define a Karcher/Fréchet mean shape by minimizing summed squared geodesic distances over a population, giving exactly the "mean shape + variability around the mean" structure the objective calls a normal range; the general Riemannian statistical-shape-model framework (mean + modes of variation) is well established for point/landmark shapes and is explicitly said to extend to curves, but I was not able to verify from primary text, within this session, an explicit SRVF-based construction of a mean shape and normal range for a whole *tree* of curves (only for individual curves / landmark configurations).

### Cited Findings
- The square-root velocity function (SRVF) transform of a parametrized curve beta(t) is q(t) = beta_dot(t) / sqrt(|beta_dot(t)|), where |.| is the Euclidean norm and beta_dot is the time-derivative of beta; the transform is invertible given the curve's starting point beta(0): beta(t) = beta(0) + integral_0^t q(s) |q(s)| ds. — [Square-Root Velocity (SRV) Representations, summarizing the SRVF transform](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/square-root-velocity-srv-representations)
- Under the SRV representation, the elastic metric (which otherwise requires separately weighting bending and stretching deformations and is difficult to compute with directly) simplifies to the plain L2 metric on the space of SRVFs; consequently the reparametrization group acts by isometries on this space, and (after imposing unit length) the space of unit-length curves' SRVFs becomes (a subset of) the unit Hilbert sphere. — [Square-Root Velocity (SRV) Representations](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/square-root-velocity-srv-representations); corroborated by search-derived summary of Srivastava, Klassen, Joshi & Jermyn, "Shape Analysis of Elastic Curves in Euclidean Spaces," IEEE TPAMI 2011 — [search summary citing the TPAMI paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921581/)
- The shape space of closed curves is defined as the quotient of (a submanifold of) this unit sphere by the rotation group SO(n) and the reparametrization group (the group of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms of the domain), so that two curves have the same "shape" if one SRVF can be rotated and reparametrized into the other; because the sphere has known closed-form geodesics, geodesics in the shape-space quotient are found via a "path-straightening" algorithm (an iterative gradient method that shortens an initial path between two points on the quotient space until it is a critical point of length, i.e. a geodesic). — [search summary of Srivastava, Klassen, Joshi & Jermyn 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921581/)
- These geodesics and the resulting geodesic distances give a metric on curve shapes that is by construction invariant to translation (curves are represented via velocity, discarding the additive constant beta(0)), to rotation and reparametrization (explicitly quotiented out), and the framework supports an optional additional quotienting/normalization for scale (fixing curves to unit length before comparison removes scale as a factor). — [search summary of Srivastava, Klassen, Joshi & Jermyn 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921581/); [Square-Root Velocity (SRV) Representations](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/square-root-velocity-srv-representations)
- The SRVF framework has been explicitly generalized beyond flat Euclidean curves, e.g. to "The Square Root Velocity Framework for Curves in a Homogeneous Space" and to curves of bounded variation, indicating the metric/mean-shape machinery is actively extended rather than fixed to the original 2011 construction. — [The Square Root Velocity Framework for Curves in a Homogeneous Space (CVPR 2017 workshop paper by Zhe Su et al.)](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2017_workshops/w7/papers/Su_The_Square_Root_CVPR_2017_paper.pdf); [A square root velocity framework for curves of bounded variation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.06633)
- The Riemannian statistical-shape-model literature generally defines a statistical shape model as consisting of (1) a detailed geometric representation of the average anatomy/shape of a population (the mean shape) and (2) a representation of population-level geometric variability, typically as a set of principal modes of variation, computed via Riemannian statistics such as the Fréchet mean and Riemannian variance/covariance within the relevant abstract metric (shape) space — this Fréchet-mean-plus-modes-of-variation structure is exactly the "mean + normal range" pattern requested, and is explicitly stated to be used for identifying intra-population variability and inter-population differences. — [Statistical Shape Modeling overview (ScienceDirect Topics)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/computer-science/statistical-shape-modeling)
- Kendall's shape space is the specific Riemannian shape-space construction for landmark configurations (as opposed to full parametrized curves): the preshape space of k landmarks in R^m is S^k_m = {X in R^(m x k) : sum_i X_i = 0 (centered), ||X||_F = 1 (unit scale)}, i.e., after centering and scale-normalizing a landmark configuration one obtains a point on a sphere (the "preshape sphere"); Kendall's shape space itself is the quotient of this preshape sphere by the rotation group, which for k landmarks in the plane is isomorphic to the complex projective space CP^(k-2). — [Landmark shape spaces with induced metrics / geomstats documentation, summarizing Kendall's construction](https://geomstats.github.io/notebooks/15_real_world_applications__optic_nerve_heads_analysis_in_kendall_shape_space.html); preshape-space formal definition — [search-derived formal definition of preshape space](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11304)
- Elastic/SRVF shape analysis is explicitly discussed as extending "to functions, curves and trajectories," and a dedicated tutorial, "Elastic Statistical Shape Analysis of Biological Structures with Case Studies," presents worked case studies applying the elastic-curve/SRVF machinery, indicating the mean-shape-and-variability construction is routinely applied to populations of curves in practice (case-study level; I did not verify from primary text whether any case study specifically concerns a branching *tree* of curves rather than isolated curves). — [Elastic Shape Analysis of Functions, Curves and Trajectories (Springer chapter)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-22957-7_10); [Elastic Statistical Shape Analysis of Biological Structures with Case Studies: A Tutorial, Bulletin of Mathematical Biology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11538-019-00609-w)

### Inferences
- Because the SRVF/elastic framework and Kendall's shape space are both instances of the general "Riemannian manifold + Fréchet mean + tangent-space PCA for modes of variation" pattern, the same three-step recipe (embed shapes in a metric space -> compute a Fréchet/Karcher mean -> compute a covariance/PCA of the log-map deviations from the mean) is the standard route to a statistical "normal range" for a population of curves; extending it to a whole tree of curves would most naturally require either (a) applying it independently per matched branch and reporting per-branch normal ranges, or (b) a genuinely joint tree-shape space (see Key Question 4), since I found no confirmed primary-source construction of a single SRVF-type Riemannian shape space whose points are entire labeled trees.
- The explicit invariance properties (translation via velocity-based representation, rotation and reparametrization via quotienting, scale via unit-length normalization) mean SRVF-based distances are close to ideal for comparing curve shape independent of how densely or where a curve was sampled — directly relevant to any application needing a resampling-robust distance between two curves' shapes (as opposed to the point-wise curvature/torsion statistics of Key Questions 1 and 2, which are more sensitive to parametrization/sampling unless carefully arc-length normalized).

## What methods in computational topology (persistent homology, Reeb graphs, merge trees, tree/graph edit distance) score the shape of a whole branching tree, and have any been used for a single robust shape/anomaly score of a tree relative to a reference population?

### Takeaway
Three complementary computational-topology routes exist for scoring a whole branching tree's shape (combining topology and geometry): (1) merge trees / Reeb graphs with edit distances or interleaving distances that are provably metrics with stability guarantees, (2) the Topological Morphology Descriptor (TMD), which converts an entire branching tree plus a chosen geometric descriptor function (e.g., distance from the root) into a single persistence diagram via an explicit birth/death algorithm at branch points, and (3) tree-space geometries (e.g., BHV space) that support Fréchet means and PCA directly on populations of trees; ensemble/outlier scoring of one tree against many reference trees has been explicitly done via pairwise merge-tree distances, and the TMD literature explicitly proves its descriptor is strictly more informative than a classical single-number tree-shape statistic (Sholl analysis), though I found no single named "the anomaly score" formula that is treated as the field's canonical choice.

### Cited Findings
- A merge tree captures the topology of sub-level (or super-level) sets of a scalar function defined on a domain (here, a tree of curves with a geometric function such as distance-from-root or arc-length-from-root assigned to every point); a Reeb graph generalizes this by an equivalence relation among points with equal function value in the same connected component of a level set, and the contour tree is the special case of a Reeb graph on a simply connected domain — so a merge tree/Reeb graph is exactly "topology plus geometry together" as requested, since its shape depends on both the tree's branching structure and the chosen geometric function's values along it. — [Scalar Field Comparison with Topological Descriptors: Properties and Applications for Scientific Visualization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.00157)
- A finitely stable edit distance for merge trees (Pegoraro, arXiv 2111.02738) defines an edit distance d_e(T,T') = inf over edit paths gamma in Gamma(T,T') of cost(gamma), where allowed edit operations on an edge-weighted tree are: (a) "shrinking" an edge's weight to a different positive value at cost equal to the absolute weight change; (b) deleting/inserting a leaf edge together with its lower vertex, at cost equal to that edge's weight; (c) "ghosting"/"splitting" order-two (degree-2) vertices at zero cost. The paper proves d_e is a genuine metric and is "finitely stable" relative to the (harder-to-compute) interleaving distance d_i, with the explicit bound d_e(T,G) <= 2*(dim(T)+dim(G)) * d_i(T,G), where dim denotes the tree's edge count — mirroring the known relationship between 1-Wasserstein and bottleneck distance for ordinary persistence diagrams. — [A Finitely Stable Edit Distance for Merge Trees, Pegoraro, arXiv:2111.02738 (2021)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02738) (equations and stability bound verified via full-text fetch of this primary source)
- This merge-tree edit-distance paper does not itself discuss anomaly detection or scoring a tree against a reference population; it is a pairwise-distance/metric-theory paper. — [A Finitely Stable Edit Distance for Merge Trees](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02738) (primary source, explicitly checked for this claim)
- Separately, ensemble analysis of merge trees is reported to support outlier detection by computing pairwise merge-tree distances across all pairs of trees in an ensemble (a population of trees), i.e., a tree's "outlierness" is derived from where it sits in the matrix of pairwise merge-tree distances to the rest of the ensemble, rather than from a distance to a single fixed reference tree. — [search-derived summary discussing ensemble merge-tree distance outlier detection](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.02919) (title: "Branch Decomposition-Independent Edit Distances for Merge Trees"; claim is at search-summary level, not independently verified against this paper's full text in this session)
- A distinct "geometry-aware" line of merge-tree comparison explicitly augments merge trees with additional geometric information beyond pure topology, to increase the descriptor's information content and to improve comparisons in real-world applications, and defines interleaving distances for these geometry-enriched merge trees. — [Geometry-Aware Merge Tree Comparisons for Time-Varying Data With Interleaving Distances](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.14373)
- The Topological Morphology Descriptor (TMD) converts a whole branching tree into a persistence diagram as follows (primary source verified via full-text fetch): a real-valued descriptor function f is defined on every point of the tree (e.g., f(x) = -||x - r|| using Euclidean distance from the root r, or a geodesic/path-length function g(x) giving total path length from the root to x). Sweeping sublevel sets T_t = {x in tree : f(x) <= t} as t increases, new connected components are "born" (e.g., at leaves/local minima of f) and, at a branching node s where two components with respective minima m1, m2 (f(m1) < f(m2)) merge, the younger component "dies": a persistence point (f(m2), f(s)) is added to the diagram, with persistence value |f(s) - f(m2)| representing that branch's "lifetime" in the descriptor function. Superlevel sets are processed symmetrically (top-down) to capture upward-forking structure, and the two resulting diagrams are merged into one overall diagram D_gf for the whole tree. — [Metrics for comparing neuronal tree shapes based on persistent homology, Kanari/Li/Wang/Ascoli/Mitra/Wang, PLOS ONE 12(8):e0182184 (2017)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0182184) (equations/algorithm verified via full-text fetch of this primary source; this method's motivating application area is neuronal tree morphology, i.e., a biological branching structure, flagged here per the scope note rather than expanded on)
- Two ways of turning the tree's persistence diagram D_gf into a distance/score between two trees are used: a (degree-1) Wasserstein-type distance, which finds a correspondence between the two diagrams' points minimizing a total (or maximum) matching cost; and a vectorized route, where the diagram is first converted into a smooth density function rho_D(x) = sum_i m_i * K_t(x_i, x), with m_i = |y_i - x_i| the persistence (lifetime) of point p_i = (x_i, y_i) and K_t a Gaussian kernel of bandwidth t, then this density is discretized on an evenly spaced grid into an m-dimensional vector so that ordinary L_p vector distances can be applied between two trees' descriptors. — [Metrics for comparing neuronal tree shapes based on persistent homology, PLOS ONE (2017)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0182184) (primary source verified)
- The same paper proves its persistence-diagram tree descriptor is strictly more informative than the classical single-function tree-shape statistic (Sholl analysis, the count N(r) of tree crossings of a sphere of radius r centered at the root): they show N(r) can be recovered by counting the diagram's persistence points lying in specific quadrants relative to r, minus one, meaning the (birth,death) persistence diagram of the whole tree contains strictly more shape information than the single-scalar-per-radius Sholl profile while still reducing to it as a special case. — [Metrics for comparing neuronal tree shapes based on persistent homology, PLOS ONE (2017)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0182184) (primary source verified); no single scalar summary statistic beyond the full persistence vector/diagram is defined in this paper for a single tree's "shape score" — the diagram or its vectorization is the comparison object, not a scalar.
- Beyond persistence-based tree comparison, phylogenetic-tree statistics offer a different, purely-geometric (non-persistence) tree shape space: BHV (Billera-Holmes-Vogtmann) tree space represents edge-weighted trees with a fixed leaf/label set as points in a space built from Euclidean orthants (one per tree topology), glued together so there is a unique geodesic between any two trees; BHV space is a CAT(0) space (globally non-positively curved), which is exactly the geometric property that makes Fréchet means, confidence regions for a population mean, and principal component analysis well-defined and computable for point clouds of trees; polynomial-time geodesic computation in BHV space was given by Owen and Provan (2011). — [search-derived summary of BHV tree space and its statistical machinery](https://www.imsi.institute/videos/expanding-statistics-in-bhv-phylogenetic-tree-space/); [A combinatorial method for connecting BHV spaces representing different numbers of taxa](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02626)

### Inferences
- The TMD construction is the clearest available answer to "a single robust shape/anomaly score for a whole tree relative to a reference population" among the sources found: because its output (a persistence diagram, or its Gaussian-kernel vectorization) lives in a fixed-dimensional vector space (once vectorized) or admits a Wasserstein metric, a population's "normal range" could in principle be built the same way as for single curves (Key Question 3) — a Fréchet mean diagram/vector plus a distribution of distances-to-mean — even though the retrieved primary source itself stops at defining pairwise distances and a single "more informative than Sholl" proof, not at defining a population mean or outlier threshold explicitly.
- BHV space is a genuinely different mechanism from persistence-diagram methods: it treats tree *topology plus edge lengths* as literally an orthant complex with its own Riemannian-like (CAT(0)) geometry, so Fréchet means/PCA are computed directly on labeled, edge-weighted trees rather than on a derived persistence summary — this is a second, largely independent route to a population-level tree shape space, but it presumes the trees being compared share a common leaf/branch labeling (as in phylogenetics), which would need explicit branch-correspondence/labeling work before it could be applied to a population of anatomical or other unlabeled branching trees.
- Combining the two: TMD-style persistence diagrams do not require solving a branch-correspondence problem across trees (a real strength for irregular branching structures), whereas BHV-style tree-space statistics require it but in exchange preserve full edge-length/topology information rather than compressing it into a persistence diagram — this is a genuine methodological trade-off the report writer may want to surface explicitly.

### Gaps
- I found no confirmed, primary-source-verified example of a single named "shape/anomaly score" formula (a scalar threshold or p-value-like statistic) built on top of merge-tree or TMD-style distances for scoring one tree against a population; the closest confirmed statement is that ensemble merge-tree pairwise distances have been used for outlier detection (search-summary level, not verified against that paper's full text), and that TMD distances are a valid metric between trees' descriptors without a stated outlier-threshold convention.
- I did not verify from primary text whether Reeb-graph-specific (as opposed to merge-tree-specific) edit or interleaving distances have been explicitly used for population-level anomaly scoring; the "labeled interleaving distance for Reeb graphs" result was found only at the search-summary level.
- Graph edit distance (the general, non-tree-specific NP-hard graph comparison) is confirmed in the network-anomaly-detection literature as a mechanism for scoring one graph against a reference/baseline graph via minimum-cost node/edge insertion, deletion, and substitution operations, but the specific cost-function choices used for network anomaly detection were not verified in exact mathematical form in this session (search-summary level only). — [Investigation of graph edit distance cost functions for detection of network anomalies (title/abstract only)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265482462_Investigation_of_graph_edit_distance_cost_functions_for_detection_of_network_anomalies)

## In robotics/motion-planning or CAD/CAM curve-fitting literature, what robust, resolution-invariant, or noise-robust curvature estimators exist for a piecewise-linear/point-cloud curve, and what do they recommend as a principled way to pick or avoid picking a single scale?

### Takeaway
The robotics/CAD/graphics literature offers several concrete responses to the same under-smoothing/over-smoothing tradeoff: robust point-cloud curvature estimators use M-estimator-style error kernels or randomized/corrected local integral measures instead of ordinary least-squares fitting so that neighborhood-size choice matters less; a distinct family (total-curvature and total-variation-of-the-normal-vector regularization) reframes curvature estimation as an optimization problem whose regularization weight substitutes for an explicit smoothing scale and is chosen to preserve sharp features rather than to fix a bandwidth; and a "scale invariance without scale selection" line of work argues that, when scale itself is the unwanted nuisance variable, the principled fix is to design the descriptor so it needs no scale parameter at all (via log-polar/Fourier constructions), rather than to pick a supposedly-correct single scale.

### Cited Findings
- Robust osculating-circle-based curvature estimation on point clouds: normal curvature at a point can be estimated by fitting an osculating circle (or, for surfaces, osculating spheres, via "normal section" circles) through the point and its neighbors; a documented robust algorithm ("Curvature estimation of 3D point cloud surfaces through the fitting of normal section curvatures") fits each normal-section circle using both position and normal information at neighboring points rather than position alone, which is reported to improve robustness. — [Curvature Estimation of 3D Point Cloud Surfaces Through the Fitting of Normal Section Curvatures](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/308557915_Curvature_estimation_of_3D_point_cloud_surfaces_through_the_fitting_of_normal_section_curvatures) (abstract/summary level)
- A more recent method, "Lightweight Curvature Estimation on Point Clouds with Randomized Corrected Curvature Measures" (Lachaud et al., Computer Graphics Forum 2023), is explicitly reported to be both accurate and robust to noise in practice, to require smaller neighborhoods than competing methods for the same accuracy, and to be faster in most practical cases — i.e., it directly targets reducing the sensitivity of the curvature estimate to the neighborhood-size (scale) choice by needing a smaller, more localized neighborhood while remaining accurate. — [Lightweight Curvature Estimation on Point Clouds with Randomized Corrected Curvature Measures, Computer Graphics Forum](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.14910) (abstract/summary level)
- "Robust and Accurate Curvature Estimation Using Adaptive Line Integrals" (EURASIP J. Adv. Signal Process., 2010) is a curvature estimator based on integral (not purely local/differential) measures along adaptively chosen line segments, aimed at improving both accuracy and robustness relative to naive finite-difference/derivative-based estimators; the adaptivity of the integration line length is itself a mechanism for responding to local noise level rather than fixing one global scale. — [Robust and Accurate Curvature Estimation Using Adaptive Line Integrals](https://asp-eurasipjournals.springeropen.com/articles/10.1155/2010/240309) (title/summary level)
- On the "avoid picking one scale" side, image/surface curvature regularization methods reframe curvature estimation as an optimization rather than a filtering choice: "Minimizing Discrete Total Curvature for Image Processing" (Zhong et al., CVPR 2020) and "Discrete Total Variation of the Normal Vector Field as Shape Prior with Applications in Geometric Inverse Problems" both regularize using a discrete total-curvature or total-variation-of-normals functional whose minimization recovers sharp corners/edges while suppressing noise, i.e., the tradeoff is controlled by a regularization weight balancing data fidelity against the curvature/TV penalty, rather than by a smoothing bandwidth applied before curvature is computed. — [Minimizing Discrete Total Curvature for Image Processing](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/papers/Zhong_Minimizing_Discrete_Total_Curvature_for_Image_Processing_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf); [Discrete Total Variation of the Normal Vector Field as Shape Prior with Applications in Geometric Inverse Problems](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.07916) (both title/summary level)
- A weighted total-variation model using mean curvature is reported to "robustly adjust the balance between noise reduction and edge preservation," combining first-order TV regularization (which alone tends to over-flatten smooth regions, "staircasing") with second-order curvature information specifically to reduce sensitivity to the amount of smoothing applied. — [A weighted total variation based image denoising model using mean curvature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0030402620307762) (title/summary level)
- On avoiding scale selection altogether: "Scale Invariance Without Scale Selection" (Kokkinos & Yuille, CVPR 2008) explicitly frames unreliable scale selection as the problem to be designed around, and constructs scale-invariant descriptors using a combination of log-polar sampling and spatially varying smoothing that converts a scale change in the input into a translation in a transformed domain, then achieves invariance by taking the modulus of a Fourier transform (which is translation-invariant) — i.e. the recommended principled fix, when this is applicable, is not to estimate/select the best scale at all, but to engineer the representation so that the answer does not depend on scale in the first place. — [Scale Invariance Without Scale Selection, Kokkinos & Yuille (ML Anthology entry summarizing the CVPR 2008 paper)](https://mlanthology.org/cvpr/2008/kokkinos2008cvpr-scale/)
- A complementary, more classical menu of scale-selection heuristics (as opposed to scale-avoidance) from the scale-space literature includes: selecting the scale at a local extremum, over scale, of some normalized differential invariant; post-smoothing after computing a nonlinear differential invariant (to stabilize the invariant before choosing its extremum); and scale selection via a weighted average of scale values along a feature's trajectory as that feature is tracked continuously across scale (rather than a single scale evaluation) — this last option is conceptually close to the CSS approach of Key Question 2, in that it exploits the whole across-scale trajectory rather than one scale. — [search-derived summary of scale-selection mechanisms in the scale-space literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.11317)
- A "similarity curvature," described as a new scale-invariant curvature measure, has been proposed specifically because traditional curvature measures are reported to lack scale invariance. — [A Scale Invariant Surface Curvature Estimator](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/bitstreams/a9d357c6-0a6d-4412-94bd-a066c3b1188a/download) (title/summary level; this source is for surfaces, not curves, so its exact applicability to a 1D curve/tree-of-curves setting is unverified)

### Inferences
- Across this literature there are, in effect, three distinct principled responses to "which scale should I use," none of which is "pick the single best sigma by eye": (i) make the local estimator itself robust/adaptive so a reasonable range of neighborhood sizes gives similar answers (the randomized-corrected-measures and adaptive-line-integral methods); (ii) replace filtering-then-differentiating with a global regularized optimization where a regularization weight, not a smoothing radius, controls the sharp/smooth tradeoff and is typically chosen by standard model-selection means (e.g., cross-validation, an L-curve, or discrepancy principle, though I did not verify a specific rule from primary text here); (iii) design the descriptor to not need a scale parameter at all (Fourier-modulus-based scale invariance), which sidesteps rather than answers the "which scale" question.
- Option (ii) (regularization-based curvature estimation) and the CSS/wavelet multiscale approach of Key Question 2 are two different resolutions of the same underlying tension: CSS keeps sigma explicit and reports the whole family of answers across sigma, whereas total-curvature/TV regularization collapses the tradeoff into one automatically-balanced optimum; a tortuosity descriptor for a branching tree could combine both, e.g. reporting a CSS-style curve across scales for exploratory/diagnostic purposes while using a regularized estimator for a single reproducible operating point.
