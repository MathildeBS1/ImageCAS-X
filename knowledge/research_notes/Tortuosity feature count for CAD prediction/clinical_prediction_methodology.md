# Clinical Prediction Model Methodology: Single vs Multiple Predictors, Sample Size, and Overfitting

## What do events-per-variable (EPV) guidelines say about how many predictors a model can safely include, and what happens when the ratio is violated?

### Takeaway
The classic rule of "at least 10 events per variable" (Peduzzi et al.) is now considered outdated and overly simplistic; the current methodological consensus (Riley, van Smeden, Steyerberg) replaces it with model-based sample-size calculations targeting explicit overfitting criteria (shrinkage ≥0.9, small optimism in R²), and empirically shows that violating EPV thresholds causes convergence failure, biased coefficients, and optimistic performance estimates that do not replicate on new data.

### Cited Findings
- The traditional rule of thumb is a minimum of 10 events per candidate predictor (variable) considered for inclusion, counted before any variable selection is performed — [Statistical primer: sample size considerations for developing and validating clinical prediction models, PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- Riley et al. (2019, *Statistics in Medicine*) propose that minimum sample size (n) and number of events (E) should be calculated to jointly satisfy three criteria: (i) small optimism in predictor effect estimates, defined as a global shrinkage factor ≥0.9 (i.e., ≤10% overfitting); (ii) a small absolute difference (≤0.05) between the model's apparent and optimism-adjusted Nagelkerke's R²; and (iii) precise estimation of the overall outcome risk (event rate) in the population — [Riley et al. 2019, Statistics in Medicine, sim.7992](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/sim.7992); confirmed by [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- Steyerberg et al. showed that at least 10-20 EPV is needed to keep shrinkage above 0.9 (limit overfitting), but roughly 20 EPV is needed for an accurate estimate of the c-statistic itself — [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- Recent guidance states that EPV ≥ 20 should be used when the predictor set includes low-prevalence binary predictors, superseding the flat EPV ≥ 10 rule — [van Smeden et al., "Adequate sample size for developing prediction models is not simply related to events per variable", PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)
- In van Smeden et al.'s simulations, at EPV = 2 with a 12-predictor logistic model, only 7 of 1,000 simulated models even converged, illustrating catastrophic instability at very low EPV — [PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)
- EPV = 10 was sufficient to eliminate coefficient bias in 3- and 5-predictor models, but EPV = 20 was required to reach the same low-bias level once the model had 7, 10, or 12 predictors — i.e., the "safe" EPV threshold rises as the number of candidate predictors rises — [PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)
- At EPV = 10, external validation performance was described as "clearly a poor choice," i.e. models developed at EPV=10 degraded materially on new data despite looking acceptable in-sample — [PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)
- When EPV cannot reach the recommended threshold, van Smeden et al. recommend penalized-likelihood approaches (e.g., Firth's method) to reduce small-sample bias rather than simply proceeding with maximum-likelihood estimation — [PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)
- The number of "candidate predictor parameters" that counts against the EPV/sample-size budget includes categorical variable expansions (e.g., a 3-level NYHA class variable counts as multiple parameters) and any interaction or nonlinear transformation terms considered, not just the final variables retained in the published model — [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- Worked example from the primer: reducing the number of candidate predictors from 25 to 15 lowered the calculated minimum required sample size from 9,658 to 5,795 participants (at the specified target events, 174), showing quantitatively how each additional candidate predictor raises the sample size floor — [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- For external validation (as opposed to development), a widely cited older rule of thumb was a minimum of 100 events and 100 non-events (Vergouwe et al. 2005), but this blanket guidance is now considered "imprecise and not specific to the model or validation setting," with more recent work (Riley 2021, Collins & Riley 2016, Pavlou et al. 2021) providing simulation- or formula-based sample-size targets instead — [Vergouwe/related discussion, PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/); [Riley 2021 Statistics in Medicine, sim.9025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/sim.9025); [Collins & Riley 2016, sim.6787](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sim.6787)

### Inferences
- The practical implication for a modest cohort (as in most single-center or single-dataset imaging studies) is that every additional candidate predictor considered — even ones later discarded — inflates the required sample size superlinearly once more than a handful of predictors are in play, because the EPV threshold itself needs to rise (10 to 20) as predictor count rises.
- A model with a small number of predictors (e.g., 3-5) is defensible at EPV=10 in cohorts with modest event counts, whereas a model with a large panel of correlated features (e.g., many tortuosity descriptors) may need EPV=20, i.e., double the events, to reach the same overfitting control.

### Gaps
- I did not find a source giving a precise formula-free "maximum predictor count" for a specific event count typical of CAD imaging cohorts (e.g., n=500-1000, moderate event rate); Riley's calculators require inputs (anticipated C-statistic/R², outcome prevalence) not looked up here.

## What does TRIPOD say about predictor selection, apparent vs. internally-validated performance, and the danger of adding predictors without external validation?

### Takeaway
TRIPOD (and its Explanation & Elaboration paper) treats "apparent" performance measured on the development data as inherently optimistic when predictor selection and estimation happen on the same data, and mandates internal validation (bootstrap optimism correction being the most common method) as a required, not optional, part of reporting; it also requires transparent reporting of exactly how many candidate predictors were considered and how selection was done, precisely because both drivers of optimism (too few events per predictor, and predictor-selection procedures) are otherwise invisible to readers.

### Cited Findings
- TRIPOD is a 22-item reporting checklist (with a companion Explanation & Elaboration paper) governing studies that develop, validate, or update prediction models for diagnosis or prognosis — [TRIPOD Statement, Annals of Internal Medicine](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/m14-0697); [TRIPOD E&E, Annals of Internal Medicine](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M14-0698)
- Quantifying a model's predictive ability on the same data used to develop it tends to give an optimistic estimate of performance, due to (a) overfitting from too few outcome events relative to the number of candidate predictors, and (b) the predictor-selection strategy itself (e.g., stepwise selection) — [TRIPOD-related summary, search synthesis citing TRIPOD E&E](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M14-0698)
- Overfitting is defined as the tendency of a model to capture random variation specific to the development dataset, and TRIPOD guidance states this is worse when sample size is insufficient for the number of parameters being estimated — [TRIPOD E&E synthesis](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M14-0698)
- TRIPOD requires that development studies using the same data to both build and evaluate a model also report an internal-validation procedure that produces an optimism-corrected performance estimate; bootstrapping (particularly Harrell's bootstrap optimism-correction) is described as the most popular such method — [TRIPOD E&E synthesis](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M14-0698)
- More than 5 percentage points of optimism in R² or 0.05 AUC units between apparent and optimism-corrected performance is treated as a signal of meaningful overfitting, and optimism exceeding ~20% relative is treated as evidence the model is too complex for the available sample — [search synthesis of optimism-correction literature](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12874-020-01201-w)
- Three bootstrap-based bias-correction methods are commonly used and compared in the literature: Harrell's bootstrap optimism correction, the .632 estimator, and the .632+ estimator; simulation evidence suggests the .632+ estimator performs comparably or better than Harrell's method, especially in small samples, though results are broadly comparable across methods except under regularized (e.g., LASSO) estimation — [Re-evaluation of bootstrap-based optimism correction methods, BMC Med Res Methodol 2020](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12874-020-01201-w)
- Penalization/shrinkage methods (ridge, LASSO, Firth) intended to control overfitting can themselves become unreliable — "penalization and shrinkage methods produced unreliable clinical prediction models, especially when sample size was small" — implying that reaching for a regularizer is not an automatic fix for an EPV shortfall in genuinely small cohorts — [Journal of Clinical Epidemiology, "Penalization and shrinkage methods produced unreliable clinical prediction models"](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(20)31209-9/fulltext)

### Inferences
- TRIPOD's logic implies that reporting only apparent (in-sample) AUC/C-statistic for a multi-predictor model, without internal validation, is specifically the reporting failure mode TRIPOD was created to stop; any comparison of a single-predictor vs multi-predictor model that only reports apparent performance is methodologically incomplete by TRIPOD's own standard.
- Because optimism grows with the number of candidate predictors relative to events (per the EPV literature above), TRIPOD's insistence on optimism-corrected performance is functionally the same concern as the EPV guidance, viewed from the reporting side rather than the design side.

- I could not access the full TRIPOD E&E text directly (403 Forbidden on acpjournals.org); statements above rely on a secondary synthesis of that paper's content rather than my own read of primary text, and I was not able to independently verify TRIPOD's exact wording on predictor-selection methods (e.g., its specific stance on stepwise selection vs. pre-specification) beyond the summary retrieved. ## Empirical relationship between number of predictors and discrimination (AUC/C-statistic) gain: diminishing returns or overfitting?

### Takeaway
There is a well-documented ceiling/diminishing-returns effect: once a baseline model already discriminates well, each additional predictor or biomarker produces a progressively smaller AUC/C-statistic gain, and the C-statistic itself is a blunt, sometimes-misleading tool for judging whether a new predictor is worth adding; a concrete case example (CVD screening) shows a large jump in model complexity (age alone vs full multivariable Framingham-style scoring) buying only ~0.01-0.02 AUC and a few percentage points of false-positive-rate improvement.

### Cited Findings
- "Stronger or more numerous predictors are needed to achieve the same increment in the AUC for baseline models with good versus poor discrimination" — i.e., the marginal AUC gain from an additional predictor shrinks as the baseline model's AUC rises — [search synthesis of C-statistic discrimination literature](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Relationship-between-change-in-c-statistic-and-c-statistic-of-univariate-model-binary_fig8_230826629)
- "The increment in C obtained by adding a new biomarker to a predictive model has no direct interpretation, and the relevance of the C-statistic to risk stratification is not obvious" — the AUC/C-statistic is described in the methodological literature as an insensitive and sometimes non-intuitive metric for evaluating whether a newly added predictor is clinically useful — [search synthesis, C-statistic added-value literature](https://www.bohrium.com/en/sciencepedia/feynman/statistical_modeling_in_medicine_graduate-assessing_model_discrimination_the_c-statistic)
- When a baseline model already has very good discrimination (C-statistic around 0.9), the C-statistic can only increase slightly toward its ceiling of 1.0, but can decrease much further if a poorly-chosen predictor is added — a structural ceiling effect that mechanically produces "diminishing returns" for discrimination metrics regardless of a predictor's true value — [search synthesis, C-statistic ceiling-effect literature](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/eci.70150)
- Direct quantitative case: a simulation-based comparison of CVD screening using age alone (cutoff 55 years) versus 5-yearly Framingham multivariable screening (8% 10-year risk cutoff), run on 500,000 simulated individuals, found nearly identical detection performance: 86% detection rate and 72% CVD-free-life-years detected for both approaches, with false-positive rates of 24% (age alone) vs 21% (Framingham); AUC was 0.89 for age screening vs 0.90 (five-yearly) to 0.91 (annual) for full Framingham screening — an AUC gain of only 0.01-0.02 from adding the full multivariable risk-factor panel on top of age — [Screening for Future Cardiovascular Disease Using Age Alone Compared with Multiple Risk Factors and Age, PMC3087724](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3087724/)
- The same paper's authors concluded that "age screening for future CVD events is simpler than Framingham screening with a similar screening performance," and that adding the full multivariable panel provided only marginal discrimination improvement over age alone — [PMC3087724](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3087724/)
- Coronary artery calcium (CAC) score added to traditional multivariable risk models is reported to produce a net reclassification improvement (NRI) of 0.24, illustrating that reclassification-based metrics can show a more clinically visible incremental effect for a single added predictor than the AUC/C-statistic does, which is part of why the literature recommends NRI/reclassification and decision-curve analysis as complements to (not replacements for) AUC when judging whether to add a predictor — [search synthesis, CAC incremental-value literature](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4731459/)

### Inferences
- Together, the age-vs-Framingham case and the general ceiling-effect literature support a specific, quantifiable pattern: once one or two strong predictors (e.g., age, or a single dominant anatomical severity measure) already achieve AUC ≈ 0.85-0.90, adding many more correlated predictors to a modest cohort is unlikely to move discrimination by more than ~0.01-0.03 AUC, and any such small apparent gain is exactly the range that optimism/overfitting (per the EPV section) can produce even with zero true signal — meaning an apparent AUC bump of this size, without internal or external validation, cannot be distinguished from overfitting artifact.
- This directly bears on any argument for adding many tortuosity-derived features to a CAD-prediction model: the discrimination ceiling effect means the "honest" test of value is not apparent in-sample AUC gain but optimism-corrected AUC gain and/or reclassification metrics (NRI), evaluated with a sample size adequate for the added parameter count (per the EPV section).

### Gaps
- I did not find a systematic review directly plotting "number of predictors" against "AUC gain" across many published CVD prediction models in one figure/table (i.e., a meta-analysis of this specific relationship); the evidence assembled here is from mechanistic/statistical-property papers (ceiling effects, C-statistic insensitivity) plus one concrete case study (age vs Framingham), not a dedicated empirical survey across many models.

## How does this differ for a model predicting a FUTURE outcome in a currently healthy population vs. classifying PRESENT disease? Are there specific warnings about applying present-disease models to future-risk prediction?

### Takeaway
The methodological literature (van Smeden, Moons, Riley, Collins) draws a formal, named distinction between "diagnostic" (present-disease, T=0) and "prognostic" (future-outcome, T>0) prediction models, treats them as requiring different sample-size formulas and different predictor/outcome-ascertainment considerations, and implicitly (via TRIPOD's separate handling of the two types) warns against conflating the two settings; I found strong general framing for this distinction but did not find an explicit, quotable statement specifically warning against reusing a model trained on present-disease classification for future-risk prediction.

### Cited Findings
- Diagnostic prediction models estimate an individual's probability that a disease is already present at the moment of prediction (T=0); prognostic prediction models estimate the probability of a specific health state occurring within a future time period (T>0) — the "main difference... is the concept of time" — [Clinical prediction models: diagnosis versus prognosis, van Smeden/Reitsma/Riley/Collins/Moons 2021, J Clin Epidemiol 132:142-145](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(21)00013-5/fulltext); [PubMed 33775387](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33775387/)
- In the diagnostic setting, the estimated probability is used to inform referral for further testing, direct initiation of treatment, or reassurance that a serious cause is unlikely; in the prognostic setting, predictions are used to plan lifestyle or therapeutic decisions based on future outcome risk over a specified time horizon — [search synthesis of TRIPOD/diagnosis-vs-prognosis literature](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/full/10.7326/M14-0697)
- The 2021 van Smeden et al. paper explicitly derives separate minimal sample-size formulas for diagnostic versus prognostic model development (with accompanying R/Stata software), rather than treating one set of sample-size rules as applicable to both — [Clinical prediction models: diagnosis versus prognosis, J Clin Epidemiol 2021](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(21)00013-5/fulltext)
- TRIPOD itself is explicitly named for "Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis," and structures its 22-item checklist and explanatory guidance to cover both model types together while flagging where reporting requirements differ (e.g., outcome ascertainment, time horizon, handling of censoring for prognosis) — [TRIPOD Statement](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circulationaha.114.014508)

### Inferences
- Because a future-risk (prognostic) model in a currently healthy population necessarily has a lower event rate than a present-disease (diagnostic) classification study of symptomatic patients, the EPV/sample-size arithmetic above means a prognostic study needs a much larger total cohort to reach the same number of events, and thus the same predictor-count budget, as a diagnostic study — a low event rate does not just weaken statistical power in the abstract, it directly shrinks the number of predictors that can be safely included per the Riley/EPV criteria.
- The formal diagnosis/prognosis distinction in the literature (separate sample-size formulas, separate TRIPOD emphasis) supports treating a model validated for present-disease classification as not automatically transportable to future-risk prediction: the predictors' associations with "disease present now" (which may reflect reverse causation, e.g., advanced anatomical remodeling that is itself downstream of disease) are not guaranteed to hold for "disease will develop by time T," and the EPV/optimism machinery required for one setting is calibrated to that setting's own event rate and horizon, not the other's.

### Gaps
- I was not able to locate a paper making an explicit, quotable warning of the specific form "a model built to classify present disease should not be applied unmodified to predict future risk" (i.e., a direct methodological caution against this specific reuse, as opposed to the general diagnostic/prognostic conceptual distinction). This may exist in the prognosis-research literature (e.g., Riley's PROGRESS series on prognosis research) but was not surfaced in the searches run in this session; flagging as a genuine gap rather than inferring a citation that does not exist. If this specific warning is important to the report, further targeted searching (e.g., "reverse causation cross-sectional predictor prognostic model", "PROGRESS framework prognosis research Riley Hemingway") is recommended.
- I could not access the full text of the van Smeden 2021 J Clin Epidemiol paper directly (403 error) and relied on abstract-level/secondary summaries; the specific sample-size formulas it proposes for diagnostic vs prognostic settings were not independently extracted.

## What role does interpretability play in the tradeoff between a single interpretable score and a multivariate/black-box model, in clinical adoption?

### Takeaway
The literature consistently frames interpretability as a major, sometimes decisive, factor in clinical adoption independent of raw discriminative performance: simple, transparent scoring systems are reported to be trusted and integrated into workflow more readily than black-box models, and several papers explicitly note that added model complexity does not reliably translate into better real-world performance, even though black-box models can technically achieve higher apparent discrimination.

### Cited Findings
- "Data-based 'white-box' models are intuitive and implementable within existing clinical workflows but are often outperformed by 'black-box' models that exploit complex variable relationships. However, black-box models have advanced computational requirements and implementation barriers, necessitating careful consideration of the performance-interpretability tradeoff" — [search synthesis, interpretable clinical risk-score literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9931273/)
- "Complex and robust machine learning models do not necessarily outperform simple scoring models" — directly challenging the assumption that more predictors / more model complexity yields better real-world performance — [search synthesis, interpretability/performance tradeoff literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21934)
- "Complex machine learning methods usually have better performance, but they obtain little approval from physicians, as they lack interpretability and, therefore, clinical confidence" — framing interpretability as a precondition for clinician trust and thus for adoption, separate from statistical performance — [search synthesis, ACS risk-prediction interpretability literature](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351567371_A_New_Approach_for_Interpretability_and_Reliability_in_Clinical_Risk_Prediction_Acute_Coronary_Syndrome_Scenario)
- A transparent, auditable scoring system that clinical staff can understand and trust is described as facilitating adoption, with the explicit goal in some published tools (e.g., the VOCAL-Penn score) being "substantial improvements over current tools... while preserving interpretability and seamless integration into existing clinical workflows" — [Five essential features for adoption of clinical risk prediction tools: Insights from the VOCAL-Penn score, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12668580/)

### Inferences
- The interpretability literature converges with the statistical (EPV/overfitting) literature toward the same practical conclusion from two independent directions: statistically, a small, modest-cohort dataset supports few reliable predictors (per EPV rules); clinically, a small predictor set is also what clinicians are more willing to trust and adopt. Both considerations favor a compact, well-justified predictor set over an expansive one, especially for a "future outcome, currently healthy population" prediction task where event counts are inherently limited.

### Gaps
- I did not find a study directly quantifying, in a controlled comparison, "how much discrimination clinicians are willing to trade for interpretability" (e.g., a stated-preference or adoption-rate study with numeric results); the sources found are qualitative/narrative statements of the tradeoff rather than a quantitative preference elicitation. The VOCAL-Penn source names "five essential features for adoption" but I did not fetch its full text to list what all five are, only that interpretability/integration is one of them.
