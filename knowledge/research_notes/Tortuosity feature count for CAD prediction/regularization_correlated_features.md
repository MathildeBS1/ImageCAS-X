# Statistical methods for combining correlated imaging/geometric features under limited sample size

## What regularization/shrinkage methods (LASSO, ridge, elastic net) are standard, and how do they treat correlated predictors specifically?

### Takeaway
LASSO is the de facto standard feature-selector in radiomics/imaging prediction modeling, but it handles groups of correlated predictors poorly and arbitrarily: it tends to pick one member of a correlated cluster and zero out the rest, which is unstable across resamples. Elastic net (Zou & Hastie, 2005) was designed specifically to fix this via its "grouping effect," and group-penalty variants exist for when correlated features are known to cluster.

### Cited Findings
- LASSO has been the standard method for feature selection in high-dimensional radiomics data, reducing many candidate features into a smaller radiomics signature — [Radiomics-Based Prediction of Treatment Response in NSCLC](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics16142261)
- LASSO is "challenged by the presence of highly correlated features"; when predictors are highly correlated, LASSO tends to select only one feature from the correlated group and ignore the others, failing to use the collective information in the group — [search synthesis citing radiomics LASSO literature](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Radiomics-feature-selection-by-using-the-LASSO-regression-method-The-optimal-a-was_fig4_343221966); see also theoretical treatment in [How Correlations Influence Lasso Prediction (arXiv:1204.1605)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.1605)
- Zou, H. & Hastie, T. (2005), "Regularization and Variable Selection via the Elastic Net," *J. R. Statist. Soc. B* 67(2):301-320, introduced the elastic net as a convex combination of the L1 (lasso) and L2 (ridge) penalties specifically to address lasso's poor behavior with correlated/grouped predictors — [Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B](https://academic.oup.com/jrsssb/article/67/2/301/7109482)
- The elastic net has a "grouping effect": strongly correlated predictors tend to be in or out of the model together, and the paper gives a quantitative bound on how close the coefficients of two highly correlated predictors will be (when their sample coefficients have the same sign) — [On grouping effect of elastic net, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167715213001752)
- Plain lasso does not have this grouping property; the elastic net's combination of penalties is what "encourages the grouping effect and has the advantage of including highly correlated variables automatically in the group" rather than arbitrarily discarding all but one — [On grouping effect of elastic net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267465100_On_grouping_effect_of_elastic_net)
- Group-penalty models (e.g., group lasso variants) have been compared specifically as a strategy for radiomics feature selection in lung adenocarcinoma and meningioma cohorts, framed as an alternative to plain lasso when features are expected to cluster — [Kwon et al., Quantitative Imaging in Medicine and Surgery](https://qims.amegroups.org/article/view/148703/html) (title/framing verified; full methodological detail not extracted in this pass — see Gaps)
- A "nonnegative garrote" approach has also been proposed as an alternative for prediction modeling with many correlated and zero-inflated predictors — [Prediction modelling with many correlated and zero-inflated predictors (arXiv:2402.02064)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02064)
- A clustering/variable-selection approach for highly correlated predictors in high-dimensional genomic data has been proposed as a further alternative when correlated blocks of features are expected — [A variable selection approach for highly correlated predictors in high-dimensional genomic data (arXiv:2007.10768)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.10768)

### Inferences
- For a small set of a-priori-chosen, moderately-to-highly correlated geometric features (e.g., a handful of tortuosity/curvature metrics rather than thousands of radiomics texture features), elastic net is the more defensible default over plain LASSO precisely because the point of including several correlated descriptors is usually to keep the informative ones together rather than let an arbitrary one "win" by chance in a given resample.
- Because plain LASSO's selection among correlated features is unstable (which specific feature is retained can flip across bootstrap/CV folds), stability selection or bootstrap-aggregated LASSO frequency counts are a natural complement when reporting which correlated feature "wins" is itself scientifically interesting.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Kwon et al. group-penalty paper's full methodology (only title/venue/topic were surfaced by search); a full read is needed before citing specific comparative results (e.g., AUC differences between group lasso and standard lasso).
- No paper was found that gives a fixed quantitative correlation threshold above which switching from LASSO to elastic net is recommended; the guidance found is qualitative ("high correlation" triggers concern) rather than a specific r cutoff.

## How is multicollinearity typically diagnosed (VIF, correlation matrices) and handled in imaging-biomarker/radiomics studies before predictors are combined?

### Takeaway
The two standard diagnostics are pairwise correlation/redundancy filtering (Pearson or Spearman, typically clustering or dropping one of any pair above a threshold commonly in the 0.7-0.95 range) applied before feature selection, and variance inflation factor (VIF) computed on the retained/candidate set, with VIF > 5 or VIF > 10 the most commonly cited action thresholds. Stepwise regression is explicitly called out in the methodology literature as an inappropriate way to "handle" multicollinearity.

### Cited Findings
- VIF = 1 indicates no multicollinearity; VIF values 1-5 are considered acceptable ("okay"); VIF values 5-10 "need a closer look"; VIF above 5 or 10 (context-dependent) suggests the predictor is redundant given the others and is a common trigger for removing a variable — [Understanding Variance Inflation Factor in Clinical Research](https://editverse.com/understanding-variance-inflation-factor-in-clinical-research/); consistent with [Model-Dependent Variance Inflation Factor Cutoff Values](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233368189_Model-Dependent_Variance_Inflation_Factor_Cutoff_Values) and the `performance` R package's `check_collinearity` documentation — [easystats performance package](https://easystats.github.io/performance/reference/check_collinearity.html)
- A stricter convention also appears in the literature: VIF < 3 is described as indicating low correlation among variables "under ideal conditions," i.e., some fields use a more conservative cutoff than the common 5/10 rule — [search synthesis of VIF literature](https://onlinehelp.ihs.com/Energy/AnalyticsExplorer/AE_9.0/Webhelp/Content/AnalyticsExplorer/VIF.htm)
- Using stepwise regression as a method to "address" multicollinearity is explicitly flagged as methodologically inappropriate in the clinical prediction modeling literature — [Using stepwise regression to address multicollinearity is not appropriate, PMC11093476](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11093476/)
- Pearson/Spearman correlation-based redundancy filtering is heavily used as an initial step in radiomics pipelines; reported thresholds in the literature range from >0.7 to >0.9-0.95, and the field lacks a single consistent convention — [Feature selection methods and predictive models in CT lung cancer radiomics](https://aapm.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/acm2.13869) / [PMC9860004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9860004/)
- In a direct multicollinearity study of a PET radiomic feature set, cluster analysis at a Spearman threshold of rho = 0.9 retained 35 non-redundant features, and at rho = 0.7 retained only 15; the authors concluded "at least two-thirds of the radiomic feature set could be omitted because of strong multicollinearity" — [Multicollinearity and redundancy of the PET radiomic feature set, PubMed 40332568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40332568/)
- The common two-step pipeline described across radiomics methodology papers is: (1) correlation-based redundancy pruning (drop/cluster features above a correlation threshold) as a pre-filter, then (2) LASSO or another penalized/embedded method applied to the reduced, less-collinear set — [search synthesis of radiomics feature-selection methodology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9860004/)

### Inferences
- For a small, hand-picked set of geometric features (rather than thousands of radiomics texture features), a defensible sequence is: (1) report the full pairwise correlation matrix of candidate features up front (this is cheap and directly answers "how correlated are these, exactly"), (2) compute VIF on the candidate set and flag/merge or drop anything above 5 (conservative) or 10 (permissive), then (3) let elastic net (or equivalent) do final shrinkage rather than doing manual stepwise elimination, which the methodology literature treats as an invalid way to resolve collinearity.
- Because there is no single agreed correlation or VIF cutoff across the literature (0.7-0.95 for correlation; 3, 5, or 10 for VIF depending on source), whatever thresholds are used should be stated explicitly and treated as a reported analytic choice, not an objective standard.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a threshold specific to vascular/geometric (as opposed to texture) imaging features; the VIF and correlation thresholds above come from general clinical-prediction and radiomics-texture literature, and may not have been validated for tortuosity/curvature-type descriptors specifically.

## What dimensionality-reduction or feature-combination strategies (PCA, composite scores, CV-based selection) are used in radiomics/quantitative imaging when many correlated features exist but sample size is limited?

### Takeaway
PCA is a recognized standard for collapsing sets of highly correlated quantitative imaging features into a smaller number of uncorrelated components, precisely because radiomics-style datasets are typically "large-p, small-n" (more candidate features than samples/events), which independently increases overfitting risk; but feature selection or dimensionality reduction must be nested inside cross-validation, not performed once on the full dataset, or performance estimates become severely biased.

### Cited Findings
- Radiomics datasets are described as "particularly prone to overfitting" because the number of extracted features often exceeds the number of samples ("large-p, small-n"), and PCA is named as one of the standard strategies (alongside other feature-reduction methods) to address this — [Principal Component Analysis Applied to Radiomics Data, MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/12/24/7731) / [PMC10743692](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10743692/)
- PCA is explicitly framed as a technique for "reducing the dimensionality of large datasets containing highly correlated variables... by creating new uncorrelated variables that successively maximize variance," which is the standard justification for applying it to correlated texture/geometric feature sets — [PMC10743692](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10743692/)
- A meta-research review of radiomics prediction models for binary outcomes found the field is systematically "overlooked and underpowered," i.e., sample sizes in published radiomics prediction studies are frequently inadequate relative to the number of candidate features/model complexity used — [Overlooked and underpowered: a meta-research addressing sample size in radiomics prediction models for binary outcomes, European Radiology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00330-024-11331-0)
- Feature selection must be performed inside each cross-validation fold, not once on the whole dataset before CV, or the resulting performance estimate is optimistically biased; applying feature selection before CV on all the data was identified as a direct cause of data leakage — [Measuring the bias of incorrect application of feature selection when using cross-validation in radiomics, Insights into Imaging](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13244-021-01115-1) (title/topic confirmed via search synthesis; full text was behind an access wall in this session — see Gaps)
- Incorrect (leaky) application of feature selection prior to cross-validation was reported to produce a positive performance bias described as up to roughly 0.15 in AUC-ROC and comparable inflation in other metrics in that study — [search synthesis of Insights into Imaging leakage study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13244-021-01115-1) (numeric values reported via search-result synthesis, not independently confirmed against the primary text in this session — treat as approximate; see Gaps)
- A newer factorial study decomposing sources of "analytical leakage" in radiomics pipelines found that leaked feature-selection decisions drive most of the resulting performance inflation, and that on real (non-synthetic) data, leaking feature selection could even lower generalization performance rather than just inflating the reported in-sample metric — [Decomposing performance inflation in radiomics: factorial quantification of analytical leakage](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42114379/)
- The recommended leakage-free protocol is that data splitting must precede all preprocessing and feature-selection steps, which must then be executed independently inside each training fold before evaluation on the corresponding held-out fold — [search synthesis of radiomics leakage literature](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8613324/)
- A related paper specifically measures "the optimistic bias of cross-validation in radiomics" as a distinct phenomenon from feature-selection leakage — [Measuring the optimistic bias of cross-validation in radiomics, PMC13385755](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13385755/) (topic confirmed by title/search snippet only; not independently fetched in full)

### Inferences
- For a study with a handful of correlated geometric features and a small/modest number of future-CAD events, PCA (or a simple, pre-specified composite score built from a fixed, documented weighting of the correlated features) is a lower-risk alternative to per-feature LASSO selection, because it sidesteps the "which correlated feature got picked this fold" instability entirely and is easier to keep leakage-free (PCA loadings fit inside each training fold only, then applied to the held-out fold).
- Whatever selection/reduction method is used (correlation pruning, VIF pruning, PCA, LASSO/elastic net), the single non-negotiable methodological requirement repeated across this literature is that the method must be fit only on training folds and reapplied (not refit) to held-out folds; doing it once on the whole dataset before splitting is the single most consistently flagged error.

### Gaps
- The two Springer-hosted papers directly quantifying leakage bias (Insights into Imaging 2021 piece and the PMC13385755 CV-optimism piece) were not fully retrievable in this session (login-wall redirect and a bot-check page respectively); the specific numeric bias values above should be verified against the primary text before being reported as precise figures in the thesis (I flagged this uncertainty explicitly rather than treating the numbers as confirmed).
- No radiomics/imaging paper was found that reports a formal comparison of PCA versus a simple composite (e.g., equally-weighted sum or clinically-motivated weighting) versus elastic net on the same small-sample dataset; the relative merits above are inferred from general methodology sources, not a head-to-head empirical study.

## What do RQS, TRIPOD/TRIPOD+AI, and PROBAST say about feature-selection procedures, specifically about outcome-driven selection on the same data used for evaluation?

### Takeaway
All three frameworks converge on the same core warning: selecting or ranking predictors by their association with the outcome in the same dataset used to evaluate the model (including univariable pre-screening) inflates apparent performance and must either be avoided or fully nested in cross-validation/external validation; PROBAST operationalizes this as explicit signaling questions in its predictor and analysis domains, RQS awards/deducts points for the same failure mode within radiomics-specific pipelines, and TRIPOD(+AI) requires it to be transparently reported.

### Cited Findings
- The Radiomics Quality Score (RQS) is a checklist-style tool used to assess radiomics study quality across domains including "image processing and feature extraction," "feature processing," and "preparation for modeling" — a meta-analysis of RQS across the field highlights recurring weaknesses in exactly these categories — [The long and winding road of radiomics: learnings from two meta-analyses of the radiomics quality score](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1361-6560/ae36e0)
- PROBAST has 4 domains (Participants, Predictors, Outcome, Analysis) with 20 signaling questions in total; the Predictors domain has 3 signaling questions and the Analysis domain is where predictor-selection and overfitting concerns are assessed — [PROBAST: A Tool to Assess Risk of Bias and Applicability of Prediction Model Studies, explanation and elaboration](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/full/10.7326/M18-1377)
- Within PROBAST's Analysis domain, one signaling question concerns whether there were a reasonable number of events relative to the number of candidate predictors (an events-per-variable check), and a separate signaling question asks whether selection of predictors was based on univariable analysis (a red flag if so) — [PROBAST explanation and elaboration PDF](https://www.probast.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/aime201901010-m181377.pdf) (paraphrased from the primary PDF, fetched and parsed in this session; exact wording should be checked against the original signaling-question phrasing before quoting verbatim in the thesis — see Gaps)
- The same PROBAST Analysis-domain guidance flags "data-driven predictor selection procedures" performed without pre-specification as a source of inflated/optimistic model performance estimates, and separately notes that highly correlated (collinear) predictors should be identified and appropriately handled during model development — [PROBAST explanation and elaboration PDF](https://www.probast.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/aime201901010-m181377.pdf)
- TRIPOD's core statement: "Quantifying the predictive ability of a model on the same data from which the model was developed will tend to give an optimistic estimate of performance, owing to overfitting and the use of predictor selection strategies" — this is the TRIPOD-level statement of the exact leakage concern (selecting predictors and then evaluating on the same data) — [TRIPOD Statement, BMC Medicine](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-014-0241-z)
- TRIPOD's original checklist has 22 items; TRIPOD+AI (the updated version incorporating machine-learning-based prediction models) expands this to a 27-item checklist with more detailed guidance, explicitly extending coverage to ML-style feature/variable selection pipelines, not only classical regression — [TRIPOD+AI statement, PMC11019967](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11019967/)
- On EPV specifically: the traditional "10 events per variable" rule of thumb is contested in the methodology literature; one paper concludes there is "no rationale for 1 variable per 10 events" as a fixed criterion for binary logistic regression — [No rationale for 1 variable per 10 events criterion, PMC5122171](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5122171/) — while a separate paper argues adequate sample size for prediction models is "not simply related to events per variable" and depends on additional factors (outcome prevalence, expected model performance, number of candidate predictors) — [Adequate sample size for developing prediction models is not simply related to events per variable, PMC5045274](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5045274/)
- Frank Harrell's more conservative recommendation, cited in secondary sources, is at least 20 events per variable (stricter than the classical 10 EPV rule) — [search synthesis of EPV literature](https://statisticalhorizons.com/logistic-regression-for-rare-events/)

### Inferences
- For a future-CAD prediction model built on baseline vascular geometry features with a modest number of outcome events, the combination of these frameworks implies a specific, avoidable failure mode to guard against: screening candidate tortuosity/curvature features by their univariate p-value or correlation with the outcome in the full dataset, then reporting cross-validated or held-out performance on a model built from only the "significant" survivors. All three frameworks (RQS implicitly, PROBAST explicitly via its univariable-selection signaling question, TRIPOD explicitly in its core rationale) treat this exact pattern as a primary source of optimistic bias.
- Given the EPV controversy, a fixed "10 EPV" cutoff should not be treated as a hard, citable threshold on its own; if a specific number of events-per-predictor is going to be reported as a justification for a chosen feature count, the more defensible framing is to note that the classical 10 EPV rule is contested and cite both the classical figure and the papers questioning it (PMC5122171, PMC5045274), rather than presenting 10 as settled.

### Gaps
- The exact verbatim wording of PROBAST's signaling questions (particularly the numbering, e.g. whether the univariable-selection question is 4.4, 4.5, or another number) was extracted from an automated read of the primary PDF rather than a direct visual check by a human reader in this session; before quoting PROBAST signaling-question text verbatim in the thesis, the primary PDF (`probast.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/aime201901010-m181377.pdf` or the ACP Journals version) should be checked directly.
- I was not able to retrieve the RQS original Lambin et al. paper or the METRICS-vs-RQS comparative paper in full text in this session; only a meta-analysis abstract-level description was obtained. A dedicated read of the original RQS paper is needed if specific point-scoring rules (e.g., how many points RQS awards/deducts for correlated-feature handling specifically) are needed for the thesis.

## Are there specific vascular/coronary imaging studies that combine multiple correlated geometric descriptors and explicitly address collinearity/redundancy?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence of vascular/coronary studies formally treating collinearity between multiple curvature/tortuosity metrics with regression diagnostics (VIF, correlation-based pruning) is thin; the two most relevant coronary tortuosity papers found in this pass report multiple correlated geometric metrics side by side and pick a "best" one by predictive performance rather than combining them in a jointly-regularized multivariable model, and neither performs a formal multicollinearity adjustment. The closest documented example of explicit multicollinearity handling in quantitative imaging biomarkers comes from a PET radiomics study, not a vascular geometry study specifically.

### Cited Findings
- A study comparing multiple coronary tortuosity/curvature metrics (tortuosity index, average absolute curvature, RMS curvature, average squared-derivative curvature) against time-averaged wall shear stress (TAWSS) found curvature-based metrics explained meaningfully more variance in TAWSS than the classical tortuosity index (curvature metrics explained roughly 6-27% of TAWSS variance across vessels versus essentially none for the tortuosity index; average absolute curvature had the strongest association, R² = 0.196 for the left main), and explicitly recommends replacing the tortuosity index with average absolute curvature as "a new benchmark measure in future research" rather than combining the metrics — [Accuracy of vascular tortuosity measures using computational modelling, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-04796-w) / [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- That same paper notes the tortuosity index shows strong deviation from normality (high skewness/kurtosis) while average curvature is closer to normal, which is a data-distribution observation relevant to modeling choices but is not itself a formal multicollinearity/VIF analysis — [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- A separate coronary study comparing a tortuosity-index method against an angle-measurement method for detecting tortuosity found a significant but imperfect association between the two metrics for LAD and LCX, but no significant association for RCA; the authors report both metrics' associations with myocardial ischemia independently (per-vessel univariable-style reporting) rather than combining both into a single multivariable model with any explicit collinearity adjustment — [Coronary Tortuosity Index vs. Angle Measurement Method, PMC10795752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10795752/)
- Outside vascular geometry specifically, a direct example of explicit multicollinearity quantification and correction in a quantitative imaging biomarker set comes from PET radiomics: cluster analysis on Spearman correlation showed at least two-thirds of a PET radiomic feature set could be omitted due to strong multicollinearity (35 non-redundant features retained at rho = 0.9, only 15 at rho = 0.7) — [Multicollinearity and redundancy of the PET radiomic feature set, PubMed 40332568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40332568/)

### Inferences
- The absence of formal multicollinearity handling in the two coronary tortuosity papers found here (both report multiple correlated geometric metrics but resolve redundancy by picking the "winning" metric based on external validity against a mechanistic target like TAWSS or ischemia, rather than through VIF/correlation-matrix-based joint modeling) suggests that vascular geometry literature is methodologically behind general quantitative-imaging/radiomics practice on this specific point. This is itself a citable observation for framing why a more rigorous approach (elastic net, VIF screening, or PCA composite) would be a methodological contribution rather than a redundant repetition of prior practice, if the thesis is combining several tortuosity/curvature metrics into one CAD-prediction model.
- The PET radiomics multicollinearity study is a reasonable methodological template to borrow from (correlation-clustering, then reporting how much of the feature set is redundant) even though it is not vascular-geometry-specific; the general finding that "most of a correlated quantitative-imaging feature set can be discarded via clustering with almost no information loss" is a transferable argument for keeping only a small number of decorrelated tortuosity/curvature descriptors before modeling.

### Gaps
- No coronary or peripheral vascular study was found in this pass that explicitly reports VIF values or a correlation matrix across more than two tortuosity/curvature metrics simultaneously and then applies LASSO/elastic net/PCA to combine them into a single CAD-prediction model; this appears to be a genuine gap in the vascular imaging literature rather than a search failure, but a broader/second-pass search (e.g., specifically on "bifurcation angle" combined with curvature metrics, or on retinal vessel tortuosity risk-prediction models, which were only lightly touched here) could not be completed within this session's tool budget and should be treated as unexplored.
- The retinal vascular tortuosity risk-factor literature (e.g., GWAS of retinal vessel tortuosity) was surfaced but not read in depth; it plausibly contains examples of combining multiple tortuosity-related traits with regularized regression given the genomics context, but this was not verified.
