# Multi-Metric vs Single-Metric Coronary Geometry/Tortuosity Prediction: Existing Evidence

## Key Question 1: Published studies combining >=2 tortuosity-family metrics (tortuosity index, curvature, torsion, bifurcation angle, diameter) into one model predicting CAD/severity/events, and reported gain vs single-feature baseline?

### Takeaway
A small number of studies do combine multiple geometric/tortuosity-family features (most notably an unpublished-in-full AHA abstract, "CArTI," which pools tortuosity+curvature+torsion into a 227-feature-then-8-feature Cox model), but none of the coronary studies found report a clean head-to-head "single metric vs combined-metric model" discrimination comparison. The clearest quantitative evidence for combining geometric features improving discrimination comes from retinal vasculature work, and even there one companion study (Edinburgh Type 2 Diabetes Study) found that adding a second geometric metric (fractal dimension) on top of a first (tortuosity) gave **no** additional discrimination benefit.

### Cited Findings
- CArTI (Coronary Artery Tortuosity Index): CCTA from 992 patients with MACE outcomes; extracted 227 features quantifying vessel tortuosity, curvature, and torsion; used univariate filtering across 50 bootstrap iterations to select the top 8 features; trained a Cox proportional-hazards model; on a 50/50 holdout test split, stratified 5-year MACE risk with a C-index of 0.648, with high-risk patients showing HR 2.69 vs low-risk — [AHA Circulation abstract 4365988](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circ.152.suppl_3.4365988)
- The CArTI abstract does not report a single-feature (e.g., tortuosity-alone or curvature-alone) baseline C-index for direct comparison against the 8-feature combined model — this comparison could not be found.
- Retinal vascular phenotyping for CAD detection combined Fractal Dimension, Vessel Density, mean blood-vessel/vein diameter (overall and zonal), Mean Branch Angle, Arteriovenous Ratio, Average Vessel Tortuosity, and Average Vein Curvature (overall and zonal) into diagnostic models. Model 1 (all fundus/retinal geometric parameters combined) AUROC = 0.694; Model 2 (clinical risk factors only) AUROC = 0.748; Model 3 (fundus + clinical combined) AUROC = 0.802 — [Retinal vascular phenotyping for early detection of CAD, PMC13052816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13052816/)
- That same study's final combined model retained multiple independent geometric predictors after multivariable adjustment (e.g., fractal dimension OR = 0.26, p<0.01; optic disc axis ratio OR = 0.04, p=0.01), indicating more than one geometric feature contributed independently — [PMC13052816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13052816/)
- Note: this retinal study's "fundus-only" arm (AUROC 0.694) is *itself* already a multi-metric combination (tortuosity + curvature + diameter + angle + fractal dimension + density), so no isolated single-geometric-metric baseline is reported within it either — the comparison available is multi-metric-imaging vs clinical vs combined-with-clinical, not single-geometric-metric vs multi-geometric-metric.
- A multimodal model combining OCTA retinal imaging features with electronic health records reached AUC = 0.850 (sensitivity 0.806, specificity 0.667), significantly outperforming an OCTA-imaging-only model at AUC = 0.512 — [Multimodal ML CAD prediction from OCTA + EHR, PMC13059466](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13059466/). This is a modality-combination result (imaging vs imaging+EHR), not a geometric-feature-count comparison, and is only tangentially relevant.
- Edinburgh Type 2 Diabetes Study (prospective cohort, 1066 adults with type 2 diabetes, 718 free of retinopathy at baseline, 10-year incidence of retinopathy 11.4%): adding retinal **venular tortuosity** to a model with established risk factors (HbA1c, blood pressure, kidney function) improved discrimination, C-statistic increasing from 0.624 to 0.640 (p = 0.013). Adding **fractal dimension** as a second geometric metric gave **no additional discrimination benefit** — [Retinal venular tortuosity and fractal dimension predict incident retinopathy: Edinburgh T2D Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8012328/) (confirmed via search-result excerpt; direct WebFetch of the PMC page was blocked by a bot-check page, so this is sourced from a search-engine-rendered excerpt of the article rather than the primary text directly, and should be treated as slightly lower-confidence pending direct verification).

### Inferences
- Across the sources found, combining multiple *different* geometric feature types into one model is rare in the coronary literature specifically; the only coronary example (CArTI) is an AHA meeting abstract, not yet available as a full peer-reviewed paper with a single-feature comparison arm.
- Where a combined-vs-single comparison *is* reported (Edinburgh T2D), the result is mixed: the first added geometric metric (tortuosity) helped, but a second (fractal dimension) added nothing further — this cuts against a simple "more metrics = monotonically better" narrative and is directly relevant to whether combining tortuosity-family features pays off.
- The retinal CAD study's large AUROC gain (0.694 -> 0.802) is driven mainly by adding clinical/traditional risk factors, not by adding more geometric metrics per se, so it should not be read as evidence that stacking more tortuosity-family geometric metrics alone drives large gains.

### Gaps
- No coronary study was found that isolates a single tortuosity-family metric's AUC/C-statistic and directly compares it, in the same cohort and modeling pipeline, against a model adding curvature, torsion, bifurcation angle, and/or diameter as covariates (i.e., an ablation-style comparison). This is the most direct test of the objective's core question and appears to be unaddressed in the literature found.
- The CArTI abstract's full paper (identity of the top 8 selected features, and any single-feature-vs-8-feature discrimination comparison) could not be located; only the AHA meeting abstract was accessible, and the ScienceDirect/PMC full-text attempts were not pursued further within budget.
- The Edinburgh T2D C-statistic figures were sourced from a search-engine summary of the PMC article rather than a direct fetch of the primary text (PMC blocked automated fetch behind a bot-check); the coordinator/report-writer may want independent verification if this number is load-bearing.

## Key Question 2: Correlation between tortuosity/curvature-family metrics with each other in real coronary cohorts; what does Kashyap et al. 2022 report?

### Takeaway
Kashyap et al. 2022 (127-patient coronary CFD/CTCA cohort) is the clearest primary source found: it directly contrasts four tortuosity-family metrics' ability to explain an outcome (low time-averaged wall shear stress, TAWSS) and finds the classic tortuosity index is uncorrelated with the outcome while curvature-based metrics (especially average absolute curvature) explain a modest but significant fraction of variance — implying these metrics are *not* interchangeable/redundant with each other with respect to hemodynamic relevance, even though the paper does not appear to report a direct pairwise correlation matrix between the metrics themselves.

### Cited Findings
- Kashyap et al. 2022 tested four tortuosity/curvature metrics on CTCA data from 127 patients without CAD, applied to the left main coronary bifurcation, LAD, and LCx branches, then modeled time-averaged wall shear stress (TAWSS) via CFD: (1) Tortuosity Index (arc length / chord length), (2) Average Absolute-Curvature, (3) RMS-Curvature, (4) Average Squared-Derivative-Curvature — [Accuracy of vascular tortuosity measures using computational modelling, Scientific Reports 2022, PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- Tortuosity Index showed **no significant correlation** with low TAWSS overall (p = 0.86 for the complete bifurcation; R² = -0.018) — [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- Average Absolute-Curvature was the strongest performer: LMCA R² = 0.196 (p<0.001), LAD R² = 0.232 (p<0.001), LCx R² = 0.061 (p=0.013), complete bifurcation R² = 0.112 (p=0.001) — [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- RMS-Curvature: LMCA R² = 0.113 (p<0.001), LAD R² = 0.164 (p<0.001) — [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- Average Squared-Derivative-Curvature: LMCA R² = 0.086 (p=0.003), LAD R² = 0.160 (p<0.001), complete bifurcation R² = 0.101 (p=0.001) — [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- Authors' summary: "curvature-based metrics accounted for 6-27% of TAWSS variance, with an average of 10% for the whole bifurcation, compared to the tortuosity index, which could not explain any amount of TAWSS variance," and they explicitly recommend average absolute-curvature over the tortuosity index for future coronary tortuosity studies — [PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)
- A separate coronary study comparing a tortuosity-index method against a visual angle-measurement method for tortuosity detection found a significant statistical association between the two methods for LCX (regression coefficient ± SE = 13.4 ± 2.2, p<0.001) and LAD (14.6 ± 2.4, p<0.001), but **not** for RCA (p = 0.506) — indicating partial, vessel-dependent overlap rather than full redundancy between an angle-based and an index-based tortuosity measure — [Coronary Tortuosity Index vs. Angle Measurement Method, PMC10795752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10795752/)

### Inferences
- Because tortuosity index (arc/chord ratio) and the curvature-family metrics diverge sharply in their relationship to a hemodynamic outcome (TAWSS) in the same 127-patient cohort, they are very unlikely to be simple redundant restatements of each other in coronary anatomy — a model combining TI with a curvature metric would plausibly capture non-overlapping information, at least with respect to shear-stress-mediated pathways.
- The fact that the tortuosity-index/angle-method association held for LAD and LCx but not RCA (PMC10795752) suggests correlation structure between tortuosity-family metrics may itself be vessel-specific, which is a caution against assuming a single correlation coefficient generalizes across the coronary tree.

### Gaps
- Kashyap et al. 2022, as far as could be extracted, reports each metric's association with the *outcome* (TAWSS) but a direct pairwise correlation matrix (e.g., Pearson r between tortuosity index and average absolute curvature, or between the three curvature variants themselves, independent of TAWSS) was not found in the excerpts retrieved. This is the single most directly relevant statistic the objective asked for and it could not be confirmed as reported (or not reported) in the paper without full-text access beyond what the fetch returned.
- No coronary study was found reporting a correlation coefficient between bifurcation angle and any curvature/tortuosity metric, or between branch diameter and tortuosity/curvature, within the same patient cohort.

## Key Question 3: Does bifurcation angle and branch diameter add independent predictive value over a curvature/tortuosity measure, or are they largely redundant (e.g., via Murray's law)?

### Takeaway
Murray's law and its deviations are diameter-based descriptors of bifurcation geometry that have their own, independent link to CAD-relevant outcomes (calcification, wall shear stress), and at least one hemodynamic study found that geometry optimized for Murray's-law diameter relationships is *not* the same geometry that minimizes low-wall-shear-stress regions when angle is varied — suggesting angle and diameter-ratio effects are not simply redundant. However, no study was found that puts bifurcation angle, branch diameter, and a tortuosity/curvature metric together as covariates in one regression/ML model predicting CAD or events and reports each one's independent contribution.

### Cited Findings
- Murray's law (Q ∝ D^P) relates a bifurcation's parent-vessel diameter to its daughter-branch diameters; the "optimal" exponent P is debated across studies: Murray's original theoretical value is 3.0, a model assuming constant blood velocity implies 2.0, the Kassab/HK diameter model (based on minimum energy in the whole tree) found bifurcations match this model and its angle rule "much more than Murray's model," with Kassab's exponent at 7/3 (~2.33); one dataset-fitting study found an exponent of 2.15 gave the strongest correlation with inlet flow while 2.38 best matched microvascular resistance — [Systematic review/meta-analysis of Murray's law in coronary circulation, PMC11380967](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11380967/); [Refining Our Understanding of Flow Through Coronary Branches, PMC9119389](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9119389/)
- In a 253-patient IVUS study, deviation from Murray's law (a diameter-ratio-derived "Murray ratio" across a bifurcation, with high ratio indicating low shear stress) remained significantly associated, after adjustment for age, sex, cardiovascular risk factors and medications, with fibrous plaque volume distal to the bifurcation, fibro-fatty volume distal to the bifurcation, and dense calcium volume both proximal and distal to the bifurcation — [Deviation from Murray's law is associated with a higher degree of calcification in coronary bifurcations, Atherosclerosis 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22261173/)
- A separate hemodynamic (CFD) study on bifurcation angle rules found that the angle rule derived from Murray's law "does not minimize the size of low WSS regions," implying diameter-optimal geometry (per Murray's law) and angle-optimal geometry for shear stress are not the same thing — [Which diameter and angle rule provides optimal flow patterns in a coronary bifurcation?, PubMed 22365499](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22365499/)
- General statement: "The branching angle and diameter ratio in epicardial coronary artery bifurcations are two important determinants of atherogenesis" (treated in the literature as two separate, both-relevant geometric determinants) — [search-aggregated summary, PMC9119389 context](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9119389/)

### Inferences
- Because Murray's-law-optimal diameter geometry and shear-stress-minimizing angle geometry appear to diverge (per the angle-rule CFD study), bifurcation angle and branch diameter ratio likely carry at least partially independent hemodynamic/atherogenic information rather than being redundant via a single physical law — this argues against assuming Murray's law alone "explains away" the need for a separate angle feature.
- That said, this inference rests on hemodynamic optimality studies, not on a direct outcome-prediction model that included both angle and diameter as covariates and reported each one's independent statistical contribution (e.g., adjusted odds ratios for each, controlling for the other) — that specific evidence was not found.

### Gaps
- No study was found that statistically tests bifurcation angle and branch diameter together as independent covariates in a CAD/outcome prediction model (e.g., multivariable regression with both terms and their individual p-values/OR after mutual adjustment).
- No direct correlation coefficient between bifurcation angle and branch diameter (or diameter ratio) across a real patient cohort was found; the Murray's-law literature discusses diameter relationships and angle rules largely as separate lines of derivation/optimization rather than reporting an empirical angle-diameter correlation.

## Key Question 4: Studies (coronary or other vascular beds) testing a MULTI-metric geometry PROFILE vs a SINGLE scalar, analogous to Tello Ayala et al.'s profile-vs-scalar tortuosity finding (0.67 vs 0.60 AUROC)?

### Takeaway
The Tello Ayala et al. 2026 comparison itself is confirmed (transformer model using the full per-point tortuosity profile plus age/sex reached AUROC 0.67 [95% CI 0.65-0.69] vs a logistic-regression model using mean tortuosity plus age/sex at AUROC 0.60 [95% CI 0.58-0.63]), but no other published study — in coronary or other vascular beds (retinal, cerebral) — was found that performs the same profile-representation-vs-scalar-representation comparison of a single geometric metric. This appears to be a genuine gap in the literature searched, not merely an artifact of search terms.

### Cited Findings
- Tello Ayala et al., "A Machine Learning Driven Approach to Quantifying Coronary Artery Tortuosity" (JACC: Advances, 2026): a transformer neural network using the full local tortuosity profile (plus age and sex) for the right coronary artery achieved AUROC 0.67 (95% CI 0.65-0.69), versus a logistic regression model using mean tortuosity (plus age and sex) at AUROC 0.60 (95% CI 0.58-0.63) — [A Machine Learning Driven Approach to Quantifying Coronary Artery Tortuosity, PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/) / [ScienceDirect version](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772963X26002504)
- A cerebral-aneurysm mouse-model study created a coarse, ordinal 4-point visual "tortuosity scale" and separately computed a continuous Tortuosity Index (arc/chord ratio) for the anterior communicating, middle cerebral, and basilar arteries; both the visual scale and the numeric index significantly predicted aneurysm formation and rupture (p<0.001 each) — [Cerebral vascular tortuosity and aneurysm formation and rupture: a novel vessel tortuosity scale, PMC12262821](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12262821/) (sourced via search-engine summary; direct WebFetch was blocked by a bot-check page on PMC, so treat as lower-confidence pending independent verification)
- No head-to-head AUC/discrimination comparison between the ordinal visual scale and the numeric tortuosity index was found reported for that cerebral aneurysm study — both were simply reported as independently significant, not compared against each other for discrimination.

### Inferences
- The cerebral aneurysm study's visual-scale-vs-index setup is only a weak analogy to Tello Ayala's profile-vs-scalar axis: a coarse ordinal category is not the same representational contrast as "full per-point profile fed to a sequence model" vs "single averaged scalar fed to logistic regression." It should not be cited as confirming or replicating the profile-vs-scalar finding, only as a loosely related instance of comparing two representations of the same underlying tortuosity concept.
- The retinal-vasculature literature reviewed for this task (Edinburgh T2D study, retinal CAD phenotyping study) uses scalar/zonal-average tortuosity metrics throughout; none of the retinal papers surfaced in this search used a per-point tortuosity profile fed into a sequence model comparable to Tello Ayala's transformer approach, despite retinal cohorts often being much larger and well-suited to such an analysis.

### Gaps
- No true replication or analogous test of the "full profile beats averaged scalar of the same metric" finding was located in any vascular bed (coronary, retinal, or cerebral) other than the Tello Ayala paper itself. Given retinal vasculature's much larger typical cohort sizes, this seems like a plausible but currently unfilled research gap rather than a settled question with contrary evidence.

## Key Question 5: Overfitting risk / reduced generalizability when combining multiple correlated geometric features in modest sample sizes — what do these studies report?

### Takeaway
None of the coronary- or retinal-vasculature-specific papers surfaced in this search explicitly report or quantify overfitting from combining correlated tortuosity-family features; the strongest concrete evidence of the field taking this risk seriously is methodological (CArTI's bootstrap-stability feature selection from 227 candidate features down to 8, and a general vascular-outcome study's practice of pruning highly correlated features and applying elastic-net-regularized Cox regression), rather than an explicit empirical demonstration of overfitting in a tortuosity-feature-combination study.

### Cited Findings
- CArTI's pipeline extracted 227 candidate tortuosity/curvature/torsion features from 992 patients, then used univariate filtering repeated across 50 bootstrap iterations to select a stable top-8 feature subset before fitting the Cox model — an implicit acknowledgment that using all 227 correlated geometric features directly would risk overfitting relative to the ~992-patient sample and its 50/50 train/test split — [AHA Circulation abstract 4365988](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circ.152.suppl_3.4365988)
- In a related vascular-outcome ML context (tumor-associated vasculature outcome prediction, not coronary), the modeling approach explicitly pruned features that were "highly correlated and likely redundant," retaining only the feature with the highest absolute coefficient in a multivariate proportional-hazards model, and used a Cox model with 10-fold elastic-net regularization to control overfitting — [general vascular-feature overfitting search results, USPTO patent filing context](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11896349)
- General biostatistical guidance (not vascular-geometry-specific): high feature dimensionality relative to sample size increases overfitting risk (e.g., unreliable within-class covariance estimation in discriminant-analysis-type methods), and shrinkage/regularization techniques are recommended to control the degree of overfitting as a function of sample size — [Overfitting in prediction models, NCI Biometric Research Program](https://brb.nci.nih.gov/techreport/Subramanian-Overfitting.pdf)

### Inferences
- The field's practical response to combining many correlated tortuosity-family features (seen in CArTI and in the tumor-vasculature analogy) is dimensionality reduction via bootstrap-stability selection or correlation-pruning plus regularization, rather than fitting all features jointly in an unregularized model — this is indirect evidence that practitioners in this space consider overfitting a live risk when many correlated geometric features are combined, even though none of the papers found present a controlled experiment (e.g., a train/test AUC gap) isolating and quantifying that risk specifically for tortuosity/curvature-family features in a coronary cohort.

### Gaps
- No coronary or retinal geometric-feature-combination study was found that explicitly reports a train-vs-test (or internal-vs-external validation) discrimination gap attributable to combining correlated tortuosity-family features, which is the most direct evidence the key question asks for.
- No sample-size-specific guidance (e.g., "with N=127 patients, combining 3+ correlated curvature metrics inflates variance by X") specific to coronary tortuosity studies was found; the regularization/shrinkage evidence gathered is generic statistical methodology, not a coronary-geometry-specific empirical finding.
