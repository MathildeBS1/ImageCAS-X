# Within-Vessel Segment-Level and Positional Tortuosity Granularity

## Standard anatomical coronary segmentation schemes (SCCT 18-segment, AHA 15/16-segment): fixed length, landmark, or both?

### Takeaway
Both the SCCT 18-segment model and the AHA 15/16-segment model divide the LAD and RCA into proximal/mid/distal (and LCx into proximal/mid-distal), but the boundaries between those sub-segments are anatomical-landmark-defined (specific branch origins, specific anatomical bends), not fixed-length cutoffs. Where two landmarks are candidates (e.g. first septal perforator vs first diagonal for proximal LAD), clinical usage is not fully standardized on which one governs.

### Cited Findings
- The SCCT 18-segment model: "The left anterior descending (LAD) artery and right coronary artery (RCA) are divided into proximal, middle, and distal segments, whereas the left circumflex coronary (LCx) artery is divided into proximal and distal segments." The 18 segments are: pRCA, mRCA, dRCA, R-PDA, LM, pLAD, mLAD, dLAD, D1, D2, pCx, OM1, mid-distal LCx, OM2, L-PDA, R-PLB, RI, L-PLB. — [ResearchGate figure/summary of SCCT 18-segment model](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Diagram-of-the-18-segment-model-of-the-coronary-tree-Diagram-showing-the-coronary-tree_fig1_383812077); [SCCT 2026 update, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1934592526004806) (abstract/summary level, not independently verified against full text)
- The AHA 15-segment model has the same proximal/mid/distal division for RCA and LAD, and proximal/mid-distal for LCx; the 16-segment model adds the ramus intermedius (when present, arising from LM between LAD and LCx); a 17-segment extension exists for cardiac imaging territory mapping. — [PMC7860735, patient-specific AHA diagram paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7860735/) (summary level)
- Segment boundaries are landmark-defined rather than length-defined: per SCCT guidance, "disease at the intersection of segments (including bifurcations) is assigned to the preceding coronary segment," implying bifurcation landmarks, not a fixed millimeter length, delimit segments. — [Search-derived summary of SCCT/AHA guidance](https://www.journalofcardiovascularct.com/article/S1934-5925%2809%2900070-7/fulltext) (full text could not be fetched directly, blocked by 403; this point is from an aggregated search summary, not independently verified against the primary PDF)
- For the LAD specifically, the proximal/mid boundary is defined by a named branch origin, but which branch is used is not fully standardized across sources: "proximal LAD is defined as the segment extending from the bifurcation of the LMCA to the first major septal perforator branch (some definitions say to first diagonal)"; one clinical note states that when "proximal" is used without further qualification it is conventionally taken relative to the first diagonal branch, not the first septal perforator. — [Search-aggregated clinical anatomy sources, e.g. STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Database training manual, AATS TSRA coronary anatomy primer](https://www.sts.org/sites/default/files/documents/CoronaryAnatomy.pdf) (aggregated from search summary, not independently fetched/verified against the primary PDF text)

### Inferences
- The two dominant clinical/CTA segmentation schemes (SCCT, AHA) already establish a precedent that "proximal/mid/distal" is the standard first level of within-vessel granularity for the LAD and RCA (2-way for LCx), and that boundary placement should follow a bifurcation/branch-origin landmark detectable on the centerline (e.g. a diagonal or septal branch origin, an acute-margin bend), not an arbitrary fixed length. A coronary tortuosity method adding segment-level granularity could reuse this exact scheme rather than inventing new boundaries, which would also make results comparable to CAD-RADS/SCCT reporting categories clinicians already use.
- Because the landmark used to split proximal/mid LAD is not fully standardized (first septal vs first diagonal), any implementation must state explicitly which rule it uses and record it as a deviation/assumption, consistent with this project's convention of recording deviations from published settings.

### Gaps
- I could not directly fetch the primary SCCT 2009 or 2026 guideline full text (both blocked by 403/CAPTCHA); the exact wording on segment boundary landmarks for the RCA (e.g. where "proximal" ends at the acute margin, where "mid" ends before the crux) was not independently verified, only inferred from secondary/aggregated summaries. This should be flagged to the report writer as needing a primary-source check if the report leans heavily on exact RCA landmark wording.
- No numeric segment *length* ranges (e.g. "proximal RCA averages X mm") were found in the sources retrieved; the schemes appear to be purely landmark-anchored rather than reporting expected lengths.

## Documented evidence that tortuosity differs systematically between proximal, mid, and distal segments of the same vessel, independent of disease

### Takeaway
There is search-level (not primary-source-verified) evidence that coronary tortuosity increases from proximal to distal within the same vessel, attributed to branch density/caliber effects (more bifurcation constraint points distally producing multiple small loops) rather than to disease per se, and that this is most reported for the LAD. Exact quantitative proximal-vs-mid-vs-distal tortuosity numbers (independent of disease) were not found in verified primary sources.

### Cited Findings
- "Different portions of the coronary arteries exhibit significantly different features, with calcium volume and extent showing a decreasing pattern moving toward distal regions, while tortuosity tends to increase." — aggregated web-search summary, source paper not identified/verified by title; flagged as summary-level only
- Mechanistic explanation for why distal segments look more tortuous than proximal ones in the same vessel: "Redundancy in a vessel with multiple branch points providing points of constraint is more likely to lead to a series of small loops (such as in a distal coronary artery) than a large conduit with no branches where the extra length will take the shape of a single loop or simple S (such as the proximal right coronary)." — aggregated web-search summary; original source paper not identified by name/DOI, so this cannot be upgraded beyond summary-level confidence
- "Coronary tortuosity mostly affects the left anterior descending coronary artery in mid and distal segments." — aggregated web-search summary of coronary tortuosity literature; original source not individually identified
- Angiographic tapering (a related but distinct phenomenon: reduction of lumen caliber along vessel length, not curvature) is itself segment- and vessel-specific and quantified: in women, LAD tapering was 18±14% (proximal) and 29±9% (distal); RCA tapering was smaller, 9±12% (proximal) and 7±8% (distal). — [PubMed: Normal angiographic tapering of the coronary arteries](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12368932/) (abstract/summary level; not independently fetched in full)
- A 2026 machine-learning study using per-point "local tortuosity" along the artery (see next section) found that a model using the full positional/local tortuosity vector discriminated CAD better (AUC 0.67, 95% CI 0.65-0.69) than a model using only mean (whole-vessel) tortuosity plus age and sex (AUC 0.60, 95% CI 0.58-0.63), implying that *where* along the vessel tortuosity is concentrated carries information beyond the whole-vessel average — though this is a disease-discrimination finding, not a clean disease-independent proximal/mid/distal comparison. — [PMC13308244, "A Machine Learning Driven Approach to Quantifying Coronary Artery Tortuosity"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/), quoted directly via fetch, verified against the actual article text

### Inferences
- The caliber/branch-density mechanism (more bifurcations distally, each a geometric constraint point, forcing curvature into more numerous smaller loops rather than one broad sweep) is a plausible geometric confound that is largely independent of atherosclerotic disease: it follows from coronary tree branching anatomy itself (progressive bifurcation toward smaller distal branches) rather than from plaque or calcification. If true, a whole-vessel tortuosity scalar systematically averages together a lower-curvature proximal trunk and a higher-curvature distal segment, which could bias comparisons across vessels or across patients whose visible/segmented vessel length differs (e.g. more distal tree captured in one scan than another) purely as an artifact of how much distal, structurally-more-tortuous vessel is included.
- This mechanism is a reasonable candidate for why chord/arc-length whole-vessel indices could be biased by how far distally the segmentation/centerline extends, which is directly relevant to a report evaluating whether a coronary method should add segment- or position-level granularity: it argues for at least reporting length-included alongside tortuosity, or normalizing/segmenting so proximal and distal contributions are not conflated.

### Gaps
- I was not able to identify the primary source(s) behind the two most directly relevant claims (distal tortuosity increase driven by branch redundancy/small loops; LAD tortuosity concentrated in mid/distal). These came back as unattributed aggregated summaries from web search rather than a paper I could fetch and quote directly. This is the single most important gap for the report: a claim central to the "does tortuosity vary by segment, independent of disease" question is not verified to a primary source and should be treated as low-confidence until re-derived from a named, fetchable paper.
- No study was found that explicitly isolates segment location from disease burden (e.g. a cohort with angiographically normal coronaries broken into proximal/mid/distal tortuosity, matched for age/hypertension) to give a clean "independent of disease" answer. The tapering finding above is a different geometric phenomenon (caliber change) and should not be conflated with curvature-based tortuosity, though both are call-outs of the same underlying "distal segments behave differently" theme.

## Sliding-window / positional local tortuosity and curvature profiles along arc length; window size choice, endpoint handling, and arc-length normalization for cross-vessel comparison

### Takeaway
At least one directly-verified coronary paper computes tortuosity as a continuous ordered vector of point-wise local curvature along the centerline (not one scalar and not discrete proximal/mid/distal buckets), using per-point discrete turning angle rather than a fixed-length sliding window, and does not state that it normalizes vessel position to a 0-1 fraction of length. Retinal-imaging literature separately establishes a curvature/arc-length local index (T4) and a partitioning approach that cuts vessels at anatomical junctions (bifurcations, crossings, endpoints) rather than at arbitrary fixed windows. Arc-length reparametrization/registration is an established functional-data-analysis technique for aligning and comparing curves of different length by remapping to normalized arc length, used in biomechanical signal analysis, and is the generic mechanism that would let 0-1 normalized tortuosity profiles from vessels of different absolute lengths be compared or averaged.

### Cited Findings
- Point-wise (per-vertex) local tortuosity, verified directly from source text: "The discrete curvature at x_i is the local change in direction at x_i, defined as the turning angle required to align edge x_{i-1}x_i with edge x_i x_{i+1}," computed as the absolute intersegment angle |θ_i|, normalized by π to give "a tortuosity metric that has a continuous score between 0 (lowest tortuosity) and 1 (highest tortuosity)," then assembled into "an ordered vector of local tortuosity along the artery" and fed to a transformer model. The paper does not state that it normalizes the vector's position axis to a 0-1 fraction of total vessel length (this was explicitly checked and not found in the fetched text). — [PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/), directly fetched and quoted
- Retinal-imaging local tortuosity index T4 is defined as total curvature divided by arc length (curvature-to-arc-length ratio), reported as "the most robust in relation to a frame center change" among tested local indices, i.e. more stable than chord-based global indices under viewpoint/framing changes. — [Diagnostics 13(6):1030, "Retinal Vessel Local Tortuosity under a Macula-to-Optic Disc Central-Framing Change"](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics13061030) (from search-result summary; direct full-text fetch was blocked by 403 on both PMC and MDPI, so the T4 formula/robustness claim is summary-level, not independently verified against the primary text)
- A directly-fetched and verified retinal tortuosity paper (PLOS ONE) partitions vessels at "endpoints, bifurcation points, and crossing points" into unbranched sections, rather than using fixed-length sliding windows, and explicitly frames the resulting problem as one of *compositionality*: it tests two competing mathematical criteria for how whole-vessel tortuosity should relate to its parts. Hart's criterion requires whole-vessel tortuosity τ(V) to lie between the values τ(V₁) and τ(V₂) of its sub-segments (equality only if the sub-segments are identical); Grisan's criterion requires the opposite, that whole-vessel tortuosity exceed any constituent sub-segment's tortuosity, reflecting accumulated curvature from multiple direction changes. The paper states these two criteria "cannot be fulfilled simultaneously," and shows empirically (their Table 7, on a synthetic three-semicircle test vessel) that some indices satisfy neither criterion consistently, with compliance "depend[ing] on the specific vessel path under consideration." It further reports that "an increase in the number of sections generally leads to lower global tortuosity values for most combinations" of index and weighting scheme — i.e. how you partition a vessel changes the answer, partitioning is not a neutral operation. — [PLOS ONE, "Bridging local and global tortuosity of retinal vessels: Objective testing of index performance"](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0329379), directly fetched and quoted
- Generic (non-vessel) arc-length curve registration/alignment method, directly relevant as the mechanism for cross-vessel positional comparison: "Arc-length parameterization reparametrizes a curve so that the speed is constant (usually 1)... it mitigates the influence of variable speed in curve comparisons and guarantees uniform distribution along the curve," and separately, "Signals are subject to signal registration to align critical features with respect to normalized arc-length... using strictly monotonic, signal-specific warping functions to continuously align features across all signals simultaneously by remapping normalized arc-length." — [Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology, "Arc-Length Re-Parametrization and Signal Registration to Determine a Characteristic Average and Statistical Response Corridors of Biomechanical Data"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2022.843148/full) (from search-result summary of the abstract/methods; not independently fetched in full, so treat the exact registration mechanics as summary-level)
- River geomorphology has an explicit multi-scale analogue to "window size choice": the scale-specific sinuosity (S3) metric is described as "a derivative of the Richardson (1961) plot" that "quantifies a frequency signature of planform bend sizes spanning a range of measurement units," i.e. it treats sinuosity as a function of the measurement scale/window rather than picking one window size, and for one worked river example "form[ed] a broad peak between log10 strides of 2 and 3.5, primarily peaking at 3, indicating that most bends range between about 100 and 3200 m in bend size, with bend frequency peaking for bends about 1000 m in size." This is a direct, quantitative illustration that sinuosity/tortuosity computed at a single fixed window size is scale-dependent and can miss the characteristic bend size of the feature; the S3 approach sweeps scale explicitly rather than fixing a single window a priori. — [ScienceDirect / USGS, "At what scales does a river meander? Scale-specific sinuosity (S3) metric"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169555X2300154X) (from search-result summary; full text not independently fetched, so exact numbers are summary-level, not primary-verified)

### Inferences
- Two structurally different "sub-whole-vessel" approaches exist in the literature and are not the same thing: (a) discrete anatomical partitioning at bifurcation/crossing/endpoint landmarks (used by the retinal PLOS paper, and structurally analogous to the SCCT/AHA proximal/mid/distal scheme), and (b) continuous per-point or windowed positional profiles that do not require detecting landmarks (used by the coronary ML paper's "ordered vector of local tortuosity," and by the T4 curvature/arc-length local index). A report recommending a method for CAS-Net-style coronary analysis should treat these as two distinct design options with different costs: landmark partitioning requires reliable bifurcation/branch detection (itself an open sub-problem, e.g. in this project's own topology extraction work), whereas a continuous per-point profile only requires a clean ordered centerline and no branch-labeling, at the cost of not aligning to clinically-named segments.
- The PLOS paper's compositionality finding, that the number of partitions changes the aggregate tortuosity value and that no index satisfies both Hart's and Grisan's criteria simultaneously, is a direct, verified caution against any scheme that tries to reconstruct one "corrected" whole-vessel number from segment-level numbers (e.g. by averaging proximal/mid/distal scores): the choice of partitioning is not neutral, and different aggregation choices are not simply more granular versions of the same ground truth.
- Arc-length reparametrization/registration is the specific FDA machinery that answers the "how do you compare profiles from vessels of different absolute length" question: normalize each vessel's centerline arc length to [0,1], build the tortuosity-vs-normalized-position profile, then either compare curves directly (as functions on a common [0,1] domain) or additionally apply landmark/feature registration if particular anatomical landmarks (e.g. D1 origin) should be forced to align across patients rather than assuming proportional position is anatomically equivalent. No coronary-specific paper doing exactly this was found; the recommendation to use it is an inference from the generic FDA/biomechanical-signal literature, not a coronary-tortuosity-specific citation.

### Gaps
- The exact T4 formula and its stated robustness advantage could not be verified against primary text; both the MDPI and PMC hosts of the Diagnostics paper returned 403/CAPTCHA blocks on direct fetch. This should be flagged if the report leans on T4's specific numeric robustness claim.
- No coronary-specific paper was found that explicitly states a chosen sliding-window length (in mm or in number of centerline points) for a local tortuosity profile, nor one that explicitly normalizes vessel position to a 0-1 fraction of length for cross-patient/cross-vessel averaging. The coronary ML paper (PMC13308244) is the closest verified precedent, and it explicitly does not state a length-normalization step, so this remains an open methodological gap in the coronary literature itself, not just a gap in this search.
- The Frontiers arc-length registration paper's exact algorithmic detail (how warping functions are fit, what landmark features are aligned) was not independently fetched; only the abstract/summary-level description is reported here.

## River reach-based sinuosity and retinal per-quadrant/branch-order/window handling of sub-curve positional binning

### Takeaway
River geomorphology has a mature, explicit reach-based tradition (dividing a channel into discrete reaches for separate sinuosity scoring) with at least one formalized automated method that defines reach boundaries by intensive (length-independent) attributes rather than by fixed length, plus a separate scale-sweeping approach (S3, above) that treats window size itself as a parameter to explore. Retinal imaging's dominant sub-curve strategy is landmark-based partitioning at bifurcation/crossing/endpoint (matching the PLOS paper above) and per-vessel-type/quadrant selection (one representative vessel of each caliber class per quadrant) rather than binning a single vessel into fixed-length windows along its own course.

### Cited Findings
- Classic sinuosity classification by reach: "based on sinuosity index (varies between 1.02 and 1.41), river segments are categorized into straight, sinuous, and meandering" — [River Sinuosity Classification: The method](https://www.academia.edu/15165166/River_Sinuosity_Classification_The_method) (from search-result summary; direct fetch was blocked by 403, so the exact 1.02-1.41 range and classification thresholds are summary-level only, not verified against the primary text)
- An automated reach-segmentation method (tested on the Magdalena River, Colombia, and the Baker River, Chile) defines reaches not by fixed length but by "a set of intensive attributes (independent of segment length), with reaches then identified by the intersection of the segmentations induced by such attributes," explicitly framed as "an improvement in geomorphic river characterization" over ad hoc fixed-length reach cutting. — [MDPI Water 12(10):2827, "A Systematic, Automated Approach for River Segmentation..."](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/12/10/2827) (from search-result summary; direct fetch blocked by 403, so this is summary-level, not independently verified against the primary methods text)
- The scale-specific sinuosity (S3) approach (see previous section) is the river-geomorphology answer to "what window size should I use": rather than committing to one reach length, it sweeps a range of measurement scales and reports sinuosity as a spectrum/histogram over bend size, explicitly because "sinuosity can vary with the magnitude of units at which it is measured." — [ScienceDirect/USGS S3 metric](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169555X2300154X) (search-result summary level)
- Retinal imaging's standard sub-curve strategy in clinical tortuosity grading protocols is spatial/vessel-type selection rather than along-vessel windowing: raters "aim for the selection of one vessel of each type in each quadrant," with vessel type meaning caliber/branch-order class (e.g. largest artery/vein per quadrant), and if that specific vessel is unavailable due to branching or overlap, an alternative vessel is substituted. Separately, branch order matters as a category: "larger tortuosity increments were observed in retinal arteries with even larger increments in branch retinal vessels, and branch arterial tortuosity was significantly associated with both [diabetic retinopathy] genesis and progress," i.e. branch (higher-order) vessels are scored as tortuosity carriers distinct from and more sensitive than the main trunk vessels, but this is a between-vessel-class comparison (trunk vs branch), not a within-single-vessel positional profile. — [Frontiers in Endocrinology, "Tortuosity of branch retinal artery is more associated with the genesis and progress of diabetic retinopathy"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2022.972339/full) (search-result summary level)
- Retinal tortuosity is also computed per unbranched segment after "branch points in vessels are detected and used to separate the vasculature into individual segments, with tortuosity measured by calculating the arc-chord ratio," matching the same landmark-based partitioning logic as the PLOS compositionality paper above. — aggregated web-search summary of the general retinal tortuosity measurement literature; no single paper individually verified for this specific sentence

### Inferences
- River geomorphology's two strategies map cleanly onto the two coronary design options identified in the prior section: intensive-attribute-based reach segmentation is the river analogue of SCCT/AHA landmark-based proximal/mid/distal segmentation, while the S3 scale-sweep is the river analogue of a continuous, window-size-agnostic positional tortuosity profile. Since river geomorphology has apparently moved toward preferring intensive/scale-aware methods over naive fixed-length reach-cutting (per the "improvement over" framing in the MDPI paper), this is weak supporting precedent for a coronary method also avoiding an arbitrarily-chosen fixed-length window, in favor of either landmark-anchored segments or a full continuous profile.
- Retinal imaging appears not to have a strong precedent for within-single-vessel positional profiling analogous to what the key questions ask about (proximal/mid/distal within one artery); its granularity axes are between-vessel (quadrant, caliber class, branch order) rather than within-one-vessel-along-its-length. This is itself a useful negative finding for the report: the closest verified within-single-vessel precedent found across all fields searched is the coronary ML paper's per-point ordered local tortuosity vector (PMC13308244), not anything from retinal imaging.

### Gaps
- Neither the academia.edu river sinuosity classification paper nor the MDPI automated river segmentation paper could be fetched directly (both 403); their sinuosity threshold numbers (1.02-1.41) and "intensive attribute" method details are reported here only at search-summary confidence, not primary-verified. If the final report needs to cite exact river sinuosity classification thresholds with confidence, these two sources should be re-attempted via a different access route (e.g. institutional proxy, or the ADS/USGS mirror for the S3 paper) before publication.
- No retinal-imaging paper was found that explicitly bins a single vessel into fixed-length along-length windows (e.g. "compute tortuosity in each 1mm window from origin to terminus"); the field's practice is closer to discrete anatomical-landmark partitioning or between-vessel-class comparison, not a positional windowing scheme, which is itself the answer to this key question but should be reported as an explicit absence, not silently omitted.

## Documented risk or artifact of computing tortuosity in short, fixed-length local windows (bifurcation instability, systematic bias)

### Takeaway
No source found gives a clean, quantified, directly-stated "bias curve" for tortuosity as a function of window length or distance-to-bifurcation. The closest verified evidence is indirect: the PLOS compositionality paper shows that partition count changes the aggregate tortuosity value in a specific direction (more partitions -> lower global tortuosity for most index/weighting combinations), and separate bifurcation-analysis literature explicitly avoids placing curvature-measurement points too close to a bifurcation (using a minimum 5 mm standoff in one coronary bifurcation CFD study), which is itself indirect evidence that practitioners in adjacent coronary geometry work treat near-bifurcation regions as unreliable for curvature measurement, without a source explicitly stating the mechanism or citing a bias magnitude for coronary tortuosity specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Directly verified partition-count effect (not a window-size effect per se, but the same underlying phenomenon: how you cut a vessel changes the number reported): "An increase in the number of sections generally leads to lower global tortuosity values for most combinations" of index and weighting scheme, per Table 7 of the PLOS paper's synthetic three-semicircle test vessel. — [PLOS ONE, "Bridging local and global tortuosity of retinal vessels"](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0329379), directly fetched and quoted; note this paper explicitly does **not** discuss the bifurcation-straddling case (a window or segment boundary landing partway across a branch point) — this was checked directly and confirmed absent from the fetched text
- Adjacent coronary-bifurcation geometry literature imposes an explicit minimum standoff distance from the bifurcation point before trusting a curvature measurement: in a CFD/tortuosity-at-bifurcations study, "each branch of the bifurcation was sliced after a length of 10 mm from the bifurcation point, and centerlines were resampled with equal spacing of 0.01 mm to minimize discretization errors," and "points of largest curvature are set as close to the bifurcation as possible, but at a distance longer than 5 mm." — aggregated web-search summary citing a coronary bifurcation tortuosity/CFD study (title/DOI not individually confirmed by direct fetch in this session; treat as summary-level, sourced from a search aggregation rather than the primary PDF)
- General vascular geometric-characterization literature separately warns that projection/dimensionality choices distort tortuosity: "Planar projections misrepresented branch trajectory, vessel length, and tortuosity, proving the importance of 3D geometric characterization" — this is a distinct artifact (2D projection vs 3D reconstruction) rather than a window-size/bifurcation-proximity artifact, but is worth flagging since it is a documented systematic bias source in the same measurement family. — [Methods for Three-Dimensional Geometric Characterization of the Arterial Vasculature, Annals of Biomedical Engineering](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-007-9307-9) (search-result summary level; not independently fetched)
- Direct search for "tortuosity measurement bifurcation artifact window straddle branch point bias" returned no source that explicitly names or quantifies a straddling artifact; the search tool's own aggregated response stated plainly that "specific discussions of window straddle artifact or branch point bias were not explicitly detailed in these particular sources," which is itself the clearest signal available that this specific artifact is not well-documented in the literature surfaced here.

### Inferences
- The convergent, if indirect, evidence (partition count changes the aggregate score; adjacent bifurcation-geometry work imposes 5-10 mm standoffs before trusting curvature near a branch point) supports treating short fixed-length windows near bifurcations as a plausible but not literature-quantified risk for a coronary tortuosity method: at minimum, a positional/windowed coronary tortuosity method should avoid placing window centers or segment boundaries within a few millimeters of a detected bifurcation, mirroring the 5 mm standoff used in the adjacent CFD literature, and should report results as sensitive to this choice rather than presenting a single profile as ground truth, consistent with the PLOS paper's broader point that partitioning choices are not neutral.
- The absence of an explicit, quantified "bifurcation-straddling bias" paper is itself informative: it suggests this is an open point that a report could flag as a genuine methodological gap in the field (worth stating explicitly as a limitation/recommendation) rather than something to cite as settled.

### Gaps
- No source gives a quantified bias curve (e.g. "% overestimate of curvature when window straddles a bifurcation by X mm") for any vascular bed, coronary or retinal. This is the largest unresolved key question in this assignment; if the report needs a firmer answer, a targeted next step would be to search specifically within vascular image-processing / centerline-extraction methods papers (e.g. VMTK-adjacent tooling, or the retinal tortuosity-index-validation literature such as the "Bridging local and global tortuosity" PLOS paper's own reference list) for papers that stress-test index behavior under synthetic bifurcation-adjacent perturbations, since the PLOS paper's synthetic-vessel testing methodology (their Table 7 approach) is structurally the right tool for this question even though it was not applied to the bifurcation-straddling case in the version fetched here.
- The 5 mm/10 mm coronary bifurcation standoff figures could not be traced to a directly-fetched, individually-confirmed primary source in this session (came from an aggregated search summary); if load-bearing for the final report, this should be re-verified against the primary paper (likely one of the CFD-based coronary bifurcation tortuosity studies surfaced in the search, e.g. the PMC7574594 "Tortuosity of Coronary Bifurcation as a Potential Local Risk Factor for Atherosclerosis" study) before citing the exact millimeter values.
