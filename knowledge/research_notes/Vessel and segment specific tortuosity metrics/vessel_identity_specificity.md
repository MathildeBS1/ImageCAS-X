# Vessel-Identity Stratification for Tortuosity Quantification

## Key Question 1: Does coronary tortuosity differ systematically by named vessel (LAD/LCx/RCA/diagonals/LM), and why?

### Takeaway
Yes, and the direction of the difference depends on which tortuosity metric is used: RCA shows the highest arc-chord-ratio tortuosity index (TI) but the lowest prevalence of discrete angle-based tortuosity (≥3 consecutive ≥45° bends), while LCx and LAD show the reverse pattern. This metric-dependent reversal is itself evidence that a single pooled coronary tortuosity score is not a well-defined quantity without specifying which vessel and which index.

### Cited Findings
- In a cohort with non-obstructive CAD (NOCAD) vs. obstructive CAD (OCAD), median arc-chord-ratio Tortuosity Index (TI) by vessel: NOCAD group — LCX 1.30 (IQR 1.13-1.47), LAD 1.26 (IQR 1.18-1.45), RCA 2.00 (IQR 1.79-2.27); OCAD group — LCX 1.15 (IQR 1.10-1.30), LAD 1.18 (IQR 1.14-1.26), RCA 1.87 (IQR 1.65-2.17). Differences between groups were significant for all three vessels (p<0.001 for LCX and LAD, p=0.014 for RCA) — [Coronary Tortuosity Index vs. Angle Measurement Method, PMC10795752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10795752/)
- The same study, using the classical angle-based definition (≥3 consecutive curvatures of ≥45° along the main trunk, present in both systole and diastole), found "tortuous" prevalence in NOCAD vs. OCAD: LCX 40.0% vs. 14.7% (p<0.001), LAD 32.9% vs. 10.7% (p<0.001), RCA 9.4% vs. 0.0% (p=0.007). The authors state most tortuosity by this definition occurred in LCX, then LAD, "and significantly less RCA," attributing the RCA's relative sparing to its baseline "C-shaped" natural curvature making additional discrete bend formation less likely — [same source, PMC10795752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10795752/)
- Note the apparent contradiction across the two metrics in the same paper/cohort: RCA has the highest continuous-curvature TI value (2.00) yet the lowest discrete-bend prevalence (9.4%). This means "coronary tortuosity" ranks vessels differently depending on whether a continuous arc-chord metric or a discrete-bend-count metric is used — [PMC10795752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10795752/)
- In a separate cohort of 1,010 patients, prevalence of coronary tortuosity (using the discrete-bend definition) by vessel: LCX 26.9%, LAD 21.1%, RCA 1.4%; combined LAD+LCX in 9.9% of patients; overall prevalence 39.1%. The paper does not offer a mechanistic explanation for the RCA's near-total sparing under this definition, describing it only as "notable" — [Clinical Implication of Coronary Tortuosity in Patients with Coronary Artery Disease, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0024232)
- A tortuosity-severity-index (TSI) study reports that among patients with any coronary tortuosity, "almost 80%" was in the LAD, especially at SYNTAX-score segments 7 and 8, and that segments 13/14 (LCx) were the next most affected; 65% of cases were single-vessel. The paper restricted analysis to vessels ≥2.5 mm caliber, implying an explicit (if coarse) vessel-caliber gate before scoring, but does not separately report numeric TSI values per vessel — [Validity of tortuosity severity index, PMC6303525](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6303525/)
- The RCA displaces roughly twice as much as the left coronary artery during the cardiac cycle in tagged-MRI motion tracking, with centroid displacements >5 mm and maximum bounding-box displacements >1 cm on average across volunteers; RCA motion is predominantly right-left directed, distinct from the left system's motion pattern, consistent with its course through the atrioventricular groove around the tricuspid orifice — [summarized from a search snippet of "Displacement and velocity of the coronary arteries: cardiac and respiratory motion," PubMed 16524092](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16524092/?dopt=Abstract); **flagged as sourced only from a search-engine summary, not independently verified against the full text** — direct WebFetch of this PubMed page returned only a reCAPTCHA wall, so the exact numbers could not be confirmed against the primary source in this session.

### Inferences
- Because two commonly used tortuosity indices (arc-chord TI vs. discrete angle-count) rank LAD/LCx/RCA in opposite orders in the same population, a pooled "coronary tortuosity" score computed vessel-agnostically is ambiguous: its value depends on which vessels happen to dominate the sample and which index is chosen, not on a stable underlying biological quantity. This is a direct, literature-grounded argument for stratifying any coronary tortuosity metric by named vessel before comparison or thresholding.
- The RCA's distinctive combination (high continuous curvature, low discrete-bend count, ~2x cardiac-cycle displacement of the left system, course around the AV groove) is consistent with it having a different baseline geometric "shape class" from LAD/LCx rather than merely a different severity along a shared scale — supporting per-vessel norms/thresholds rather than a single pooled cutoff.
- The TSI paper's caliber gate (≥2.5 mm) and its finding that tortuosity concentrates in specific SYNTAX segments (7/8 in LAD, 13/14 in LCx) suggests that even within a named vessel, sub-segment location matters, but that is explicitly out of scope here (assigned to another researcher on segment-level effects) — flagged only as a boundary note.

### Gaps
- No source found gives numeric tortuosity indices specifically for diagonal branches or the left main (LM) trunk as distinct from LAD/LCx/RCA; the literature retrieved treats "coronary tortuosity" as a three-vessel (LAD/LCx/RCA) construct and does not appear to routinely score diagonals or LM separately. This should be flagged as an evidence gap rather than assumed to mean diagonals/LM behave like their parent vessel.
- Could not independently verify the exact RCA-vs-LCA displacement numbers from the primary PubMed/full-text source (paywalled/CAPTCHA-blocked); only a search-snippet summary was obtained. A report relying on these exact figures should re-verify from the full text before quoting them as confirmed.
- No source directly decomposed how much of the RCA-vs-LAD/LCx tortuosity difference is attributable separately to caliber vs. length vs. motion vs. anatomical course; the studies found each mention one or two of these factors qualitatively but none ran a multivariable decomposition isolating vessel identity's independent contribution.

## Key Question 2: How does retinal vascular imaging handle stratification by vessel class before computing tortuosity?

### Takeaway
Retinal tortuosity research treats artery/vein identity as a first-class stratification variable, not just an analysis convenience: arteries and veins differ in mean tortuosity, in heritability, and in which genetic loci associate with tortuosity, and large-scale studies report artery-specific, vein-specific, and combined scores as separate, complementary outputs rather than defaulting to one pooled score. Quadrant (temporal vs. nasal) is a second, independent stratification axis also documented to carry systematic tortuosity differences.

### Cited Findings
- Across multiple retinal datasets, veins consistently show higher tortuosity than arteries in healthy eyes, and diabetic retinopathy amplifies this artery-vein separation — [search-derived summary spanning several retinal tortuosity papers, incl. arxiv.org/pdf/2301.01791](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.01791); **this specific comparative claim was obtained from a search-engine synthesis across sources, not verified by direct fetch of a single primary paper's full text in this session** — flagged accordingly.
- The UK Biobank retinal-vessel-tortuosity GWAS ran separate artery-specific and vein-specific genome-wide association analyses in addition to a combined-vessel analysis, and found 116 significant loci for arteries and 63 for veins; combining overlap across all three analyses gave 175 independent lead SNPs. Heritability differed by vessel type: h²SNP = 0.23 (SE 0.020) for arteries vs. h²SNP = 0.15 (SE 0.021) for veins, vs. 0.25 (SE 0.025) for the combined-vessel analysis — [Genome-wide Association Studies of Retinal Vessel Tortuosity, PMC10149284](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10149284/) (verified by direct fetch of full text)
- The paper does not explicitly argue that pooling is invalid; it presents artery-specific, vein-specific, and combined analyses as complementary, and notes the combined analysis actually surfaced additional loci beyond the sum of the two vessel-specific analyses — [PMC10149284](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10149284/)
- Tortuosity genes identified (COL4A2, ACTN4, LGALS4, LGALS7, LGALS7B, TNS1, MAP4K1, EIF3K, CAPN12, ECH1, SYNPO2) were reported as overexpressed in arteries and heart muscle specifically, linking arterial-specific tortuosity to distinct structural/vascular pathways from venous tortuosity — [PMC10149284](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10149284/)
- Independent of the artery/vein axis, retinal tortuosity and branching are reported to be more extensive in temporal than nasal quadrants, with temporal arterioles roughly evenly split between "wavy" and "tortuous" morphology while nasal arterioles are mostly straight; the four terminal arteriole classes (superior nasal, inferior nasal, superior temporal, inferior temporal) are named as anatomically and functionally distinct territories with no anastomoses between them — [search-derived summary, not independently verified by direct fetch of a single cited primary source](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/retinal-arteriole); flagged as summary-level, not primary-verified.
- The superior papillary trunk take-off angle averages ~50° (acute) while the inferior papillary trunk averages ~122° (obtuse), a geometric asymmetry between superior and inferior branch classes documented at the point where quadrant-specific vessels originate — [search-derived summary](https://www.imaios.com/en/e-anatomy/anatomical-structures/superior-temporal-retinal-arteriole-1553667772); flagged as summary-level.

### Inferences
- The retinal field's practice of running artery-specific and vein-specific GWAS in parallel, and finding different heritability and different loci for each, is a concrete precedent for "vessel identity changes the statistical properties of the tortuosity measurement itself," not just its mean value — this is stronger evidence than a simple mean-difference finding, because it shows the two vessel classes are governed by partially distinct underlying biology.
- The additional temporal/nasal and superior/inferior quadrant effects suggest retinal tortuosity stratification in practice is at least two-dimensional (vessel-type x quadrant), which is a useful analogy if the coronary tree analysis is considering a similarly multi-axis stratification (e.g. vessel identity x segment position), though the segment-position axis is explicitly out of this researcher's scope.

### Gaps
- Did not find a source stating explicitly "you must not pool artery and vein tortuosity" as a normative methodological rule; the evidence found is descriptive (different heritability/loci) rather than prescriptive. A report should characterize this as strong indirect justification, not a direct citable rule.
- Did not verify primary full text for the artery/vein mean-tortuosity-difference claim or the quadrant-asymmetry claims; these came from search-engine synthesis across multiple unfetched sources. Flag any use of these specific numbers as needing re-verification.

## Key Question 3: How does cerebral/intracranial tortuosity literature (Bullitt et al. and successors) handle named-segment stratification?

### Takeaway
Bullitt's original tortuosity metrics (Sum-of-Angles Metric / SOAM, Inflection Count Metric / ICM, distance/relative-length metric) were developed as generic per-vessel-segment measures applied to whatever vessel a region-of-interest contained (e.g. tumor-associated vasculature), rather than being tied to named large-vessel identity; downstream intracranial literature, by contrast, explicitly separates specific named segments — most clearly the carotid siphon — from the rest of the ICA/circle-of-Willis for both measurement and clinical-risk analysis, and at least one recent paper shows a segment-restricted score outperforming a global pooled score for a clinical outcome.

### Cited Findings
- SOAM sums the angles between consecutive point-triples along a vessel centerline and normalizes by path length; it is elevated by high-frequency, low-amplitude coiling. Bullitt and colleagues' methodology (introduced 2003, "Measuring Tortuosity of the Intracerebral Vasculature From MRA Images") computes SOAM (and related ICM) per-vessel-segment within a region of interest, then reports the regional average — i.e., the original framework is segment-generic (any traced vessel piece), not keyed to named large-artery identity such as "MCA" vs. "ACA" — [summarized from search results referencing Bullitt et al. 2003 methodology](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/10582739_Measuring_Tortuosity_of_the_Intracerebral_Vasculature_From_MRA_Images); flagged as search-summary level, original 2003 paper full text not directly fetched in this session.
- The carotid siphon (the S-shaped terminal segment of the ICA spanning the posterior bend of the cavernous segment through the C5/clinoid and C6/ophthalmic segments to the ICA bifurcation) is measured and analyzed as a distinct anatomical unit using three specific angular parameters (posterior angle α, anterior angle β, clinoid-ophthalmic angle γ), not as part of a pooled whole-ICA tortuosity score. Reported values (aneurysmal vs. healthy sides): β angle 16.95° vs. 21.35° (optimal threshold 18.25°); α angle 85.55° vs. 103.55°; γ angle 81.30° vs. 115.35° — smaller angles (sharper bends / more tortuous siphon) associated with paraclinoid aneurysm presence — [Increased Carotid Siphon Tortuosity Is a Risk Factor for Paraclinoid Aneurysms, PMC9127410](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9127410/) (verified by direct fetch)
- The stated reason for treating the siphon separately: it is "the primary flexible part of the carotid artery" and "one of the sites highly susceptible to vascular lesions," representing a naturally tortuous segment with sharp bends distinct from the straighter cervical/petrous ICA — [PMC9127410](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9127410/)
- A separate paper on ACA tortuosity and ACoA aneurysm risk reports segment-specific tortuosity metrics for the A1-A3 ACA segments specifically (not pooled with ICA or MCA): mean relative length (RL) 0.60 ± 0.23, mean SOAM 0.23 ± 0.18, mean product-of-angle-distance (PAD) 0.11 ± 0.12, mean triangular index (TI) 0.50 ± 0.16, mean ICM 0.09 ± 0.12 — [search-derived summary, likely from the Springer ACA/ACoA paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00330-019-06146-3); flagged as search-summary level, not independently verified by direct fetch (the fetch of this specific numeric claim was not performed against full text in this session; treat as unverified pending primary-source check).
- A 2024 Scientific Reports study explicitly contrasts a "global" pooled tortuosity index against a segment-restricted one: Global Tortuosity Index (GTI, standardized mean curvature across the entire intracranial arterial tree) was independently associated with intracranial atherosclerotic burden (adjusted OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.09-1.62, n=516: 274 no ICAS, 140 low burden, 102 high burden), but the association **strengthened** when tortuosity was computed restricted to the basal arteries only (adjusted OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.22-1.81) — [Global intracranial arterial tortuosity is associated with intracranial atherosclerotic burden, Scientific Reports / PMC11636879](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11636879/) (title/abstract-level findings from search synthesis; full text fetch was attempted but blocked by an authentication redirect, so exact methodology detail beyond these headline numbers could not be verified in this session — flag as abstract-level, not fully primary-verified).

### Inferences
- The intracranial literature's pattern is: use a generic, segment-agnostic tortuosity metric (SOAM/ICM/etc., following Bullitt), but apply it selectively to anatomically and clinically motivated named segments (carotid siphon, basal arteries, A1-A3) rather than to the whole tree indiscriminately, specifically because those segments carry disproportionate clinical signal (aneurysm risk, atherosclerotic burden association). This is a "same metric, different scope" pattern rather than a "different metric per vessel" pattern.
- The GTI vs. basal-artery-restricted comparison (OR 1.33 pooled vs. OR 1.48 restricted) is the clearest quantitative example found across all fields of a pooled, tree-wide tortuosity score being measurably weaker than the same score restricted to an anatomically/pathophysiologically relevant vessel subset, for the same clinical endpoint, in the same cohort. This is strong, if not perfectly clean (no formal Simpson's-paradox sign reversal, just an attenuated effect size), evidence that pooling across vessel identity can dilute a real, vessel-specific effect.
- No source found gives a documented case of a full sign reversal (a true Simpson's-paradox instance) specifically for tortuosity; the strongest documented case is attenuation/dilution (GTI vs. basal-restricted), not reversal.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch Bullitt et al.'s original 2003/2004 full text directly (search results returned secondary summaries and citing papers, e.g. arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14195 and the Carolina Digital Repository record, rather than full original text); the claim that the original method is applied per-ROI/per-segment generically rather than per-named-vessel is inferred from multiple secondary descriptions, not confirmed by reading Bullitt's primary methodology section directly.
- Could not verify the ACA A1-A3 numeric values or the GTI/basal-artery study's full methodology against primary full text (Nature.com redirect blocked access; PMC mirror PMC11636879 was found but not fetched due to tool-budget constraints in this session). A report drawing on these specific numbers should re-fetch and confirm before treating them as verified.
- No source found addresses MCA M1 vs M2, or vertebral-artery-specific tortuosity norms with the same level of quantitative rigor as the carotid siphon or ACA A1-A3 findings; the search only surfaced a qualitative claim that ICA/vertebral/MCA (smaller-caliber, higher-curvature vessels) are "frequently tortuous" without segment-specific numeric distributions.

## Key Question 4: Is there a documented general principle for when pooling across anatomically distinct sub-units is invalid or misleading?

### Takeaway
The general statistical principle (Simpson's paradox / confounding from heterogeneous subgroups) is well documented in the general biostatistics literature, and the vascular-tortuosity literature reviewed here provides a concrete, domain-specific instance consistent with that principle (the GTI-vs-basal-artery-restricted attenuation, Key Question 3) plus strong indirect evidence from retinal genetics (different heritability and loci by vessel type, Key Question 2) and from coronary anatomy (metric-dependent, even sign-reversing, rankings across LAD/LCx/RCA, Key Question 1). No source found documents a clean, named "Simpson's paradox in tortuosity" case study with an explicit sign reversal, so the connection between the general statistical principle and this specific domain is an inference this report draws, not a citation that states it directly.

### Cited Findings
- Simpson's paradox is defined generally as a statistical phenomenon where an association or trend present in the aggregated (pooled) data reverses or disappears when the data is broken into meaningful subgroups, and vice versa; it is caused by a confounding variable whose distribution differs across the subgroups being pooled — [Simpson's Paradox, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson's_paradox); [Simpsons Paradox Explained, Statistics By Jim](https://statisticsbyjim.com/basics/simpsons-paradox/)
- "A Simpson's paradox driven by heterogeneous effects arises as subgroup associations differ from one another; some may align with the overall aggregated association, while others oppose it" — and this is explicitly noted as common when subgroups vary widely in size and behavior, which is the general structural condition (heterogeneous subgroup effect sizes/directions) that would apply to pooling tortuosity across anatomically and hemodynamically distinct named vessels — [Computational Social Scientist Beware: Simpson's Paradox in Behavioral Data](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.08615)
- Standard methodological guidance from meta-analysis practice: "Proper meta-analysis proceeds on the basis of weighting the effects rather than pooling the data, either by a fixed effects model or (if the study populations are heterogeneous) by introducing a random effect of the study in the statistical model" — i.e., the general remedy for heterogeneous sub-unit pooling is stratified/weighted analysis, not raw pooling — [On Biostatistics and Clinical Trials: Simpson's Paradox](http://onbiostatistics.blogspot.com/2024/04/simpsons-paradox-in-integrated-analyses.html)
- Domain-specific instance consistent with this principle: pooling intracranial tortuosity across the whole arterial tree (GTI) showed a weaker association with atherosclerotic burden (adjusted OR 1.33) than the same tortuosity construct restricted to the basal arteries alone (adjusted OR 1.48), in the same cohort of 516 patients — [Global intracranial arterial tortuosity, PMC11636879](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11636879/) (abstract-level, see Key Question 3 verification caveat)
- Domain-specific instance consistent with this principle: coronary tortuosity rankings across LAD/LCx/RCA reverse depending on which index is used (arc-chord TI ranks RCA highest; discrete angle-count ranks RCA lowest), meaning a single pooled score computed from a mixture of these vessels, or reported without specifying the index, cannot be unambiguously interpreted — [PMC10795752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10795752/), see Key Question 1

### Inferences
- No source in this search directly states "pooled tortuosity scores across vessel identity are a Simpson's-paradox risk" — that specific connection is this researcher's inference, built by combining (a) the general statistical definition/mechanism of Simpson's paradox and pooling-of-heterogeneous-subgroups guidance, with (b) the two concrete domain findings (GTI attenuation; TI/angle-count rank reversal) that structurally match the paradox's preconditions (subgroup-specific effects differing in magnitude and, in the coronary case, in rank order). A report author should present this as a reasoned argument supported by analogous evidence, not as a directly citable "known Simpson's paradox case in tortuosity."
- The strongest available general-principle argument for stratifying a proposed coronary tortuosity method by vessel identity is therefore three-legged: (1) general statistics says pooling heterogeneous subgroups risks confounded/attenuated/reversed associations; (2) the coronary-specific evidence shows metric-dependent rank reversal across LAD/LCx/RCA, matching precisely the kind of subgroup heterogeneity the general principle warns about; (3) the closest available intracranial analogue (GTI vs. basal-restricted) shows measurable attenuation of a real clinical association when pooling across the whole tree instead of restricting to the relevant named subset.

### Gaps
- Did not find a published case study, in any vascular tortuosity subfield, explicitly framed by its authors as "a Simpson's paradox" with a full sign reversal on a headline endpoint. The evidence assembled here is analogical/inferential, and this should be stated plainly to the report writer so the argument is not overstated as directly citable.
- Did not find general biostatistics literature specifically addressing anatomical/vascular pooling (as opposed to social-science or clinical-subgroup examples); the general Simpson's-paradox sources found are domain-general (behavioral data, clinical trial subgroups), and the bridge to vascular anatomy is this researcher's inference rather than a direct citation.
