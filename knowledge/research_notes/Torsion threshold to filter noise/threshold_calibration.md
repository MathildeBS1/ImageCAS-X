# Calibrating a noise-floor threshold for torsion without outcome leakage

## Leakage-free ways to determine a noise floor for a noisy geometric measurement (independent of the outcome)

### Takeaway
The rigorous version of "torsion with a built-in minimum" exists in the applied-math literature under exactly this name: analytically-defined space curves with known ground-truth curvature and torsion are discretized, perturbed with simulated noise, and run through the same Frenet-Serret estimation pipeline (spline reconstruction, finite differences) to build a first-order variance bound on the estimator's error, which is what a "known-zero" or "known-value" phantom curve would give you for a real centerline pipeline. Coronary-artery-specific evidence for the complementary approach (inter/intra-observer segmentation-based reproducibility) also exists, but no source found used a synthetic zero-torsion vessel phantom or a scan-rescan CTA protocol; both remain gaps for this project's specific pipeline.

### Cited Findings
- A 2026 paper models curvature and torsion estimation error under the Frenet-Serret framework for analytically-defined space curves (i.e., curves where the true curvature and torsion are known exactly, including the straight-line and planar-curve special cases where they are exactly zero), discretizes them, adds simulated noise, reconstructs via cubic spline / cubic Hermite spline interpolation, and derives a Curvature Stability Index (CSI) and Torsion Stability Index (TSI) as first-order variance bounds under the delta method to characterize noise amplification theoretically, cross-checked against Monte Carlo simulation for empirical variance, stability ranges, and error amplification — [Spline-Based Smoothing of Noisy Discrete Curves in the Frenet-Serret Framework (Axioms, 2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/axioms15050365)
- The same paper's core finding is that torsion is theoretically more susceptible to perturbation than curvature because of its dependence on third-order derivatives, and that "higher-order derivatives required for computation of these geometric invariants exhibit pronounced sensitivity to measurement noise" — [Spline-Based Smoothing of Noisy Discrete Curves (Axioms, 2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/axioms15050365)
- General differential-geometry sources confirm curvature measures deviation from a straight line and torsion measures deviation from planarity, so a straight-line synthetic centerline gives ground-truth curvature = torsion = 0, and a planar synthetic centerline gives ground-truth torsion = 0 with nonzero curvature — useful as the two canonical "known-zero" test objects for a noise-floor calibration — [Curvature and torsion diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Curvature-and-torsion-Curvature-measures-the-deviation-from-a-straight-line-and-torsion_fig3_266325523)
- Separately, discrete-curve literature on knot shapes notes that "at full original resolution, the curvature and torsion plots of discrete knots are not reliable since they contain small scale details that must be seen as artifacts of the discretization," and recommends a Gaussian-weighted moving average filter to suppress noise without over-smoothing real curvature/torsion features — this is a pipeline-independent (not outcome-dependent) diagnostic for discretization noise — [The shapes of physical trefoil knots (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.03254)
- For coronary arteries specifically, a study compared manual vs AI-derived segmentations of the same cardiac PET/CT scans (i.e., two independent processing pipelines on identical imaging data, not two different patients or two different outcomes) and computed ICC via a two-way mixed-effects, absolute-agreement model across 428 radiomic features per modality per coronary artery; this is a directly analogous "independent-of-outcome" reliability design — [Auto-segmentation, radiomic reproducibility... coronary arteries (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034606/)
- In that coronary study, reproducibility was low overall: for CT, only 133/428 features (35.8%) were "reproducible" (ICC ≥ 0.75) under intra-observer (repeat manual segmentation) comparison, and only 48/428 (12.6%) under inter-observer comparison; AI-vs-manual agreement was 147/428 (34.3%) — showing that even well-studied radiomic features are frequently unstable under repeat processing of the same scan, before any outcome is considered — [Auto-segmentation, radiomic reproducibility... coronary arteries (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034606/)
- The RIDER lung cancer dataset (patients rescanned twice within 15 minutes) is the standard test-retest imaging dataset used in the general radiomics reproducibility literature to estimate feature stability from repeat acquisitions of the same patient, independent of any outcome label — [Comparing effectiveness of image perturbation and test-retest imaging (Sci Rep)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10600245/)
- The IBSI (Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative) provides digital phantoms and benchmark reference values specifically so that feature-extraction pipelines can be validated against a known ground truth, independent of any clinical outcome, and this phantom-based validation approach has been extended to shape/geometric-type features across different toolkits — [Evaluating feature extraction reproducibility... using a digital phantom (J Appl Clin Med Phys, 2025)](https://aapm.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acm2.70110); [IBSI narrative review (Cureus/PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12640658/)
- An image-perturbation-chain method (repeated random small perturbations — translation, rotation, volume adaptation — applied to a single segmentation/scan) has been proposed by Zwanenburg et al. as an alternative to genuine test-retest imaging when repeat scans of the same patient are not available, producing a distribution of feature values from one scan that estimates the same kind of measurement variance a test-retest study would — [Radiomics feature reliability systematic review (QIMS)](https://qims.amegroups.org/article/view/73273/html)

### Inferences
- The single most directly transferable, low-cost, outcome-independent calibration for this project is the synthetic-curve approach from the Axioms 2026 paper: generate analytically-defined 3D curves (straight lines, circular arcs, helices, general Frenet curves) with exactly known curvature/torsion, discretize them at the same voxel spacing / point spacing as the actual centerline extraction pipeline, run them through the *same* centerline-smoothing and torsion-computation code used on real coronary centerlines, and record the distribution of "noise torsion" the pipeline reports for cases whose true torsion is exactly zero (straight or planar test curves) or exactly some other known value. This requires no patient data, no repeat scans, and cannot leak outcome information because no patient or outcome ever enters the calibration.
- A complementary, still outcome-independent check would be an image-perturbation-chain analysis (small random perturbations of the segmentation mask or centerline points, repeated N times per real patient scan) to see how much the computed torsion value changes under processing-level noise alone — using Zwanenburg et al.'s method as substitute for real test-retest imaging.
- Both of these are much cheaper than acquiring new phantom or repeat-scan data, and both operate entirely upstream of any outcome/label, so a threshold set from either is defensible as leakage-free by construction, not merely by discipline.

### Gaps
- No source found describes a synthetic "known-zero-torsion" vessel-shaped phantom test specifically for coronary centerline extraction pipelines (segmentation → skeletonization → centerline smoothing → Frenet-frame torsion), only the general differential-geometry / applied-math version (Axioms 2026) and generic discrete-curve/knot literature. Applying it to this project's actual centerline-extraction pipeline (not just to a torsion formula in isolation) is not something the literature has already done and would need to be built.
- No coronary-CTA-specific scan-rescan (same patient, two acquisitions) reliability study of centerline torsion was found; the closest analog (PMC12034606) uses repeat *segmentation* of the same scan, not repeat *acquisition*, so it captures segmentation/processing noise but not acquisition-level noise (patient motion, contrast timing, reconstruction kernel differences between two real scans).

## Radiomics/quantitative-imaging feature reproducibility literature: thresholds and standard workflow

### Takeaway
There is a well-established two-stage workflow in radiomics — screen features for reproducibility/stability (via ICC from test-retest, inter/intra-observer, or perturbation-chain designs) strictly before any outcome modeling, then only pass features above a pre-set ICC cutoff (commonly 0.75 or 0.9) into predictive modeling — but the literature itself documents no single agreed cutoff, wide heterogeneity in what "reliable" means across studies, and no explicit methodological requirement that this screening happen before outcome-looking (it is best practice, observed inconsistently).

### Cited Findings
- Koo and Li's widely cited classification: ICC < 0.5 poor, 0.5-0.75 moderate, 0.75-0.9 good, ≥0.9 excellent — used as the reference scheme in multiple radiomics reproducibility studies, including the coronary-artery PET/CT study above — [Radiomics feature reproducibility under inter-rater variability (Sci Rep)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-69534-6); [Auto-segmentation, radiomic reproducibility... coronary arteries (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034606/)
- "A cut-off ICC value ensuring reproducible features has not yet been established" in the radiomics field generally, though ICC > 0.75 or ICC > 0.9 are the most commonly proposed choices — [Radiomics feature reproducibility under inter-rater variability (Sci Rep)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-69534-6)
- A systematic review of ICC use in radiomics found substantial heterogeneity: upper-bound cutoffs of >0.7, >0.75, >0.8, and >0.9 were all "frequently used" to define the highest reliability tier, and lower-bound cutoffs of <0.2, <0.4, and <0.5 were all frequently used to define the lowest tier, with study counts spanning binary through six-level reliability schemes — [Radiomics feature reliability assessed by ICC: a systematic review (QIMS)](https://qims.amegroups.org/article/view/73273/html)
- The same systematic review found the dominant reproducibility design was intra/inter-observer segmentation variability (416 of 481 included studies), with test-retest imaging and image-perturbation chains as less common alternatives — [Radiomics feature reliability: systematic review (QIMS)](https://qims.amegroups.org/article/view/73273/html)
- Radiomics features are often described as "highly repeatable" only when they jointly satisfy ICC > 0.9 AND coefficient of variation (CV) < 10%, i.e., a stability screen commonly combines a correlation-type criterion (ICC) with a dispersion-type criterion (CV) rather than relying on ICC alone — [Repeatability of Cardiac MR radiomics (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7738466/) (cross-referenced against the general search summary; CV<10% AND ICC>0.9 combined criterion is reported across several of the search-summarized radiomics reproducibility papers)
- One study found "higher testing performance... at higher feature ICC thresholds, but it dropped significantly at ICC = 0.95," with similar optimal reliability achievable at ICC = 0.9 — i.e., using an overly strict reproducibility cutoff can discard usable signal, so the cutoff itself is a tradeoff, not a fixed constant — (source surfaced only via aggregated search summary; original paper not independently fetched, flagged as lower-confidence)
- IBSI has achieved "strong or better consensus" for the mathematical definitions and cross-software agreement of 169 of 174 standardized features across two validation phases, substantially reducing implementation-driven (as opposed to biological or noise-driven) variability for shape and first-order features especially — [IBSI narrative review (Cureus/PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12640658/)
- The QIMS systematic review explicitly notes, as a critical gap in the field it reviewed, that it "does not explicitly address whether studies screened features for stability before building predictive models to prevent data leakage" — i.e., even the meta-literature does not confirm this is uniformly done correctly — [Radiomics feature reliability: systematic review (QIMS)](https://qims.amegroups.org/article/view/73273/html)

### Inferences
- The field-standard workflow that would apply directly here is: (1) compute torsion (or the thresholded torsion feature) on repeat measurements of the same scan/patient that do NOT depend on the outcome label (repeat segmentation, perturbation chains, or ideally repeat acquisition), (2) compute an ICC (or CV) purely from that repeated-measurement data, (3) decide inclusion/exclusion, or a threshold, from that ICC/CV value alone, and only afterward, with the decision frozen, proceed to fit the outcome model. This ordering, not any single numeric cutoff, is what makes the workflow leakage-free; the field has not converged on a single cutoff (0.75 vs 0.9 vs a combined ICC+CV rule are all defensible with citation), but is unanimous that whatever cutoff is used, it should be set from reproducibility data alone.
- Given that even well-established coronary radiomic features achieve only 12-36% "reproducible" rates under real segmentation-repeat testing, and torsion is a higher-order (worse) quantity than most first-order/shape radiomic features, an a priori expectation should be that raw (unthresholded) torsion will very likely fail a 0.75-0.9 ICC bar; the open empirical question this project would need to answer is whether *thresholded* torsion (zeroing sub-threshold values) can be pushed into the reproducible range, and by how much floor is required.

### Gaps
- No source specifies a standard or recommended minimum number of repeat measurements (or repeat patients) needed specifically to certify a *derived/thresholded* transformation of an unstable base feature (as opposed to certifying the raw feature) — this project would need its own calibration study design, not a literature-supplied number.
- No radiomics reproducibility source addresses centerline/topological (as opposed to voxel-intensity texture) features directly; torsion sits outside the feature classes IBSI has standardized (first-order, shape, texture), so no citable IBSI reference value or consensus benchmark for torsion specifically exists.

## Safeguards for tuning a threshold on training data (nested cross-validation and equivalents)

### Takeaway
If a torsion threshold cannot be fixed from outcome-independent data alone and must instead be tuned using the training cohort, nested cross-validation (threshold search confined to an inner loop, generalization performance measured only on an outer loop the threshold search never saw) is the standard safeguard, and it is specifically recommended in the literature for small-sample settings — but it should be understood as validating whether the *chosen* threshold generalizes, not as removing the researcher-degrees-of-freedom cost of having searched for it at all.

### Cited Findings
- Nested cross-validation "allows for both performance estimation and algorithm selection or hyperparameter tuning and is useful for small datasets," and consists of an inner loop responsible for model/hyperparameter selection and an outer loop responsible for estimating generalization accuracy, so that "each performance score from the outer loop [is] based on data the model never saw during tuning" — [A Guide to Cross-Validation for AI in Medical Imaging (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10388213/); [Model Evaluation, Model Selection, and Algorithm Selection in ML (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.12808)
- For very limited sample sizes, plain k-fold cross-validation "produces strongly biased performance estimates," which is why nested cross-validation is specifically recommended to obtain "robust and unbiased performance estimates regardless of sample size," providing an "almost unbiased estimate of true error" — [Practical Considerations and Applied Examples of Cross-Validation... Tutorial (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11041453/)
- On threshold/cutpoint selection specifically (not just general hyperparameters): "outcome-oriented cut-point selection, if not properly validated, can produce biased estimates of effect size and substantially inflated Type I error rates," and "different cut-points can lead to contradictory conclusions from the same dataset, creating a high risk of data-dredging bias" — [Optimal cut-point definition in biomarkers (BMC Med Res Methodol)](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12874-015-0009-y) (cross-referenced against aggregated search summary of cut-point literature)
- "Internal validation strategies such as cross-validation and bootstrap resampling are widely used to assess stability in cut-point selection or model performance," but "even with such approaches, selected thresholds may remain unstable under resampling, reinforcing the need for validation across independent cohorts" — i.e., internal (nested-CV-style) validation reduces but does not eliminate instability risk for a tuned cutoff in a small cohort; independent/external validation is called for as the stronger check — (from aggregated summary of the optimal cut-point selection literature; original per-study citations not independently re-verified beyond the summary, flagged as moderate confidence)
- "A tailored fit that doesn't fit all: the problem of threshold overfitting in diagnostic studies" (2025) frames threshold overfitting as occurring specifically "when thresholds are derived and evaluated within the same dataset," which "frequently inflates sensitivity and specificity estimates," and identifies "misuse of post hoc thresholds" and "the frequent absence of independent validation" as the core mechanisms — [A tailored fit that doesn't fit all (De Gruyter, 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1515/dx-2025-0096) (abstract-level access only; full text not retrievable, see Gaps)
- For hyperparameter search cost specifically, the statistical "degrees of freedom" literature treats a data-driven search over a parameter space (including something as simple as searching for the best-placed knot or cutoff) as adding to the effective number of parameters fit by the procedure, i.e., a searched-for threshold is not "free" even if the final model reports it as a single fixed number — [Degrees of Freedom and Model Search (arXiv, Tibshirani)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.1920)
- In a full nested-CV design for tuning a single threshold, this specifically translates to: within each outer fold, split the remaining training data further into an inner train/validation split (or inner k-fold); grid-search candidate torsion thresholds on the inner split only, select the threshold that (say) maximizes inner-fold discrimination or matches an inner-fold reliability criterion; freeze that threshold; evaluate the resulting model (predictor computed using that threshold) on the untouched outer test fold; repeat across all outer folds and pool the outer-fold performance estimates as the reported, deleakage-corrected estimate.

### Inferences
- Nested cross-validation is the correct machinery if this project insists on tuning the torsion threshold from outcome data, but note the composability problem: this project's Model 1 is already small (8 parameters on presumably a small cohort), and nested CV for a single hyperparameter on a small cohort produces noisy, unstable "optimal" thresholds across outer folds (a symptom the cut-point literature explicitly names: "selected thresholds may remain unstable under resampling"). A tuned threshold that varies substantially fold-to-fold is itself evidence the threshold is not well identified by the data and should be reported as such rather than collapsed into a single reported cutoff.
- Given the project already has an established EPV/predictor-budget accounting exercise (referenced in the assignment as Model 1's 8-parameter budget), the correct place to fold in the cost of threshold tuning is that same budget, not a separate exemption (see next section) — nested CV manages generalization-performance bias, but it does not manage the "how many models could I have reported" multiplicity problem addressed in the next question.

### Gaps
- The full text of "A tailored fit that doesn't fit all" (De Gruyter, 2025) could not be retrieved (blocked by a reCAPTCHA/paywall on both the PubMed and DOI landing pages); only the abstract-level summary was accessible. Its explicit list of recommended safeguards (beyond "transparent reporting" and "rigorous risk of bias classification," both from the abstract) is not independently confirmed.
- No source was found giving a rule of thumb for how many outer folds / repeats are needed to get a stable nested-CV estimate specifically for a single scalar threshold hyperparameter (as opposed to a full model) on a cohort in the 100s-of-patients range typical of this kind of thesis project; this would need to be worked out from general nested-CV small-sample guidance rather than a threshold-specific citation.

## Does a thresholded torsion feature count as one new predictor, or an extra researcher degree of freedom against the predictor budget?

### Takeaway
By both the clinical-prediction-modeling literature's own accounting logic (PROBAST/TRIPOD-adjacent sample-size and complexity guidance) and by the general "garden of forking paths" argument (Gelman & Loken), a threshold that was tuned rather than fixed a priori from independent data is not "free": it must be counted as consuming additional degrees of freedom / effective model complexity beyond the one nominal predictor slot, because it represents a data-contingent choice that could have gone a different way and would have produced a different reported result.

### Cited Findings
- PROBAST's risk-of-bias framework explicitly flags, as increasing risk of bias, situations "when the predictors included in the final model are selected from many candidate predictors, relative to a low number of participants with the outcome, and when predictor selection was based on univariable analysis" — this is the general principle (predictor/parameter selection informed by outcome-looking inflates optimism) that a data-tuned threshold instantiates in miniature — [PROBAST: A Tool to Assess Risk of Bias... Explanation and Elaboration (Annals of Internal Medicine)](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/full/10.7326/M18-1377) (title/abstract-level access; full text paywalled, see Gaps)
- In sample-size/EPV accounting for clinical prediction models, "the number of events relative to the number of regression coefficients needs to be estimated for candidate predictors," and a single categorical predictor with multiple categories already "requires 5 degrees of freedom" for 6 categories — establishing the general principle that a predictor's "true cost" in the budget is its effective number of estimated/searched parameters, not a flat 1 unit per named variable — [Statistical primer: sample size considerations (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)
- Formal statistical "degrees of freedom in model search" work shows that even simple in-sample optimizations over a parameter (e.g., best cutpoint, best knot location) increase the effective degrees of freedom of the fitted model beyond the nominal count of terms in the final formula — [Degrees of Freedom and Model Search (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.1920)
- Gelman and Loken's "garden of forking paths" argument: researcher degrees of freedom inflate the false-positive rate "even when there is no fishing expedition or p-hacking and the research hypothesis was posited ahead of time," because analysts make data-contingent decisions (their named examples include "exclusion criteria... removing outliers or invalid cases based on observed data" and "threshold selection... choosing cutoffs that maximize effects or significance") without treating each decision as an implicit additional comparison — [The garden of forking paths (Gelman & Loken)](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/unpublished/p_hacking.pdf)
- Their recommended mitigations are preregistration of the analysis plan (including, by direct extension, prespecifying the threshold or the exact outcome-independent procedure that will set it), holding out data for independent validation, reporting robustness across the plausible alternative specifications that were not chosen, and transparently documenting all analytical choices considered rather than only the final path — [The garden of forking paths (Gelman & Loken)](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/unpublished/p_hacking.pdf)
- The cut-point literature frames the identical concern for thresholds specifically: "arbitrary cut-point selection can lead to overfitting and spurious conclusions, highlighting the need for a statistically rigorous and reproducible framework," and outcome-oriented cutpoint choice risks "data-dredging bias" — [Optimal cut-point definition in biomarkers (BMC Med Res Methodol)](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12874-015-0009-y)

### Inferences
- Reasoning from these sources together: whether the threshold "counts as one new predictor" or as something heavier depends entirely on how it was set, not on what it is called. If the threshold is fixed from a source fully independent of the outcome (synthetic-curve noise floor, image-perturbation-chain ICC screen, or a pre-registered fixed rule such as "3 x the noise-floor SD from the synthetic-curve calibration"), then thresholded torsion behaves like any other single derived predictor and should cost exactly the 1 predictor-parameter slot that any other new feature would cost in the budget, since no outcome-contingent search occurred. If instead the threshold is selected by cross-validated search over candidate cutoffs against the outcome (even inside a nested-CV scheme that correctly reports unbiased generalization performance), it should be counted as more than one predictor's worth of researcher degrees of freedom: it is a tuned hyperparameter with its own effective degrees of freedom (per the model-search DoF literature), and per Gelman & Loken it is exactly the kind of data-contingent forking choice ("threshold selection... choosing cutoffs that maximize effects") that inflates the false-positive rate of whatever else the model claims, independent of whether the CV procedure itself is unbiased.
- Practically, for an 8-parameter budget already near its estimated safe limit: the leakage-free path (outcome-independent noise-floor calibration, e.g., the synthetic-curve or perturbation-chain approach from the first key question) is the only version of "torsion with a built-in minimum" that can be added at the stated cost of one ordinary predictor. The outcome-tuned path should either (a) be charged more than 1 unit in the predictor-budget/EPV accounting (there is no universal citable conversion factor for exactly how many extra "equivalent predictors" a single tuned threshold costs; the literature establishes the direction and mechanism, not a numeric conversion), or (b) be reported with full transparency as an exploratory, non-prespecified analysis requiring independent/external validation before being treated as confirmatory, per both PROBAST's bias framework and the forking-paths mitigations above.

### Gaps
- No source gives a specific numeric "conversion rate" (e.g., "a tuned threshold costs N equivalent predictor degrees of freedom") that could be dropped directly into this project's existing 8-parameter accounting; the literature supports the qualitative direction (tuned thresholds cost more than their face value) but not a formula. This project would need to either treat the tuned-threshold path as categorically excluded from the fixed predictor budget (safest, matches PROBAST's spirit) or derive its own DoF-inflation estimate via simulation (e.g., Tibshirani-style search-degrees-of-freedom estimation applied to this specific threshold search), which is outside what a literature search alone can supply.
- Full text of the PROBAST "Explanation and Elaboration" paper could not be retrieved (paywalled at Annals of Internal Medicine and the PDF was returned in an unparseable binary-encoded form); the exact wording of its signaling questions on dichotomization/thresholding was not independently confirmed beyond what is quoted above from the abstract-accessible material.

## How much additional data leakage-free calibration would require, and whether it likely already exists in this cohort

### Takeaway
The genuinely leakage-free option (synthetic analytically-defined test curves) requires zero additional patient data and can be built from the existing centerline-extraction code alone; the second-best option (image-perturbation-chain reliability screening) requires zero additional scans, only repeated re-runs of the existing single-scan-per-patient pipeline under small simulated perturbations; the strongest option (genuine test-retest CTA or repeat independent segmentation) requires data this project's single-scan-per-patient cohort does not have and is not likely to already exist in a typical dataset of this kind.

### Cited Findings
- The Axioms 2026 synthetic-curve sensitivity-analysis approach uses only analytically-defined curves (straight lines, helices, arbitrary Frenet curves) generated in code, not patient imaging data at all, and derives its stability indices via delta-method variance bounds plus Monte Carlo simulation on those synthetic curves — [Spline-Based Smoothing of Noisy Discrete Curves (Axioms, 2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/axioms15050365)
- Zwanenburg et al.'s image-perturbation-chain method was explicitly proposed "as an alternative to test-retest imaging" for exactly the situation where repeat acquisitions of the same patient are not available, generating a distribution of feature values from repeated small perturbations (translation, rotation, volume adaptation) of a single existing scan/segmentation — [Radiomics feature reliability: systematic review (QIMS)](https://qims.amegroups.org/article/view/73273/html)
- The dedicated test-retest imaging design used elsewhere in the radiomics field (e.g., the RIDER lung dataset, patients rescanned within 15 minutes) requires a purpose-built repeat-acquisition protocol that a standard single-scan-per-patient clinical cohort, such as an ImageCAS-style CTA dataset, does not routinely include — [Comparing effectiveness of image perturbation and test-retest imaging (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10600245/)
- The coronary-specific reproducibility study used a *repeat-segmentation* (not repeat-acquisition) design: the same underlying CT/PET scans were segmented twice (manual repeat, inter-observer, and AI-vs-manual), which needs zero new patient scans, only additional segmentation labor/compute on scans already in hand — [Auto-segmentation, radiomic reproducibility... coronary arteries (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034606/)
- Sample-size guidance for ICC-based reliability studies indicates that estimating an ICC with reasonable precision (e.g., targeting ICC ≥ 0.75 within ±0.20 CI width) needs on the order of 20 participants with repeated measurements, and that estimating variance components with tighter precision (±0.05, 80% confidence) can require anywhere from about 10 to over 300 participants depending on the variance structure — giving a rough scale for how many repeat measurements a project-run reliability calibration would need if it chose the repeat-segmentation or perturbation-chain route — [Sample size recommendations for studies on reliability and measurement error (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10742-022-00293-9)

### Inferences
- Ranked by data cost from lowest to highest: (1) synthetic analytic-curve noise-floor calibration needs no patient data at all and is achievable purely in code against the existing centerline/torsion pipeline; (2) perturbation-chain reliability screening needs no new scans, only repeated re-processing (segmentation perturbation + re-extraction) of a subset of the existing cohort, on the order of ~20 patients per the reliability-study sample-size guidance above, to get a usable ICC estimate; (3) repeat-segmentation-by-a-second-observer needs additional expert labor on existing scans but no new imaging; (4) genuine scan-rescan CTA test-retest needs a prospective repeat-imaging sub-study this project's existing single-scan-per-patient retrospective CTA cohort almost certainly does not have, and re-scanning patients purely for a reliability calibration is unlikely to be ethically or logistically feasible within a thesis timeline.
- Given the project's known cohort structure (single CT angiogram per patient, matching the general pattern in ImageCAS-style datasets), only options (1) and (2) are realistically available without new data collection; option (3) is available if a second rater/observer is willing to re-segment a subset; option (4) should be treated as infeasible for this thesis and its absence should be explicitly recorded as a limitation if raw ASSD-style ground-truth-independent validation is ever claimed.

### Gaps
- Whether this specific ImageCAS-X-derived cohort has any existing repeat scans, repeat segmentations, or a second independent rater's segmentations already on hand was not established by this research (it is a question about this project's actual data holdings, not something a literature search can answer) and should be checked directly against the project's data inventory rather than assumed.
