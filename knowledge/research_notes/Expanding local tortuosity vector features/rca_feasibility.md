# RCA-Specific Feasibility of Torsion, Bifurcation Angle, Diameter, and Other Centerline-Derived Descriptors

## How much branching does the RCA have along its main course, and is bifurcation angle a well-populated per-point feature there compared to curvature?

### Takeaway
The RCA's branching is anatomically sparse and concentrated at a small number of named landmarks (conus, SA-nodal, acute marginal, and crux/PDA-marginal split), in contrast to the LCA tree, which the anatomy literature describes as routinely bi/tri/tetrafurcating over a short left main. This means a per-point "bifurcation angle" channel would be defined (non-missing) at only a handful of locations along a typical RCA centerline, whereas curvature is defined at every sampled point by construction. RCA-specific quantitative counts of branch points per vessel were not found; the evidence is anatomical/descriptive rather than a per-vessel branch-point tally.

### Cited Findings
- The RCA gives off seven named branches along its course: conal branch, sinu-atrial (SA) nodal branch, atrial branches, anterior ventricular branches, right (acute) marginal branch, the terminal posterior descending (inferior interventricular) artery, and the atrioventricular (AV) nodal branch — [Kenhub, Right coronary artery: Anatomy, branches, supply](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/anatomy/right-coronary-artery)
- At the crux of the heart the RCA terminates by giving off one or two large terminal branches (the posterior descending artery and an inferolateral/posterolateral branch); trifurcation of the RCA is described in the literature as an "infrequent anatomical finding," reported in individual case reports rather than as a common variant — [Kenhub](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/anatomy/right-coronary-artery); [Trifurcation of the RCA, cadaveric case report](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12887354/)
- Branches of the RCA "can significantly vary in position, location, diameter and number" across individuals — [Kenhub](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/anatomy/right-coronary-artery)
- By contrast, a cadaveric study of the left coronary artery (LCA) found the LCA bifurcates in 60.0% of hearts, trifurcates in 26.7%, and shows tetra- or pentafurcation in 6.7% each — i.e. essentially 100% of LCA specimens have at least one branch point near the ostium, and a third or more have multiple close together — [Anatomical variations in coronary artery origin, branching pattern, dominance and morphometry: a cadaveric study from Northeast India](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/17233)
- Zhu et al.'s angiographic geometry study defined the RCA as three ordinal segments (proximal, middle, distal) bounded by only two internal landmarks — the ostia of the right ventricular branch and the acute marginal branch — before the vessel reaches the posterior descending branch; the LAD's segmentation used analogous but distinctly-spaced landmarks. This is consistent with the RCA having a small, fixed number of along-course branch landmarks rather than a dense, evenly distributed set — [Cataloguing the geometry of the human coronary arteries](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)
- Practical CT measurement protocols place only three standardized caliber-measurement stations along the whole RCA (proximal RCA 1.5 cm distal to the ostium, mid-RCA 1.0 cm proximal to the acute marginal branch, distal RCA 1.0 cm proximal to the terminal bifurcation) — implying that in routine clinical/CT geometric workups the RCA is treated as having very few along-course landmark sites, unlike a densely bifurcating tree — [Coronary artery lumen diameter and bifurcation angle in healthy people](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221834308_Coronary_artery_lumen_diameter_and_bifurcation_angle_derived_from_CT_coronary_angiographic_image_in_healthy_people) (via search synthesis; full text not accessible, see Gaps)
- The clinically significant RCA bifurcation, from an interventional standpoint, is essentially singular: the crux split into the posterior descending and acute marginal (or posterolateral) arteries — [search synthesis on RCA bifurcation angle literature](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9917625/) and general anatomy sources above

### Inferences
- Because curvature is a differential-geometric quantity defined continuously along any parameterized centerline, it can be sampled at every point of an RCA centerline with no missing values. Bifurcation/branch angle, by contrast, is only defined at a small, anatomically fixed set of locations (roughly 2-4 named points: conal/SA-nodal takeoffs near the ostium, the acute marginal takeoff, and the crux/PDA split) for a typical single-RCA-trunk centerline. If the thesis's "curvature profile" samples the vessel at dozens to hundreds of points, a naive per-point bifurcation-angle channel would be undefined/zero-padded for the overwhelming majority of those points — the descriptor is structurally sparse for the RCA in a way curvature is not.
- This sparsity is worse for RCA than for LAD/LCx as a *single*-artery centerline problem, but note the asymmetry: the LCA's density of branch points is concentrated right at its origin (left main splitting into LAD and LCx, then each of those may further bi/trifurcate), so "LAD-specific" or "LCx-specific" centerlines inherit one dominant early bifurcation (the LM split) plus their own downstream diagonal/marginal branches — likely still sparser along-course than curvature, but the LM bifurcation itself is a strong, well-defined, always-present first feature that has no RCA analogue of comparable prominence (the RCA's ostial takeoff from the aorta is a takeoff angle, not a bifurcation, see next section).
- Given this, a per-point bifurcation-angle channel for RCA-only modeling is likely to be a mostly-empty/sparse feature unless the model design explicitly handles it as an event-based (few nonzero entries) rather than dense channel, e.g., encoding it as a small number of scalar covariates (angle at acute-marginal takeoff, angle at crux) rather than a per-sample profile entry.

### Gaps
- No source gives a direct RCA-only quantitative count of "branch points per unit length" or "bifurcations per vessel" analogous to the LCA's 60/26.7/6.7% bi/tri/tetrafurcation breakdown; the RCA-side comparison here is inferred from the branch-naming anatomy and the segment-landmark conventions rather than a directly reported per-vessel tally. This is a genuine evidence gap: the anatomy literature describes RCA branch *anatomy* (named branches) far more than RCA branch *point density* along the extracted centerline as an image-processing/geometry problem.
- The bifurcation-angle CT paper (lumen diameter and bifurcation angle derived from CT in healthy people) could not be fully retrieved (paywalled/ResearchGate); its RCA-specific angle values and count of measured bifurcation sites per vessel are not confirmed beyond the search snippet.

## How does coronary dominance affect RCA branching pattern, length, and PDA origin, and does it confound RCA-specific angle/topology features?

### Takeaway
Dominance is a major, well-documented anatomical variable specific to the RCA/PDA relationship (it does not have an LAD-side analogue), and it materially changes both RCA length and its terminal branching pattern; the literature explicitly flags dominance as a source of confounding for angle-based coronary measurements, so any RCA topology feature derived near the crux/PDA region should be expected to covary with dominance class unless it is controlled for or dominance is included as a covariate.

### Cited Findings
- Dominance is classified strictly by the origin of the posterior descending artery (PDA): right-dominant when the PDA arises from the RCA, left-dominant when it arises from the LCx, and codominant when both contribute — [Right coronary artery, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_coronary_artery); [Coronary Dominance on Coronary CT Angiography: Prevalence and Potential Implications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363422/)
- Prevalence estimates: roughly 80-85% right-dominant, 7-13% left-dominant, 2-5% codominant/balanced in the general population, though one autopsy-based coronary angiogram study reports right dominance in 86.7% of hearts and notes prevalence of left/balanced dominance decreases with increasing patient age at autopsy — [search synthesis, coronary dominance prevalence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30725892/); [Prevalence of left and balanced coronary dominance decreases with age](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1054880712000324)
- A cadaveric study specifically reports RCA length by dominance: in cases where RCA length was in the 8-14 cm range, the artery was dominant in 65% of cases, indicating RCA length itself correlates with (or is a marker of) dominance status — [Right coronary artery length and dominance table](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Right-coronary-artery-length-and-dominance_tbl1_320765447)
- There is a dedicated cadaveric study of "Terminal branching pattern of the right coronary artery in left-dominant hearts," implying that the RCA's terminal branch topology (not just whether it reaches the crux) is qualitatively different when the heart is left-dominant versus right-dominant — [Terminal branching pattern of the RCA in left-dominant hearts](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1054880712001056) (abstract-level only; full text not retrieved, see Gaps)
- In codominance, the PDA territory / posterior interventricular septum is supplied by branches from both the RCA and the LCx, i.e., the terminal "bifurcation" region of the RCA is anatomically shared with, and truncated by, a contribution from the LCx — [PDA/dominance search synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_coronary_artery)
- Dominance pattern is explicitly identified in a clinical-outcomes review as a potential *confounder* for other measurements: in a study of exercise electrocardiographic testing (EET), left dominance was significantly more prevalent among patients with normal angiograms despite an abnormal EET, "producing false positive results" — the authors explicitly label left dominance a confounding factor for that other test — [Anatomical variations / dominance confound synthesis](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/17233); also discussed in [Clinical Significance of Coronary Arterial Dominance: A Review, JAHA](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.123.032851) (full text behind 403; see Gaps for direct confirmation of specific numbers from that review)
- Dominance is likely a heritable, developmentally-determined trait: recent work links coronary dominance establishment during development to sites of CXCL12 expression, and shows CXCL12-linked variation in coronary anatomy across diverse human populations — [CXCL12 drives natural variation in coronary artery anatomy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10635223/)

### Inferences
- Because the RCA's terminal segment (where it either continues as, gives rise to, or is truncated before the PDA) changes identity, length, and caliber depending on dominance, any centerline-derived feature computed near the crux — a bifurcation angle at the terminal split, a curvature or torsion value in the terminal-most centimeters, or the overall vessel length used to normalize a tortuosity index — will systematically differ between right-dominant, left-dominant, and codominant hearts for anatomical reasons unrelated to CAD. This is a genuine confound risk specific to the RCA: the LAD does not have an equivalent "does the vessel terminate here or continue" ambiguity driven by a named, prevalent (~15-20% of the population, when combining left/codominant) alternate anatomy.
- A practical implication for the CAD-prediction model: if bifurcation angle or a terminal-segment feature is added for the RCA, dominance class (derivable from whether the segmented tree shows RCA-to-PDA continuity) is a natural companion covariate or stratification variable, similar to how age/sex are already included. Omitting it risks the model learning a dominance signature rather than (or in addition to) a disease signature at the terminal RCA.

### Gaps
- Full text of the JAHA "Clinical Significance of Coronary Arterial Dominance" review could not be retrieved (403 error) — its specific pooled statistics on outcome associations by dominance class, and any explicit discussion of dominance as a confound for CT-derived geometric/angle biomarkers specifically, are not confirmed beyond the secondary sources cited above.
- The cadaveric "terminal branching pattern of the RCA in left-dominant hearts" paper's actual findings (what specifically differs in left-dominant terminal branching versus right-dominant, e.g., number of terminal branches, angle differences) were not retrievable in full text; only its existence and title-level scope are confirmed.
- No source quantifies how much variance in a specific centerline-derived feature (e.g., terminal bifurcation angle, or curvature near the crux) is attributable to dominance class versus to disease status — this would require a dedicated geometry study stratified by dominance, which was not found for the RCA.

## How reliable/reproducible is torsion computed from CT-derived centerlines, compared to curvature? Is torsion known to be noise-sensitive as a third-derivative-type quantity?

### Takeaway
Torsion is consistently treated in the vascular-geometry literature as a higher-order, more noise-sensitive quantity than curvature because it requires a third derivative of the centerline coordinates; multiple sources describe dedicated smoothing/discrete-geometry techniques specifically to stabilize its estimation, but no source found here reports a direct, quantitative reliability/reproducibility comparison (e.g., a coefficient of variation or inter-run variability number) between torsion and curvature computed from real, possibly noisy CT-derived coronary centerlines specifically.

### Cited Findings
- General vascular-centerline literature: "To calculate the curvature and torsion of the vessel centerline, spatial derivatives must be computed from discrete coordinate points. Traditional finite difference methods may cause large errors and amplify noise, especially when computing third-order derivatives, so a discrete geometry method with adjustable window size and noise robustness is used to improve stability of high-order derivative estimation." — [search synthesis of centerline geometry / torsion noise literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.04215) and related sources
- "The discrete spatial coordinates inevitably contain noise during centerline extraction. A standard Gaussian smoothing filter is applied directly to the sequence of extracted 3D coordinates, which reduces microscopic coordinate jitters and limits noise amplification before the extraction of geometric invariants." — [search synthesis, torsion/curvature noise handling](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Curvature-torsion-and-tortuosity-of-the-internal-carotid-artery-centerline_fig7_24432621)
- A 2026 arXiv paper on discrete-geometry tortuosity feature measurement for blood vessel morphology classification builds a six-feature set that explicitly separates curvature-related features ($\mathcal{AC}$, $\mathcal{TC}$, $\mathcal{AC}/\mathcal{AT}$) from torsion-related features ($\mathcal{AT}$, $\mathcal{TT}$), noting torsion-related features "contribute additional information for more detailed morphological classification" — implying torsion is treated as a distinct, worthwhile-but-separate signal requiring its own dedicated discrete-geometry (not naive finite-difference) computation — [A Hybrid Framework for Blood Vessel Morphology Classification](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14195)
- In the RCA/LAD angiographic geometry study (Zhu et al., 52 patients, 35 RCA + 32 LAD vessels reconstructed from biplane cineangiography), torsion, curvature, and tortuosity were all computed from reconstructed 3D vessel axes and used in a PCA-style decomposition, but the paper reports no explicit statement comparing measurement noise or reliability between torsion and curvature — this specific reliability comparison was not addressed even in one of the more rigorous vessel-geometry-from-imaging papers found — [Cataloguing the geometry of the human coronary arteries](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)
- Clinical literature independently confirms MPR-based image analysis "may limit the prediction of functional stenosis, as it does not account for the effects of coronary artery curvature and torsion," treating torsion as a real but under-exploited signal in CT coronary workups generally, without RCA-specific reliability discussion — [search synthesis, torsion/curvature/CT functional stenosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11502768)

### Inferences
- The consistent methodological pattern across the (non-RCA-specific) vessel-geometry literature — dedicated smoothing, adjustable-window discrete-geometry estimators, and treating torsion as needing separate, more careful derivation than curvature — is itself strong indirect evidence that torsion computed via naive finite differences on a discretized, algorithmically-extracted coronary centerline (as opposed to a densely-sampled, hand-digitized, or heavily smoothed research centerline) would be substantially noisier than curvature. This matches the general numerical-analysis expectation stated in the task's key question (third-derivative quantities amplify high-frequency centerline noise more than second-derivative quantities).
- No paper found here reports torsion computed directly from an automated CT segmentation-derived centerline (as opposed to hand/semi-manual biplane cineangiography reconstruction, which is comparatively smooth and low-noise). This is a meaningful gap: the RCA is anatomically the more tortuous-at-low-frequency but geometrically "simpler" single vessel, and if the thesis's own centerline extraction pipeline (topology/ modules, per project context) introduces voxel-level jaggedness, torsion reliability could be materially worse in practice than any published number would suggest, since none of the found studies used a comparable automated, voxel-resolution extraction+smoothing pipeline.

### Gaps
- No direct quantitative reliability/reproducibility statistic (e.g., ICC, coefficient of variation, test-retest, or algorithmic vs. manual comparison) for torsion versus curvature computed specifically from CT-derived coronary centerlines was found. This is an explicit, load-bearing evidence gap for the thesis's decision: the literature supports the *qualitative* claim that torsion is noisier (via general numerical-derivative reasoning and the existence of dedicated stabilization methods) but not a *quantitative* RCA-specific or even CT-coronary-specific number.
- No RCA-specific study of torsion measurement reliability (inter-observer, inter-algorithm, or repeat-scan) was found; the Zhu et al. RCA/LAD study is the only one found that reports RCA torsion values by segment, and it does not address reliability.

## What is known about RCA luminal diameter along its course, and is diameter typically a normalization factor or an independent feature? Is it confounded by segmentation quality/partial volume effects?

### Takeaway
RCA diameter tapers from proximal to distal with well-documented reference ranges from standardized CT measurement stations, and in the one RCA-specific FFR-CT vessel-morphology study found, diameter (and a diameter-derived volume/length ratio) was used as an *independent* predictor rather than purely as a normalization denominator for curvature/tortuosity; however, none of the retrieved sources explicitly discussed partial-volume or segmentation-quality confounds for RCA diameter measurement specifically, which is a real reliability gap for CT-derived diameter as a feature.

### Cited Findings
- Standardized CT measurement protocol places three caliber-measurement stations along the RCA: proximal RCA 1.5 cm distal to the ostium, mid-RCA 1.0 cm proximal to the acute marginal branch, and distal RCA 1.0 cm proximal to the terminal bifurcation — [search synthesis, RCA diameter measurement locations](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221834308_Coronary_artery_lumen_diameter_and_bifurcation_angle_derived_from_CT_coronary_angiographic_image_in_healthy_people)
- General CCTA interpretation guidance: the lumen "should be examined for overall caliber and narrowing that exceeds the expected gradual tapering of the vessel," i.e., gradual proximal-to-distal tapering is the expected normal pattern against which focal narrowing (stenosis) is contrasted — [SCCT 2026 update, Interpretation and Reporting of Coronary CTA](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1934592526004806)
- Absolute lumen sizing outperforms percent-stenosis for predicting hemodynamically relevant lesions: lumen area <1.8 mm² and diameter <1.2 mm were the most accurate cutoffs for functionally significant stenosis in one quantitative CCTA study (not RCA-specific; pooled across vessels) — [Quantitative coronary CT angiography: absolute lumen sizing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5052288/)
- RCA-specific FFR-CT vessel-morphology study (non-obstructive CAD): using vessel length, proximal/distal lumen diameter, lumen volume, and a novel volume-to-length (V/L) ratio, RCAs with FFRCT ≤0.80 (positive) had smaller proximal diameter (3.9 ± 0.8 mm vs 4.6 ± 1.0 mm, p<0.0001), smaller distal diameter (2.4 ± 0.5 mm vs 2.9 ± 0.6 mm, p<0.0001), smaller lumen volume (825.7 ± 255.0 mm³ vs 1113.0 ± 337.9 mm³, p<0.0001), and lower V/L ratio (6.5 ± 1.6 vs 9.9 ± 2.4, p<0.0001) than RCAs with FFRCT >0.80; vessel length itself was not significantly different (118.3 ± 22.3 mm vs 113.4 ± 17.4 mm, p=0.11) — [Impact of vessel morphology on CT-derived FFR in non-obstructive CAD in the RCA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10873436/)
- In that same study, proximal diameter remained an independent predictor in multivariable analysis (β=0.09, p=0.04), and the V/L ratio was a stronger independent predictor (β=0.48, p=0.03) than diameter alone — i.e., diameter (and a diameter/length composite) was used as a direct predictive feature, not merely as a normalizing denominator for a separate curvature/tortuosity metric — [same source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10873436/)
- The same study reports "good repeatability and reproducibility" for its measurements (referenced to supplementary intra-/inter-observer figures) but does not detail this in the main text, and does not discuss segmentation or partial-volume artifacts as a source of diameter measurement error — [same source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10873436/)
- Separately, Zhu et al.'s RCA/LAD geometry study explicitly did *not* include lumen diameter in its analysis despite noting it could in principle be obtained from the same angiograms, stating axial (centerline) geometry "is not the sole determinant of the mechanical environment" — implying that at least in the geometry-focused literature, diameter/caliber is sometimes treated as a separate axis of analysis from curvature/torsion/tortuosity rather than being folded in as a normalization factor for those centerline metrics — [Cataloguing the geometry of the human coronary arteries](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)

### Inferences
- The one RCA-specific study that actually used diameter (the FFR-CT vessel-morphology paper) used it as a standalone predictive feature (and combined it multiplicatively with length via the V/L ratio) rather than as a divisor to normalize a curvature or tortuosity index. This suggests that, at least in RCA hemodynamic-outcome literature, diameter is valued for its own physiological information (larger, higher-volume vessels are hemodynamically healthier / less likely to show reduced FFR) rather than purely as a scale-correction term for shape descriptors. This somewhat cuts against a default assumption that diameter's "natural" role is as a curvature-normalizer; the retrieved evidence more directly supports it as an independent channel, at least for RCA CAD/FFR-relevant prediction.
- No retrieved source directly confirms or refutes the partial-volume/segmentation-quality confound concern for RCA diameter in CT specifically. Given that RCA distal diameters in the normal range are frequently near or below 2.5-3 mm (approaching typical CT voxel dimensions and partial-volume blur scales), this is a plausible and thesis-relevant concern that the literature located here does not directly address — it should be treated as an inferred risk, not a documented finding, and flagged as such if used in the thesis.

### Gaps
- No source explicitly discusses partial-volume effects, voxel size relative to RCA lumen diameter, or lumen-segmentation-quality confounds for CT-derived RCA diameter measurement. This is a notable absence given how central this concern is to the key question; it likely needs to be sourced from CT physics / segmentation-validation literature rather than the coronary-geometry-clinical literature covered here.
- The precise numeric tapering profile of a "typical" RCA (e.g., mean diameter at each of the three standard stations, and how strongly it depends on right- vs. left-dominance or on total RCA length) was not retrieved beyond the FFR-CT study's two-group (FFRCT positive/negative) comparison and the general station-location description; a true normative diameter-by-position curve for the RCA, stratified by dominance, was not found.

## Are there RCA-specific tortuosity/curvature/geometric biomarker studies (vs. generic "coronary artery" or LAD-focused) reporting on CAD/stenosis association in the RCA specifically?

### Takeaway
RCA-specific geometry studies do exist and are separable from pooled or LAD-focused work, but they are comparatively few and are split between two clusters: (1) a rigorous vessel-geometry characterization study (Zhu et al.) that establishes RCA-specific curvature/torsion/tortuosity reference values by segment but does not test CAD association, and (2) newer RCA-specific angle/morphology-vs-CAD or vs-FFR studies (RCA-aorta angle; RCA vessel-morphology-vs-FFRCT) that do test clinical association but focus on a single ostial angle or on volumetric/diameter descriptors rather than a dense curvature/tortuosity profile. A study directly linking a dense RCA curvature or torsion *profile* to CAD/stenosis presence, comparable to what the thesis proposes, was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Zhu et al. (52 patients, 35 RCA + 32 LAD, angiographically normal vessels, biplane cineangiography reconstruction): RCA curvature, torsion, and tortuosity were smallest in the *middle* segment (median curvature 0.58 cm⁻¹ mid vs 0.66 proximal / 0.62 distal; median torsion 2.03 cm⁻¹ mid vs 2.46 proximal / 2.91 distal; median tortuosity 0.06 mid vs 0.16 proximal / 0.17 distal), a U-shaped along-course pattern distinct from the LAD, where curvature/torsion/tortuosity were generally highest distally. Torsion was more than 30% higher in the LAD than the RCA overall (p<0.0001), and >80% of total geometric variance was captured by two factors, one curvature/tortuosity-dominated and one torsion-dominated. This paper explicitly separates RCA from LAD and is the clearest RCA-specific quantitative reference for curvature/torsion/tortuosity found in this research, though it studied angiographically *normal* vessels as a geometry-cataloguing exercise, not a CAD-association study — [Cataloguing the geometry of the human coronary arteries](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)
- An RCA-specific ostial angle study (250 patients CCTA, 91 normal / 159 CAD) found the RCA-to-aorta takeoff angle was significantly smaller in the CAD group (79.07° ± 24.88°) than in the normal group (92.08° ± 19.51°, p=0.001), with excellent inter-observer reproducibility (ICC r=0.999, p<0.001); narrower angles were also associated with smoking and higher BMI. This is a genuine RCA-specific, CAD-associated geometric finding, but it is a single ostial takeoff angle (RCA origin relative to the aortic root), not an internal bifurcation angle or a dense along-vessel profile feature — [Investigation of the Relationship between RCA-Aorta Angle and CAD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9917625/)
- An RCA-specific FFR-CT vessel-morphology study (non-obstructive CAD cohort) found smaller RCA proximal/distal diameter, smaller lumen volume, and lower volume/length ratio in RCAs with reduced FFRCT (≤0.80) versus preserved FFRCT (>0.80), all p<0.0001, with proximal diameter and V/L ratio remaining independent predictors in multivariable analysis. This is RCA-specific and disease/functional-severity-associated, but is diameter/volume-based rather than curvature/torsion/tortuosity-based — [Impact of vessel morphology on CT-derived FFR in the RCA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10873436/)
- By contrast, a pooled dynamic-geometry CCTA study (137 arteries: 53 RCA, 45 LAD, 39 LCx from an unspecified number of patients) explicitly analyzed vessels "on artery level" without stratifying by vessel type, and found no significant relationship between dynamic-cycle changes in curvature/tortuosity/inflection-point-count and degree of stenosis or plaque type for the pooled set — i.e., RCA-specific signal (if any) is not separable from this study's reported results — [Assessment of Dynamic Change of Coronary Artery Geometry and CAD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7165136/)
- A broader, non-RCA-specific finding repeatedly surfaced in search results: coronary tortuosity as a general imaging biomarker "is associated with CAD after adjusting for age, sex and risk factors," but the specific study behind this claim was not vessel-stratified in the retrieved snippet, so it cannot be confirmed as RCA-separable — [search synthesis, tortuosity as CAD biomarker](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13239-019-00403-8) (full text behind institutional login; not independently verified, see Gaps)

### Inferences
- The clearest pattern across the RCA-specific literature found here is a division of labor: studies that characterize RCA curvature/torsion/tortuosity in detail (Zhu et al.) do so on normal anatomy without a disease outcome, while studies that link an RCA-specific geometric measure to disease/functional severity (the aorta-angle study, the FFR-CT vessel-morphology study) use simpler, single-value descriptors (one ostial angle; diameter/volume/length) rather than a dense per-point curvature or torsion profile. This suggests the specific combination the thesis proposes — a dense per-point curvature (and candidate torsion/angle) profile tested against RCA CAD presence — does not yet have a direct RCA-specific precedent in the literature surveyed; the thesis would be filling a genuine gap rather than replicating an established RCA-specific pipeline.
- Given that the one study with the most granular RCA curvature/torsion/tortuosity data (Zhu et al.) used only normal vessels and did not test disease association, and the two disease-associated RCA studies used only single-value or volumetric descriptors, there is no existing RCA-specific evidence either supporting or refuting that a dense torsion profile channel would add predictive value beyond curvature for CAD classification specifically in the RCA. The absence of such a study is itself the most decision-relevant finding for the thesis: the choice to add torsion as an extra channel is not currently backed by an RCA-specific outcome study either way.

### Gaps
- No RCA-specific (or RCA-stratified) study directly testing a dense curvature or torsion *profile* (per-point along the centerline) against CAD presence/severity was found; existing RCA-disease-association studies use single-value angle or volumetric descriptors instead.
- The Springer paper on RCA geometric parameters and hemodynamic performance (ostium area, side-branch angles, tortuosity, curvature, cross-sectional area) could not be accessed in full (institutional login wall); its RCA-specific numeric findings and branch-count details are not confirmed beyond the search-result snippet, which does describe RCA-specific angle/tortuosity/curvature/cross-sectional-area analysis at side-branches — this is a promising RCA-specific source that should be chased down through an institutional subscription if possible, since it appears to combine geometry (including angle) with RCA branch structure directly.
- The pooled 137-artery dynamic-geometry study (PMC7165136) reports its 53-RCA subgroup count but does not give RCA-only statistics, so it is a confirmed instance of "RCA pooled with LAD/LCx, not separable" for the specific claim about dynamic geometric change and stenosis/plaque association.
- No RCA-specific study of bifurcation angle (as opposed to ostial takeoff angle) versus CAD/stenosis was found; the aorta-angle study is the closest RCA-specific angle-CAD finding but measures a different anatomical angle than an internal branch-point angle.
