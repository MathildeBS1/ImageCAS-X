# Let regression lead, let segments sharpen

**Regression should stay the primary model at about 2000 Herlev–Østerbro participants. XGBoost moves up from an optional check to a prespecified secondary model, and an outcome-trained curvature encoder becomes affordable, but only as a cross-fitted exploratory arm.** The participant is the unit of information, not the segment. Under the assumptions stated below (35 to 50% of participants with new or progressing plaque, about 25 to 30 candidate parameters), the Riley criteria at a conservative anticipated R² support roughly **25 parameters**. That is enough for one carefully specified model but leaves no room for spending degrees of freedom freely. The roughly 30,000 segments add information mainly about how geometry varies within a person. Because everyone is included whatever their baseline plaque, baseline plaque becomes both a predictor and a source of reverse causation through outward remodeling. The primary model should therefore be **incident plaque in segments that were plaque-free at baseline**, fitted as a complementary log-log (cloglog) model with a log-interval offset and a participant random intercept. Its segment hazards sum to a participant risk. Progression in diseased segments is secondary, and a sensitivity analysis restricted to plaque-free participants removes reverse causation entirely. "Does geometry add to risk factors" is still answered by a block test on unpenalized fits, and penalized fits are reserved for prediction. XGBoost on summary features and a late-fusion network on the curvature profile answer different questions. XGBoost checks whether the regression missed nonlinearity or interaction among the summaries. The network asks whether the order of curvature along the artery carries information the summaries miss, and only a MiniRocket control and a shuffled-profile control can show that. The draft methodology needs its Study Population and Outcome and Statistical Analysis sections rewritten, plus smaller changes in three other sections.

## Two thousand participants buy about 25 parameters, not 30,000 observations

The earlier version of this analysis assumed a few hundred participants, all plaque-free at baseline, with a binary outcome per artery domain. That design gave 1 to 10 events per candidate parameter. The real design differs on every one of those points, so the sample-size argument has to be redone from first principles, with the unknown quantities stated as assumptions.

| Quantity | Assumed value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Participants with baseline and follow-up CCTA | about 2000 | given |
| Plaque-free at baseline | about 54% (about 1,080) | 46% of 9,533 CGPS CCTA participants had plaque ([ACC journal scan](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2023/04/03/16/15/subclinical-coronary-atherosclerosis)); assumes the rescanned subset resembles the full cohort |
| New plaque among plaque-free participants, 3 to 5 years | 25 to 35% | 35.6% over a median 3.6 years in 402 clinically referred PARADIGM patients without baseline plaque ([Won et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9655903/)); a general population is probably lower |
| New or progressing plaque among plaque-positive participants | 50 to 70% | assumption; depends heavily on how progression is defined |
| Participants with any event | about 700 to 1,000 (35 to 50%) | arithmetic from the rows above |
| Segments per participant | 16 to 18 | SCCT-type segment scheme |
| At-risk (plaque-free) segments | about 30,000 | arithmetic |
| Incident segment events | about 500 to 1,500 | assumption: 1 to 2 new segments per incident participant |
| Latent intraclass correlation (ICC) of incident plaque across a person's segments | 0.1 to 0.4 | no published value found; estimate from the data |

**For participant-level predictors, only participants count.** Riley et al.'s first criterion requires an expected shrinkage factor of at least 0.9, which gives n ≥ P / ((S − 1) ln(1 − R²cs / S)) with S = 0.9. The other two criteria require optimism in Nagelkerke R² of at most 0.05 and the overall risk estimated within ±0.05 ([pmsampsize manual](https://rdrr.io/pkg/pmsampsize/man/pmsampsize.html); [Riley et al., Stat Med 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519266)). No anticipated R² has been published for incident plaque. A commonly used conservative default is 15% of the maximum Cox–Snell R². At an event proportion of 0.3 to 0.5, the maximum is 0.71 to 0.75, so the default is about 0.11. That works out to **76 to 80 participants per candidate parameter, or about 25 parameters at n = 2000 and about 13 parameters in the 1,080 plaque-free participants.** If Cox–Snell R² is 0.20, which is plausible once baseline plaque is a predictor, the figure falls to about 40 per parameter, or 50 parameters at n = 2000. Criterion (iii) needs only about 320 to 390 participants, and criterion (ii) is not binding in these scenarios. These figures were computed directly from the published formula, not with the software, and the 15% default should be checked against the BMJ paper before it is cited.

The parameter count is close to that limit. The risk-factor model alone uses about 15 degrees of freedom: age and LDL as 3-knot splines, sex, smoking in three levels, systolic pressure, diabetes, BMI, lipid-lowering therapy, dominance, family history, and a baseline plaque burden term (for example a spline of segment involvement score). The geometry block adds another 10 to 15: three bifurcation angles, two daughter-ratio terms, Δ_HK, mean curvature, two or three FPCA shape scores, and lumen volume to myocardial mass. In the conservative scenario, **2000 participants support one pooled model with a prespecified geometry block, but not per-artery models, many interactions, or splines on every geometry feature.** The cheapest reductions are to share curvature and FPCA slopes across LAD, LCx and RCA, and to fix K (the number of FPCA scores) on ImageCAS-X.

**Segments add information mainly for geometry that varies within a person.** For a cluster-level covariate, the Kish design effect is about 1 + (m − 1)·ICC. With m = 16 and an ICC of 0.1, 0.2 or 0.4, that gives 2.5, 4.0 or 7.0, so 30,000 segments behave like roughly 12,000, 7,500 or 4,300 independent ones for a person-level quantity. That information is still bounded by the 2000 participants. For a covariate that itself has within-person correlation ρx, the design effect is roughly 1 + (m − 1)·ICC·ρx. With ρx = 0.3 (an assumption for local curvature), the design effects fall to 1.45, 1.9 and 2.8. That is why a segment-level geometry effect is far better identified than any participant-level effect. The covariate-ICC version of the formula is standard survey-sampling reasoning, but it was not retrieved from a source in this research. No published within-person ICC for incident plaque was found either ([segment-level notes, PMC3085136 for contrast at 2 to 5% in neighbourhood data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3085136/)). Estimate it in the cohort as σ² / (σ² + π²/6) on the cloglog latent scale, and report it.

There is a practical consequence for every learner. Segments from one person share risk factors, scan, reader and interval. A model tuned or validated on segment-wise splits learns person-level patterns as if it had 16 times more data than it does. Saeb and colleagues showed that record-wise cross-validation leaks subject identity and overstates accuracy, and that it is only appropriate when the deployment target is new records from people already seen ([Little et al., GigaScience 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5441396/)). Here every future patient is a new person, so **all folds, all tuning (including XGBoost early stopping), and all bootstrap resamples must be grouped by participant.**

## Baseline plaque is both a predictor and a source of reverse causation

Including everyone changes what geometry means. Glagov et al. found in 136 left-main sections at autopsy that **lumen area did not fall as stenosis rose from 0 to 40%**, because the artery enlarged outward around the plaque ([JCI Insight reference entry for Glagov, NEJM 1987](https://insight.jci.org/references/scholar/6633/B1)). In PREDICTION, segments with low shear stress went on to develop more plaque and excessive expansive remodeling over 6 to 10 months ([TCTMD](https://www.tctmd.com/news/endothelial-shear-stress-works-prediction-tool)). As a result, lumen-derived geometry at a diseased segment is partly a product of the process being predicted. Radius-based quantities are affected most directly: daughter ratio, Δ_HK, and lumen volume to myocardial mass. Centreline curvature and bifurcation angle shift less, mainly where plaque is eccentric. No CCTA study was found that measures how much each feature changes as plaque develops, so this ranking is reasoning, not a measured result.

The analysis therefore needs a hierarchy, and PARADIGM shows that splitting by baseline status is accepted practice. That registry analysed 402 patients without baseline plaque separately from 1,297 patients with non-obstructive lesions ([Won et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9655903/); [Lee et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/)).

**Primary: incident plaque in baseline plaque-free segments, all participants.** Geometry is measured at baseline in those segments, and the participant's baseline plaque burden enters as an adjustment. A plaque-positive participant still contributes the segments that were clean. Baseline plaque burden carries person-level susceptibility, which is why it belongs in Model 0.

**Secondary: progression in segments with baseline plaque.** This is a separate model, adjusted for that segment's baseline burden. Use outer-vessel or reference-diameter geometry where the reads allow it, because lumen geometry there is contaminated by remodeling. Fitting the two processes in one model with a baseline-status × geometry interaction is more efficient for the shared risk-factor terms. It does not remove the contamination, though, and it spends degrees of freedom that the budget above cannot spare.

**Sensitivity: restrict to participants with no plaque anywhere at baseline.** This removes reverse causation and person-level confounding by existing disease. With about 1,080 participants and 270 to 380 events, the same Riley arithmetic allows only about 13 parameters, so this analysis needs the reduced geometry block: pooled slopes, at most 2 FPCA scores, and no splines on geometry.

Two consequences follow. First, participant-level geometry summaries such as lumen volume to myocardial mass should be computed from plaque-free segments or from vessel contours, or reported separately for plaque-free participants. Otherwise the "who" model inherits the reverse causation that the segment restriction removed. Second, adjusting for baseline plaque changes the estimand. If geometry causes plaque, baseline plaque lies on the path from geometry to future plaque. The adjusted coefficient then answers "does geometry add beyond current disease", which is the clinically relevant incremental question but not the total effect, and the thesis should say so. Statin initiation between the scans is a time-varying confounder in all three analyses.

The participant-level composite "new or progressing plaque anywhere" remains a useful descriptive outcome. It should not be the primary inferential target, because it mixes a process geometry may cause with a process that changes the geometry.

## Segment hazards add up to participant risk

The cloglog link is what lets segment outcomes sharpen a participant-level answer. Each scan pair is an interval-censored observation, and a binary model with a cloglog link is the grouped-time proportional hazards model ([SAS PROC LOGISTIC documentation](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/63962/HTML/default/statug_logistic_sect072.htm)). With an offset of log(Δt), its coefficients are log hazard ratios under a constant hazard over the interval ([MetricGate tutorial, secondary](https://metricgate.com/docs/discrete-time-survival-cloglog/)).

Give segment s of participant i the hazard λ_is = exp(x_is β + b_i), where b_i is a participant random intercept. If segments are independent given b_i, the probability of at least one new segment over the interval is 1 − exp(−Δt_i Σ_s λ_is). **The participant hazard is the sum of the segment hazards, so the rolled-up model is again cloglog with offset log Δt.** Participants with more plaque-free segments automatically have more opportunities for an event. The logit link has no such closed form. To obtain marginal participant risk, integrate over b_i. A normal random intercept needs quadrature. A gamma frailty with variance θ gives the closed form P_i = 1 − (1 + θ Δt_i Σ_s λ_is)^(−1/θ). This derivation is this report's reasoning and should be checked against a frailty-model text before it is written up.

The roll-up is the reason to prefer a GLMM over GEE as the primary fit. A marginal fit (GEE, or GLM with participant-clustered sandwich errors) estimates population-average segment hazards. Plugging those into 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) assumes independence that the positive within-person correlation violates, so it overstates participant risk. Hubbard et al. argue that GEE is safer when the question is an average association, because mixed models rest on unverifiable random-effect assumptions ([Berkeley CTML record](https://ctml.berkeley.edu/publications/gee-or-not-gee-comparing-population-average-and-mixed-models-estimating-associations)). Subramanian and O'Malley reply that the two have **fundamentally distinct targets of inference**, and that mixed models are right when between-cluster heterogeneity matters ([PMC3085136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3085136/)). Heterogeneity in susceptibility is the substance of "who develops plaque". So use a **cloglog GLMM with a participant random intercept as primary**, and a cluster-robust marginal fit as a sensitivity for the segment hazard ratios. Conditional hazard ratios are larger in magnitude than marginal ones, so the text should say which is reported. An artery-level random intercept is weakly identified with 4 to 6 segments per artery and a rare outcome, so add it only as a sensitivity. The simplest fallback for the roll-up is a one-parameter recalibration of the summed hazard against observed participant outcomes in held-out folds. Prediction models for clustered data are calibrated only when the validation measure matches the model's level ([Wynants et al., PMC4525751](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751)).

**A within/between decomposition separates "who" from "where".** For each segment-level geometry variable, enter the participant mean x̄_i and the deviation x_is − x̄_i (the hybrid or Mundlak model). The within-person coefficient says whether a segment is more at risk because of its local geometry. That is a question about where plaque forms, and the thesis treats localization as mechanism. The between-person coefficient says whether people with more tortuous trees develop more plaque, which is the "who" estimand. Some features exist only at participant level (lumen volume to myocardial mass, the LM bifurcation) and enter the between part directly. If within and between coefficients are equal, a test of that equality licenses pooling them, and the pooled estimate borrows the precision of the within-person contrast. If only the within coefficient is non-zero, geometry explains where plaque forms but not who gets it. That would be a clean, reportable finding, not a failure. The primary "who" test is therefore the block test of the between-person geometry terms. The joint within-plus-between block is the test of "geometry adds anything".

## The block test still answers the thesis question

Several things from the earlier analysis do not depend on sample size and carry over unchanged.

The null hypotheses of no improvement in AUC, IDI and several reclassification measures are all equivalent to the null that the new coefficients are zero ([Pepe et al. 2013, PMC3617074](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3617074)). The ΔAUC test is **much less powerful than the likelihood-ratio and Wald tests, with a type I error rate below 0.006 in every configuration studied** ([Vickers, Cronin and Begg 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3042425/)). NRI tends to be positive even for uninformative markers ([Pepe et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4615606)), and decision curves need an action threshold ([Vickers, Van Calster and Steyerberg 2016](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/952669)) that asymptomatic plaque risk does not have. So the geometry question is a **block likelihood-ratio test in the GLMM**. GEE has no likelihood, so a GEE sensitivity uses a cluster-robust Wald test instead. ΔAUC, calibration and Brier score are effect sizes, not tests.

**Inference and prediction stay separate.** A likelihood-ratio statistic from a ridge fit has no chi-square reference distribution, so block tests are run on unpenalized fits. Penalized fits appear only in the predictive arm. The case for penalization is weaker at n = 2000 than at a few hundred, but the caveats still apply. Shrinkage improves calibration slopes on average but **makes them more variable and works worst when most needed** ([Van Calster et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11493)), and penalty parameters are estimated with large uncertainty at small effective sample sizes ([Riley et al. 2021](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/187644934/1_s2.0_S0895435620312099_main.pdf)). Lasso stays out, because unstable variable selection would undermine the block test. At this size, an unpenalized GLMM with a prespecified block will probably calibrate about as well as ridge. Report both, with the bootstrap distribution of the ridge penalty.

**Model 2 must be nested in Model 1.** The draft "replaces" mean curvature κ̄ with FPCA scores. The first K principal components of the centred profiles do not in general span κ̄, so the two models are not nested, and the likelihood-ratio test does not ask whether position adds to the mean. The fix is to keep κ̄ and add K scores computed from the mean-removed profiles κ(s) − κ̄. The test then has K degrees of freedom and asks only about shape beyond the mean. Registration of profiles to anatomical landmarks changed FPCA results in AneuRisk ([Sangalli et al., JASA 2009](https://sangalli.faculty.polimi.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/2009_Sangalli-Secchi-Vantini-Veneziani-JASA_compresso.pdf); [Gervini 2014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.2236)). So the arc-length origin, L, K and smoothing are all fixed on ImageCAS-X before outcomes are opened. Fixing them in advance also keeps the test from being anticonservative.

**The interval is exposure time.** Enter log(Δt) as an offset, not as a covariate. As a check, fit log(Δt) as a free term: a coefficient near 1 supports the offset. Logistic regression with an interval covariate stays as a sensitivity analysis.

**Attribution is by refit, and every model is reported against current standards.** Permutation importance forces extrapolation with correlated features, and importance needs "at least one more model" ([Hooker, Mentch and Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03151)). Every feature family, for every learner, is therefore assessed by refitting without it on identical participant folds. TRIPOD+AI is harmonized for regression and machine learning and supersedes TRIPOD 2015 ([Collins et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11025451/)). PROBAST+AI covers development and evaluation across participants, predictors, outcome and analysis ([Moons et al. 2025](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:8b15c087-459b-43fa-ad03-1f66ed6ba370)). Add the Riley and Collins stability outputs (prediction instability plot and mean absolute prediction error) for each model ([Riley and Collins 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952221/)).

What the larger n does change is the nonlinearity budget. Age, LDL and baseline plaque burden can each take a 3- or 4-knot restricted cubic spline ([Frontiers tutorial](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/epidemiology/articles/10.3389/fepid.2023.1283705/full)). Geometry still enters linearly in the primary model, with 3-knot splines as a sensitivity analysis, because the conservative budget is nearly used up. Continuous geometry is never categorized.

## Answer one: regression is primary, XGBoost is a prespecified secondary model

**The primary model is regression: the cloglog GLMM above.** The case is no longer that flexible learners cannot run at this size. It is that regression answers the thesis question directly and XGBoost does not.

The data-hungriness argument has softened but not reversed. In van der Ploeg et al.'s simulations, logistic regression reached a stable AUC at about **20 to 50 events per variable**. Random forests needed **more than 200 and were "unstable even above this"** ([PMC4289553](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4289553)). With 700 to 1,000 participant events and about 25 parameters, Herlev–Østerbro sits at roughly 28 to 40 events per parameter. That is inside regression's stable band and still well below where tree ensembles stabilized. For calibration specifically, Austin, Lee and Wang found that penalized logistic regression was **substantially less data hungry** than ensemble methods, with boosted trees in between ([PMC11539735](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539735)).

The discrimination evidence matches this. Christodoulou et al.'s review has a median n of 1,250 and median 19 predictors, close to this design. Among comparisons at low risk of bias, the difference in logit(AUC) between ML and logistic regression was **0.00 (95% CI −0.18 to 0.18)** ([ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4167c905-5171-4fc7-b157-ff36d56a73db)). The closest plaque-endpoint precedent, SCOT-HEART with n = 1,769, found that **XGBoost did not improve prediction of low-attenuation plaque burden over the risk score** ([PMC12406813](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12406813/)). In tabular benchmarks with about half the datasets under 2,300 rows, light tuning of a boosted model mattered more than the choice between trees and networks for about a third of datasets. TabPFN had the best average performance on small datasets ([McElfresh et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.02997v4)). None of those benchmarks compares against a well-specified clinical regression.

What 2000 participants do buy is a credible XGBoost arm. In GUSTO-I at only 752 participants, a random forest restricted to depth 3 already matched LASSO's individual-risk stability (MAPE 0.019 each), while a default forest reached 0.047 ([PMC10952221](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952221/)). At nearly three times that size, a shallow booster is defensible. The recommended role is a **prespecified secondary model at participant level**:

- depth 2 to 3 and strong shrinkage, with hyperparameters tuned in nested, participant-grouped cross-validation;
- fitted on the same summary features and the same baseline-plaque term;
- the same refit with and without the geometry family;
- calibration and instability plots reported.

Its job is to check that the regression did not miss a strong nonlinearity or interaction. If it does not beat the spline regression out of sample, that is reportable evidence. XGBoost can also serve as an interaction screen, but only with a split design: screen with SHAP on a discovery half of participants, encode at most two to four candidate terms in the regression, and test them on the confirmation half. In a simulation based on 47,770 Swedish participants, a SHAP screen detected only **28% of small, 83% of moderate and 100% of large** interactions ([PMC9340268](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9340268/)). At 2000, a null screen is therefore not evidence of no interaction.

Two further arms are cheap and worth including:

- **TabPFN v2 as a no-tuning tabular benchmark.** It was built for datasets of up to about 10,000 samples ([Hollmann et al., Nature 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11711098/)), though its clinical calibration needs checking.
- **GPBoost for a flexible segment-level model.** It combines tree boosting with grouped random effects ([Sigrist, JMLR 2022](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v23/20-322.html)).

Every test participant is a new cluster, so the random effect does not help prediction. Its value is in estimating the fixed function without letting event-heavy participants dominate.

XGBoost stays out of the primary role for three structural reasons:

- **No block test and no coefficients.** SHAP misattributes credit among correlated features ([arXiv 2306.11985](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.11985)), and the geometry families are correlated with each other and with age, sex and body size.
- **No native fit for this design.** The cloglog offset structure, the participant random effect and the hazard-sum roll-up are not native to it.
- **Fair comparisons favour regression.** The cardiology results that show ML gains mostly compared against fixed clinical scores, not regressions refit on the same inputs. The PARADIGM boosting model moved from **AUC 0.62 to 0.83** mainly by adding quantitative plaque features ([Han et al. 2020](https://doaj.org/article/636830fb82c14cdaaff903a3ea5e9d51)). Here plaque sits in Model 0, so geometry's increment is measured beyond plaque and should be expected to be small.

## Answer two: XGBoost on summaries and a profile network test different things

The comparison bundles two choices: the learner (trees or a network) and the input (summaries or the ordered profile). XGBoost on summaries varies the learner. A late-fusion network varies the input. A network whose profile branch outputs one scalar, which then enters a logistic or cloglog layer with the risk factors, is structurally **a regression with one learned covariate**. The network arm therefore asks whether a learned profile score adds to the summaries, and the honest comparison for it is the regression with summaries, not XGBoost.

The larger cohort changes three things about that arm.

**First, cross-fitting is now practical.** With 5 or 10 participant-grouped folds, each out-of-fold profile score is learned from 1,600 to 1,800 participants instead of a few hundred. The score then enters the unpenalized model as one covariate, so the outcome model spends one degree of freedom on it. The Riley logic applies only if the score is produced out of sample ([Riley et al. 2020](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:743761e7-58a5-4968-9195-2b266abe6ad3)). Its Wald test ignores fold-to-fold variation in the score, so repeat the cross-fitting over several fold splits and report the spread. The whole two-stage pipeline goes inside the outer folds for performance estimates.

**Second, an outcome-trained encoder becomes affordable as exploratory work, but not end-to-end training of a large one.** Consider a 1D CNN with a few hundred weights, shared across LAD, LCx and RCA with artery as an input. It would see 700 to 1,000 participant events, or 500 to 1,500 segment events that count as perhaps 200 to 800 effective events after the design effect. Networks needed **more than 100 events per variable** for a stable AUC in van der Ploeg et al.'s simulations ([PMC4289553](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4289553)). Fine-tuning only the last layer with strong weight decay, on top of a pretrained encoder, keeps the outcome-trained parameter count near that of a spline. That configuration is the affordable one. Prespecify the architecture, because data-driven tuning adds instability ([Riley and Collins 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952221/)).

**Third, the segment outcome invites a local design.** Instead of one whole-artery profile per artery-level label, the encoder can read the curvature window within and upstream of each segment and output a segment-level score. That score feeds the same cloglog GLMM and rolls up to participant risk like any other segment covariate. The design matches the local-curvature framing of the question and gives far more labels than the artery-domain design did.

Pretraining should be outcome-free and locked before outcomes are opened. ImageCAS-X supplies about 800 curated trees for self-supervised tasks such as masked-curvature prediction. The Herlev–Østerbro baseline trees themselves can also be used for pretraining without touching follow-up outcomes, and this has the added benefit of matching the scanner and population the encoder will be applied to. ImageCAS-X stays a test bench for these choices, never an analysis cohort.

Controls decide whether a profile result means anything. MiniRocket matched leading time-series classifiers on **109 UCR datasets at up to 75 times lower cost**, fitting only a linear model to the label ([Dempster et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.08791)). A cross-fitted MiniRocket-plus-ridge score is the gate any learned encoder must pass. A network fed a shuffled-order profile separates "the order matters" from "the network is a different learner". Without both controls, a positive network result cannot be interpreted. A null network result is uninformative too, because networks are hurt by weak inputs ([Grinsztajn et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08815)). Even the fusion literature's headline gain is modest and weakly founded: few of 128 studies ran a unimodal baseline, and those that did averaged a **6.4% accuracy gain**, mostly from large imaging inputs ([Kline et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9640667/)).

The resulting ablation ladder runs on shared participant folds and seeds:

1. Model 0, the risk factors with baseline plaque.
2. Model 1, adding the summaries in the regression.
3. XGBoost on the same inputs as Model 1.
4. Model 2, adding mean-removed FPCA scores.
5. Model 2 plus the cross-fitted MiniRocket score.
6. Model 2 plus the frozen pretrained encoder score.
7. Model 2 plus the cross-fitted fine-tuned encoder score.
8. The shuffled-profile control.

Each rung is reported as a paired difference in log-likelihood, Brier score and AUC, with participant-bootstrap intervals and calibration.

| | Cloglog GLMM on summaries and FPCA | XGBoost on summaries | Late-fusion profile network |
|---|---|---|---|
| Events per parameter at 700 to 1,000 events | About 28 to 40, inside the stable band | Below the stable range for ensembles; viable when shallow | Far below; affordable only frozen or last-layer tuned |
| Tests "geometry adds" | Likelihood-ratio test of within, between and joint blocks | Refit comparison only | Refit comparison with MiniRocket and shuffled controls |
| Handles offset, clustering, roll-up | Natively | Offset only through workarounds; GPBoost for clustering | Only through the downstream GLMM |
| Uses position along the artery | Through FPCA scores | Through hand-built features only | Learned |
| Role | Primary | Prespecified secondary at participant level | Exploratory, two-stage, cross-fitted |

## Conclusion

Moving from a few hundred participants to 2000 shifts the binding constraint from event count to design validity. The choices that will decide whether the thesis finds a geometry effect are no longer which learner to use. They are which segments count as at risk, how baseline plaque enters, whether folds leak participants, and how segment hazards become a participant risk. Including everyone makes the analysis more informative and more exposed at the same time: baseline plaque is the strongest predictor available, and it is also the mechanism that contaminates lumen geometry.

The within/between split turns the thesis question into two falsifiable halves. If geometry predicts where plaque forms but not who develops it, the segment model will show a within-person effect with a null between-person effect. That result would be consistent with the shear-stress mechanism literature and would still answer the individual-risk question in the negative. The profile network earns its place through pretraining and cross-fitting, and its strongest possible result is a learned covariate that beats MiniRocket inside the same regression. For that reason the network is best treated as a test of how much information the profile carries, not as a separate prediction model.
