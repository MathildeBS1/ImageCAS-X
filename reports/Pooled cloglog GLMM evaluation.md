# Keep the GLMM, shrink its claims

The pooled segment-level cloglog GLMM is the right primary model for the thesis, and no non-machine-learning alternative found in this research is clearly better. What has to change is the reason given for it. The published evidence does not show that a random-intercept model predicts better for a new person than a model that ignores clustering; it shows a tie when the random intercept is integrated out and worse calibration when it is set to zero. The model earns its place for two other reasons: it gives valid inference for the prespecified 5-df likelihood-ratio (LR) test of the geometry block, and it is the only regression formulation that supplies a joint distribution from which nine segment risks can be combined into one person risk. That second role is also its weakest point, because the person risk depends on the random-intercept variance σ² and on the assumed normal shape, and nine binary segments per person identify neither well. Three parts of the design (cloglog link, log-length offset, pooled person risk) have no precedent in the coronary papers that could be read, so they must be justified from the grouped-hazard and frailty arguments, not from convention. The practical answer is to keep the model, make three changes (a free coefficient on log length, integrated prediction stated explicitly, adaptive quadrature only), and prespecify a short set of sensitivity analyses led by a person-level cloglog benchmark. Most of the evidence comes from logit models with 20 to 50 large clusters; no simulation covers about a thousand clusters of nine, so several conclusions below are reasoned and are labelled as such.

Three earlier reports cover how the model works, its machine-learning challengers and the regression-versus-XGBoost choice. This report does not repeat them and contradicts them in one place only, noted in the last section. Evidence grades are stated in the text: "opened" means the full text was fetched (numbers were relayed by a summarising fetch tool and need checking against the PDF before they are quoted), "abstract only" and "snippet only" mean what they say, and "inference" means reasoning by the researchers or by this report with no source.

## The random intercept buys valid inference and a joint model, not better prediction

Three simulation papers, all opened, agree on what a random intercept does for prediction in a new cluster. Bouwmeester et al. found the same external C-statistic for a standard logistic model and a random-intercept model (0.77 for both at an ICC of 15%), with a **calibration slope of 0.83 against 0.97** when the random effect was set to zero; the model discriminated better "only if the cluster effect is used for predictions", which is impossible for a held-out person ([Bouwmeester et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3658967/)). Pavlou et al. showed that the miscalibration is an artefact of the b = 0 shortcut: integrated predictions had slopes of **0.97 to 0.98 across ICC 0.05 to 0.30**, the same as GEE and as a model ignoring clustering, while b = 0 fell to 0.788 at ICC 0.30 ([Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/)). Falconieri et al. recommend random-intercept models over GEE and standard logistic regression, but for centre-specific predictions in centres of 50 to 200 patients ([Falconieri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7383814)). None of the three is a cloglog model, none has clusters near nine, and none evaluates a joint "any event in the cluster" probability.

The consequence for the thesis is direct. Any sentence claiming that the GLMM "predicts better" should go. The defensible claims are that the random intercept gives honest standard errors for the geometry coefficients, and that it supplies the dependence model needed to aggregate: a marginal model gives only segment risks, and the independence product 1 − ∏(1 − p) overstates the person risk under positive within-person correlation (derivation in the [background report](Pooled%20segment%20GLMM%20background.md)). The figure in the thesis notes, "slope 0.79 vs 0.98", is Pavlou's logit result at ICC 0.30 for individual predictions. It illustrates the direction of the b = 0 error and should not be presented as its expected size for a cloglog any-of-nine risk; that size must be computed in the cohort (inference).

Practice supports the random intercept and nothing else. A EuroIntervention methods paper, opened, found that **only 5 of 15** intracoronary imaging studies with several observations per patient clearly accounted for clustering, and recommends "to always use multilevel models when analysing multiple observations per patient" ([Lingsma et al. 2013](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/tools-amp-techniques-analysis-of-clustered-data-in-interventional-cardiology-current-practice-and-methodological-advice)). The shear-stress IVUS studies that were opened use linear mixed models with a patient or vessel intercept for continuous plaque change, for example 4,029 frames in 40 patients ([Costopoulos et al. 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6503452/fullTextXML)) and 10,205 sectors in 40 patients, with a binary plaque-growth outcome fitted by a GLMM whose link is not stated ([Hartman et al. 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8586146/fullTextXML)). Lesion-level event studies use marginal Cox models with a robust variance, with shared frailty planned as a robustness analysis in PROSPECT II; this rests on search excerpts of the protocol and analysis plan, not on the published paper ([PROSPECT II protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/65/NCT02171065/Prot_000.pdf)). CCTA cohort papers either use GEE and marginal Cox (PARADIGM, abstract only, [Bax et al. 2022](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3631731/view)) or sum plaque per patient and fit an ordinary Cox model ([Williams et al. 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7195857/fullTextXML)). **No paper whose methods were read uses a cloglog link, a length offset, or a pooling of sub-unit risks into a patient risk.** That is absence in about eight full texts plus abstracts, with no targeted search for either term, so it is weak evidence of a gap. The thesis is therefore at or above common practice on clustering and unconventional on link, offset and aggregation.

Guidance gives less cover than expected. TRIPOD-Cluster describes repeated measurements as "clustered within individuals" and states that their analysis "is beyond the scope of TRIPOD-Cluster" (opened, first two thirds; quotes relayed by the fetch tool) ([Debray et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9903176/)). It applies by analogy only. Three of its principles transfer: state how clustering was handled, state which intercept the predictions use (it lists a pooled intercept and integration over the random-intercept distribution as options that "typically lead to different calibration performance"), and keep every model-building step inside the resampling. It says nothing, in the part read, about LR tests of predictor blocks. TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST could not be opened in this round, so nothing here is attributed to them.

## The person risk rests on a variance that nine segments barely identify

The weaknesses differ a great deal in severity, and the ordering matters more than the list. The table gives the ranking; the paragraphs after it explain the top three.

| Weakness | Severity here | Evidence grade | What addresses it |
|---|---|---|---|
| σ² weakly identified; person risk depends on σ̂² and the normal shape | High for person-level calibration | Simulations opened, but for other regimes; the link to the person risk is inference | Fold-wise σ̂, cold starts, gamma-frailty refit, person-level calibration, person-level benchmark |
| Segments not independent given the person (same vessel, adjacent rows) | Moderate to high for the left-main features on the pLAD and pLCx rows | Inference; no source quantifies it | Adjacent-pair co-occurrence check, person-cluster bootstrap, GEE Wald test |
| Fixed offset on log length | Moderate; can leak into curvature and taper | Inference; no coronary precedent | Free coefficient (1 df), offset as sensitivity |
| Riley criteria do not cover clustering or the pooled model | Moderate | Opened (Riley 2019); abstract only (Wynants 2015) | Count persons; estimate shrinkage empirically |
| LR test is asymptotic | Low to moderate with about 1,000 persons and 5 df | GLMM FAQ opened; primaries not opened | Parametric bootstrap of the LR statistic |
| Cloglog versus logit | Low for coefficients and the test; matters for the person risk | Snippets and derivation | Logit refit as sensitivity |
| Laplace approximation | High if used, nil if avoided | Earlier report | Adaptive quadrature, doubled-node refit |

**The variance is the main exposure.** Information about σ² comes almost only from persons with two or more positive segments (inference). The opened simulations show what small binary clusters do to variance estimates: with clusters of five, Moineddin et al. found relative bias in the random-intercept variance of **+174% at ICC 0.04**, convergence of about 80%, and under-coverage of its interval, and they recommend more than one expected event per cluster for rare outcomes ([Moineddin et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1955447/)); Austin found that "estimation of variance components tended to be poor when there were only five subjects per cluster" whatever the software, while fixed effects were nearly unbiased from 20 subjects per cluster ([Austin 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2949382)). Both designs have 5 to 100 clusters at outcome rates of 25 to 30%. The thesis has the opposite shape, about a thousand clusters of nine, and **no simulation was found for that regime**, so the size of the bias here is unknown. The robustness literature does not close the gap. McCulloch and Neuhaus report that maximum likelihood is largely robust to the random-effects shape, but only the abstract was read and it does not say for which quantities ([McCulloch and Neuhaus 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.1980)); the usual reading, that slopes are robust while intercepts and variance components are not, rests on snippets of Litière et al. and others. The person risk is a Laplace transform of the whole frailty distribution, so it is exactly the kind of quantity that robustness results about slopes do not protect (inference). Segment-level fit and the C-statistic cannot see this error; only person-level calibration can.

One caveat on the research notes themselves. They reason throughout as if events were rare ("a few percent" per segment, "most clusters all-zero"). The earlier report gives about 452 of 1,076 plaque-free participants with new trunk plaque, a person-level risk near 42%, and says the segment-level event count was not yet available ([Models to beat pooled cloglog GLMM](Models%20to%20beat%20pooled%20cloglog%20GLMM.md)). If those figures still hold, the person risk sits where cloglog and logit differ and where the b = 0 error is not small, which strengthens the point that the aggregation needs its own check. The segment-level rate and the number of persons with two or more positive segments should be tabulated before the model is locked, because they decide how well σ² is identified.

**Dependence beyond one shared intercept threatens the primary test in one specific place.** Three of the five geometry features (bifurcation angle, daughter asymmetry, Finet's ratio) are person-level constants that the current script carries on the pLAD and pLCx rows only. Those rows are neighbours at the bifurcation. If their outcomes are more correlated than an exchangeable intercept allows, the two rows count as more independent evidence than they are and the LR statistic is inflated (inference; no source measures adjacent-segment correlation of incident plaque). A parametric bootstrap does not protect against this, because it simulates from the fitted model; a person-level cluster bootstrap or a robust Wald test does. Informative cluster size is a related risk with an opened source: when cluster size is informative, marginal predictions from GLMM and GEE were more miscalibrated than those from a model ignoring clustering ([Pavlou, Ambler and Omar 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8254921/)). Whether the number of evaluable segments depends on disease or image quality in this cohort is an open empirical question.

**The offset is an untested assumption that touches the tested block.** A coefficient fixed at 1 on log segment length says hazard is proportional to length. No coronary study using length as an exposure was found in this round or the previous one; the intravascular studies avoid the problem by cutting equal-sized units ([Hartman et al. 2023](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10153640/fullTextXML)). With segment-position intercepts in the model, most of a wrong offset is absorbed by position, but curvature and taper are computed per segment and depend on length, so the residual length effect lands on the block under test (inference). A free coefficient costs one parameter and removes the assumption.

On sample size, the Riley criteria assume independent observations and are silent on clustering ([Riley et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519266/)). Counting persons and person-level events is the only input the criteria license; counting segment rows would overstate the budget, most of all for the three person-level geometry features and the SCORE2 factors (inference). The one clustered-data simulation, read as an abstract only, recommends at least 10 events per variable and reports that the amount of clustering "was not meaningfully associated with the models' predictive performance" ([Wynants et al. 2015](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/91572)); its cluster sizes are unknown. The target shrinkage of 0.9 describes a person-level model with p parameters. The pooled model has parameters outside that count (eight position intercepts, log length, σ²), so its person-level calibration slope has to be estimated by bootstrap or repeated cross-validation of the whole procedure, not asserted from the formula. No published extension of the criteria to clustered data was found; that absence is unverified.

## Ranked alternatives: a person-level benchmark first, penalisation last

No alternative beats the planned model on all four criteria used (validity of the block test, prediction for a new person, parameter cost, implementation). The useful ones are cheap sensitivity analyses. The ranking is a synthesis by the researchers; the evidence behind each row is described after the table.

| Rank | Formulation | Test of the geometry block | Person-level prediction | Verdict against the planned GLMM |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Planned cloglog random-intercept GLMM, adaptive quadrature, integrated person risk | LR, valid on the same rows and settings | Calibrated if b is integrated and σ̂² is sound | Reference |
| 1 | Person-level cloglog or logistic model on per-person geometry summaries | Valid and simplest, but tests a summarised hypothesis | Similar for "who"; no "where" | Equal for "who"; add as benchmark |
| 2 | Working-independence cloglog GEE, robust 5-df Wald test | Valid with this many persons; no likelihood | Segment marginals only; person risk needs a dependence model | Worse as primary; best check on the LR test |
| 3 | Interval-censored or discrete-time shared-frailty survival model | Same as planned | Same as planned | The same model; use as justification |
| 4 | Bayesian cloglog GLMM, weakly informative priors | No LR test | No evidence of gain | Equal at best; optional |
| 5 | Vessel-within-person intercept | Valid, but Laplace in R | No evidence of gain | Optional; likely a boundary estimate |
| 6 | Conditional logistic, or between-within terms in the GLMM (+2 df) | Valid for curvature and taper only | No absolute risk | Worse for prediction; useful "where" check |
| 7 | Ridge, lasso or Firth-type GLMM | Test lost or non-standard | Unreliable at small effective n | Worse |
| 8 | Beta-binomial count model | Cannot hold segment covariates | Answers "how many", not "any" | Worse |
| 9 | Random slopes; spatial or tree-correlated effects | Not estimable with nine rows | Not applicable | Not feasible |

The **person-level model** ranks first because it needs no σ², no position factor and no aggregation assumption, and the Riley criteria apply to it without argument. Its cost is that curvature and taper must be collapsed to a per-person summary chosen in advance, which changes the hypothesis from "segment geometry predicts plaque in that segment" to "summarised geometry predicts who". For the three left-main features nothing is lost, since they are person-level already. No simulation or applied study was found that compares a segment mixed model pooled to a person risk against a person-level model on aggregated features; the thesis would be contributing that comparison. It also guards the interpretation the background report warned about: the segment LR test can reject on which segment gets plaque while the thesis asks who gets it.

**GEE** is the natural question from a cardiology examiner, since the CCTA cohort papers use it. Its sandwich variance is known to be biased with fewer than about 30 to 40 clusters (snippets only, [arXiv 2205.05624](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.05624)), which is not a concern with about a thousand persons. It fails as primary for a structural reason, not a statistical one: it treats within-person correlation as a nuisance, so the any-segment risk is not identified from its output. As a sensitivity analysis it is the best single check, because agreement between the robust Wald test and the LR test shows that the conclusion does not depend on the normal intercept or on exchangeable dependence. No simulation was found that compares the type I error of the two tests for many small clusters and a multi-df block.

The **survival formulation** is an argument, not an alternative. With two scans each segment contributes one interval, so an interval-censored proportional-hazards frailty model collapses to the planned model. A log follow-up offset corresponds to a constant baseline hazard and a free coefficient to a Weibull baseline. This is a standard result reasoned by the researchers and not verified in a primary source this round; Prentice and Gloeckler 1978 could not be read. It lets the thesis present the model as the discrete-time counterpart of the shared-frailty Cox model that the lesion literature already accepts, and it settles what the open follow-up choice means.

**Penalisation** is ranked low on opened evidence. Riley et al. conclude that penalisation methods "are more unreliable when needed most" and "are best applied with large effective sample sizes" ([Riley et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8026952/)), and Van Calster et al. report (abstract) that shrinkage "often worked poorly in individual datasets" ([Van Calster et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11493)). Both concern unclustered models; nothing was found on penalised or Bayesian GLMMs for clustered binary prediction. A penalised block also has no chi-square reference distribution, so it would weaken the confirmatory test. **Conditional logistic regression** has no cloglog form, drops persons without events and cancels every person-level covariate, so the 5-df test would become a 2-df test of curvature and taper (Allison's blog post, opened, for the general properties: [Statistical Horizons](https://statisticalhorizons.com/?p=7229)). The between-within version inside the GLMM keeps most of the benefit for two extra parameters, with the caveat that its bias has been reported as largest in small clusters (as relayed by the same blog post; primaries not opened). The claims that a vessel-level variance will be weakly identified and that software for it falls back on Laplace in R are reasoned and from memory, not sourced.

## What to keep, what to change, and what to prespecify

**Keep** the segment-level cloglog GLMM with a random person intercept as the primary model; maximum likelihood by adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature with the same node count in both models and the doubled-node refit; one prespecified LR test of the five linear geometry terms on identical rows; segment position as a fixed factor; cross-validation folded by person with standardisation, coefficients and σ̂² estimated inside each training fold; and person-level calibration as the diagnostic that carries weight.

**Change** four things. First, enter log segment length with a free coefficient in the base model, report its interval against 1, and move the fixed offset to a sensitivity analysis. This agrees with the background report's "safer default" and costs one parameter of the budget. Second, state in the Methodology that the person risk integrates over the random intercept, that b = 0 and the independence product are not used, and quote the size of the b = 0 error computed in the cohort instead of Pavlou's 0.79. Third, remove any claim of better prediction from the random intercept and replace it with the two real justifications: valid inference and a joint model for aggregation. Fourth, decide which rows carry the left-main features before the test is locked, since the pLAD-and-pLCx coding is where both the adjacency problem and the "who versus where" problem concentrate; a person-level test of those three features is clean and should sit beside the block test whichever coding is chosen. The follow-up term stays an open choice, but the thesis should say what each option assumes (constant versus Weibull baseline) and that proportional hazards cannot be tested with one follow-up scan.

**Prespecify** the following sensitivity analyses, in order of value for effort.

| Order | Analysis | What it checks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Person-level cloglog model, same five features with one prespecified summary each | Whether the segment model earns its complexity; whether a rejection is about "who" |
| 2 | Working-independence cloglog GEE with a robust 5-df Wald test, and a person-cluster bootstrap of the block's standard errors | Dependence of the test on the normal intercept and on exchangeable correlation |
| 3 | Parametric bootstrap of the LR statistic | Small-sample error of the chi-square reference, under the fitted model |
| 4 | Gamma-frailty refit with its closed-form person risk | Sensitivity of person-level calibration to the frailty shape |
| 5 | Fold-wise table of σ̂ with fits from several starting values; boundary estimates reported | Stability of the variance that drives the person risk |
| 6 | Logit-link refit; fixed-offset version of length | Link and exposure assumptions |
| 7 | Person means of curvature and taper added (+2 df) | Confounding by person in the segment-varying features |
| 8 | Number of evaluable segments as a covariate; calibration by segment count | Informative cluster size |
| 9 | Observed versus model-implied co-occurrence of plaque in adjacent and non-adjacent segment pairs | Independence given the person |
| 10 | Vessel-within-person intercept | Nested dependence; report a boundary estimate if it occurs |

One further piece of work would turn most of the inferences in this report into evidence: **a short simulation at the fitted base-model parameters** (the cohort's number of persons, nine segments, the estimated σ and segment rates) recording how often σ̂ hits zero, the type I error of the LR and robust Wald tests for a 5-df block, and the person-level calibration of the integrated risk under normal and non-normal frailty. No published simulation covers this regime.

**An examiner will want seven things justified**: why a GLMM and not GEE, given that GEE and marginal Cox are what the coronary cohort papers use; why cloglog and not logit, with the honest answer that fit cannot distinguish them at segment level and the reason is interpretive; why length enters as exposure and whether its coefficient is fixed; whether hazard ratios are conditional or population-averaged and which prediction is evaluated; the aggregation step and its independence-given-the-intercept assumption; that resampling is by person and includes every estimated quantity; and the quadrature settings. To these the evidence adds an eighth: how a sample-size criterion derived for independent persons was applied to a model fitted on segment rows.

The researchers' brief said 10 to 18 segments per person; they worked with nine, as the thesis does, and report that the conclusions do not depend on the difference.

## Conclusion

The search for a better regression model ends with the same model and a different argument for it. The GLMM is not the best predictor on offer, because for a new person nothing in the literature beats a correctly integrated model and nothing loses to it either. It is the only formulation that tests segment geometry with honest standard errors and also produces a coherent person risk, and the survival reading shows it is the two-scan form of a shared-frailty model that cardiology already uses. The thesis is stronger if it says this plainly and treats the person risk as a derived quantity whose validity is an empirical question, answered by person-level calibration and by agreement with a person-level benchmark that needs none of the model's assumptions.

The more useful shift is in where the uncertainty sits. It is not in the link or the choice between GEE and a mixed model, which is where an examiner will start, but in σ² and in the dependence between neighbouring segments, neither of which the published simulations reach for this data shape. A thesis that reports fold-wise σ̂, the co-occurrence check and a small cohort-calibrated simulation would be supplying evidence the methods literature does not yet have, and that is a contribution in its own right whatever the geometry test shows.
