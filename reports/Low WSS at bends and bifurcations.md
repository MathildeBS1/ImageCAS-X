# Rebuild the WSS-sites paragraph on primary sources

The paragraph has the sites right and the citations wrong. chatzizisis2007ess licenses *where* low and oscillatory WSS occurs (inner wall of curvatures, lateral walls of bifurcations), but it describes no mechanism at either site. It never mentions a flow divider, a fast core or a skewed velocity profile, and it uses "recirculation" only in a generic definition of disturbed flow ([Chatzizisis 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2007.02.059)). The open CHECK should therefore be closed as "sites yes, mechanism no". The bend mechanism belongs to Ku 1997 (with Dean 1927 as origin) and a coronary curved-tube model (Qiu & Tarbell 2000). The bifurcation mechanism belongs to Asakura & Karino 1990 and the Antoniadis 2015 review, with Zarins 1983 for the velocity skew toward the divider. Three wordings overstate the sources: "can recirculate" for coronary bends, where models show slow, oscillating inner-wall flow rather than separation; "separates and recirculates" stated unconditionally for coronary bifurcations; and "accordingly", which turns co-location of plaque and low WSS into causation. A drop-in paragraph and seven new BibTeX entries close the gaps. One bibliographic error in the notes also needs fixing before use: Antoniadis 2015 is on pages V18 to V22, not V102 to V109 ([Crossref](https://doi.org/10.4244/EIJV11SVA4)).

## Chatzizisis covers both sites in two sentences and neither mechanism

The full text was read. Its site claims are explicit: "Low ESS typically occurs at the inner areas of curvatures as well as upstream of stenoses", and "Oscillatory ESS occurs primarily downstream of stenoses, at the lateral walls of bifurcations, and in the vicinity of branch points" (p. 2382). The introduction adds that lesions form "in the vicinity of branch points, the outer wall of bifurcations, and the inner wall of curvatures, where disturbed flow occurs" (p. 2379) ([Chatzizisis 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2007.02.059)). That licenses the opening sentence of the paragraph. Note the split: the review assigns **low** ESS to bends and **oscillatory** ESS to bifurcations, so "low and oscillatory" is the faithful umbrella term for the opening sentence, not "low" alone.

It licenses nothing after that. A text search found no "flow divider", "carina", "apex", "skew" or "helical"; velocity is discussed only for straight undisturbed flow; and none of its three figures shows a curved or branching vessel. "Recirculation" appears only in the generic definition of disturbed laminar flow ("flow separation, recirculation, and reattachment to forward flow"), never placed at a bend or bifurcation wall ([Chatzizisis 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2007.02.059)). Its own sources for the site claims are Ku 1997 (ref 34) and Ku 1985 (ref 10), which is a further reason to cite Ku directly for the mechanism.

The existing refs.bib note on chatzizisis2007ess says "full text not read"; it can now be upgraded with the p. 2379 and p. 2382 quotes above.

## Bends produce slow, oscillating inner-wall flow, not a stable recirculation zone

Ku's review states the mechanism in plain words: fully developed flow through a curved tube "exhibits velocity that skews toward the outer wall of the bend", secondary flow "develops as counter-rotating vortices", and "With higher Dean numbers, flow can separate along the inner curve" (p. 407) ([Ku 1997](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.fluid.29.1.399)). The vortex pair goes back to **Dean 1927**, which has a verified record but was not read; cite it only beside Ku ([Dean 1927](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786440708564324)). Ku defines the Dean number through the curvature ratio (tube radius over centerline radius of curvature) and the Reynolds number, so "tighter bends and faster flow" is a fair plain-language reading of "higher Dean numbers". That is an inference from the definition, not a quoted claim, and no source gives a separation threshold for coronary geometry.

At coronary scale the picture is low and oscillating inner-wall shear, not a bubble of reversed flow. A compliant curved-tube model with characteristic coronary curvature and flow found **mean WSS about 50 percent lower on the inside wall** with stronger WSS oscillation there ([Qiu & Tarbell 2000](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.429629)). Pulsatile curved-tube simulations of coronary conditions found reversal of WSS direction at the inside wall under resting flow but reported no separation zone ([Chang & Tarbell 1988](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(88)90131-5)). In an anatomically realistic human RCA, the small oscillatory shear index "was attributed to flow reversal in the waveform, rather than separation" ([Myers et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.1349703)). The only coronary report of recirculation at a bend is Asakura's 1988 PhD thesis (steady flow, fixed post-mortem vessels), which describes "a very thin-layered region of slow reverse flows (recirculation zone)" at the inner wall of an RCA bend ([Asakura 1988 thesis](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/21125/files/1.pdf)). The Circ Res abstract pools bends and bifurcations, so the article itself was not verified to place recirculation at bends ([Asakura & Karino 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2317887)). Hence the replacement: inner-wall flow is slow, WSS is lower and oscillates more, and separation is a conditional statement for tight bends at high flow.

Two limits belong in the literature review or discussion, not this paragraph. The curved-tube model fits the LAD well and the tortuous RCA and LCX poorly ([Wahle et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2006.03.002)), and one patient-specific RCA study found the opposite WSS pattern at bends, with WSS driven mainly by lumen radius ([Johnston & Johnston 2007](https://doi.org/10.1088/0031-9155/52/9/013)). They do justify keeping "may" in the closing sentence.

## Bifurcations are better supported, but separation is conditional

The bifurcation half has the stronger evidence. In flow visualisation of human coronary trees, lesion sites on the wall opposite the divider were "where flow was either slow or disturbed with the formation of slow recirculation and secondary flows and where wall shear stress was low", and the flow divider was free of lesions ([Asakura & Karino 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2317887)). The thesis behind the article describes "wide regions of separated flow" at the hips of the left main bifurcation, filled with spiral and recirculating flow ([Asakura 1988 thesis](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/21125/files/1.pdf)). The Antoniadis review (full text read) gives the coronary mechanism in one place: bifurcation flow patterns result "in flow separation, recirculation and secondary flow patterns", producing "local low and oscillatory ESS" along the lateral walls, while "high ESS develops in the flow divider (carina)" ([Antoniadis et al. 2015](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/impact-of-local-flow-haemodynamics-on-atherosclerosis-in-coronary-artery-bifurcations)).

The current clause "the fastest flow enters each daughter branch beside the ridge" has no verbatim support in any source read. The closest statement is carotid: streamlines along the divider wall have "velocity maxima shifted toward the flow divider apex", while the opposite wall shows separation and reversal ([Zarins et al. 1983](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.res.53.4.502)). This is a general consequence of inertia, so Zarins is acceptable for the mechanism, never for coronary plaque location. Separation should stay conditional: the classic separation evidence comes from the dilated carotid bulb, which coronaries lack, and Antoniadis names the side-branch flow fraction as "the primary factor determining the ESS patterns", so the extent of separation varies between bifurcations ([Antoniadis et al. 2015](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/impact-of-local-flow-haemodynamics-on-atherosclerosis-in-coronary-artery-bifurcations)).

On terminology, "lateral wall" is the term used in pathology and interventional work ([Nakazawa et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2010.01.021)), while older flow papers say "outer wall", and van der Giessen uses "outer wall" for the bifurcation and "outer bend" for curvature in the same scheme ([van der Giessen et al. 2009](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/plaque-and-shear-stress-distribution-in-human-coronary-bifurcations-a-multislice-computed-tomography-study/pdf)). Defining the lateral wall once, as opposite the flow divider, avoids the clash with the outer wall of a bend, which has high WSS.

## The plaque sentence shows co-location, and needs a bend source

"Accordingly" claims more than the sources show. Asakura & Karino report plaque at the low-WSS sites; van der Giessen did **not compute WSS** but ranked quadrants by WSS "assumed" from geometry, finding plaque in the lateral wall in 72% of cross-sections versus 31% at the flow divider, and never at the divider alone ([van der Giessen et al. 2009](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/plaque-and-shear-stress-distribution-in-human-coronary-bifurcations-a-multislice-computed-tomography-study/pdf)). Both are co-location. "Plaque is concentrated at the same sites" says exactly that.

The sentence also rests on one source for bends: van der Giessen samples only just past bifurcations, and its "inner bend" quadrant is the myocardial side, not a study of curvature. Iwami 1998 supplies an in-vivo bend source: atherosclerosis "preferentially formed at the inner arc of the curved coronary vessels", more so at greater curvature ([Iwami et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0002-9149(98)00340-3)). For the bifurcation half, adding IVUS and pathology stops it resting on one modality: maximal plaque thickness spared the flow divider in every case of the proximal LAD and sat opposite the circumflex takeoff ([Kimura et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/0735-1097(95)00551-x)), and in pathology "the lateral wall showed significantly greater intima as well as necrotic core thickness than the flow divider" ([Nakazawa et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2010.01.021)). "Largely spared" is the right strength for the divider: van der Giessen still found divider plaque in 31% of cross-sections, almost always alongside lateral-wall plaque.

## Drop-in replacement paragraph

```latex
Low and oscillatory WSS occur at two types of site, both determined by the shape of the vessel: the inner wall of bends and the lateral walls of bifurcations (Figure~\ref{fig:wss-sites}) \cite{chatzizisis2007ess}.
In a bend, the fast core of the flow is carried towards the outer wall, and a pair of counter-rotating secondary vortices forms across the lumen \cite{dean1927curved,ku1997blood}.
Flow along the inner wall is therefore slow, and the WSS there is lower on average and oscillates more over the cardiac cycle than at the outer wall \cite{qiu2000compliant}.
In tight bends and at high flow, the flow can also separate from the inner wall \cite{ku1997blood}.
At a bifurcation, the fast core of the parent flow is directed onto the ridge at which the vessel splits, referred to as the flow divider, where the WSS is high \cite{zarins1983carotid,antoniadis2015bifurcation}.
Flow along the lateral walls, opposite the flow divider, is slow and can separate from the wall to form recirculating and secondary flows, so the WSS there is low and oscillatory \cite{asakura1990flow,antoniadis2015bifurcation}.
Plaque is concentrated at the same sites, on the inner wall of bends and on the lateral walls of bifurcations, while the flow divider is largely spared \cite{asakura1990flow,iwami1998curvature,kimura1996atheroma,vandergiessen2009bifurcation,nakazawa2010pathological}.
Consequently, a person whose tree contains more of these sites, or sharper ones, may carry risk that the classical risk factors do not capture.
```

The opening changes "Low WSS occurs" to "Low and oscillatory WSS occur" so that it matches Chatzizisis' split and the preceding paragraph's "low and oscillating kind". It also names the two sites up front, which lets the figure reference sit where the sites are named. If the opening must stay verbatim, drop the colon clause and keep the rest unchanged. The figure caption already says "lateral walls", so it needs no edit.

## Conclusion

The fix is a citation swap more than a rewrite. A clinical review cited for fluid mechanics it never states becomes a review for the sites plus engineering and flow-visualisation sources for the mechanism, which is the standard the thesis-writing rules set. The substantive correction concerns bends. The coronary curved-tube literature describes a slow, oscillating inner wall with separation only at high Dean numbers, so the thesis should not picture a recirculation zone at every bend. This also matters downstream: if inner-wall WSS falls with curvature and flow rate rather than switching on at a separation threshold, a continuous curvature feature is better motivated than a count of "sharp" bends.

Two items remain open. The bend recirculation and the deflection at the divider are verified only in Asakura's 1988 thesis, not in the Circ Res article, so the replacement paragraph deliberately does not rely on them. Nakazawa, Iwami, Kimura and Qiu were read at abstract level, which suffices for the site and direction claims they carry here but not for any number.
