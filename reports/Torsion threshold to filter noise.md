# A magnitude floor cannot rescue torsion

A "built-in minimum" magnitude threshold on torsion should not be attempted for this thesis's locked Model 1 or Model 2: the verdict from this research is a clear no. Torsion's dominant error component is systematic and pipeline-dependent, not the kind of zero-mean random noise a magnitude floor is built to suppress, and the differential-geometry literature's own consensus solution is to regularize the estimator itself (smoothing splines, total-variation-regularized differentiation) rather than to threshold its output after the fact ([Chartrand](https://jasoncantarella.com/downloads/chartrand-2007-numerical.pdf); [arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02398)). No precedent exists anywhere in the vascular tortuosity or curvature literature of thresholding turning a null or noisy continuous shape descriptor into a real predictor, and the one directly relevant counter-example shows dichotomization can just as easily destroy a real signal ([Journal of Nuclear Medicine 62(5):605](https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/62/5/605)). Even a threshold constructed with zero outcome leakage would only address the noise problem; it would do nothing about the separate, unresolved fact that no study anywhere links scalar or per-point torsion to a clinical or hemodynamic outcome, a gap this project's earlier locked decision already identified and that a denoising step cannot close. If the threshold were instead tuned against this cohort's own outcome data, even inside nested cross-validation, the closest quantitative precedent shows outcome-optimized cutpoint selection inflates the nominal false-positive rate from 5% to roughly 40% ([Altman et al. 1994](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/86/11/829/955201)), a risk this project's already-tight 8-parameter, near-EPV-20 budget cannot absorb. The one methodologically defensible path, if the user wants to attempt it anyway, is synthetic-curve calibration with zero outcome-data involvement, reported strictly as an exploratory sensitivity analysis outside the locked primary model.

## The estimator, not the sample, is what is broken

The core reason a magnitude floor cannot work is that it targets the wrong failure mode. A minimum-magnitude deadband is a denoising strategy suited to random, exchangeable measurement noise scattered symmetrically around a true value; that is not what the literature finds in torsion. Every source surveyed on numerical differentiation of noisy curves converges on regularizing the differentiation process itself rather than filtering its output: Chartrand's foundational statement is explicit that "denoising the data before or after differentiating does not generally give satisfactory results... the real secret is to regularize the differentiation itself" ([Chartrand, Numerical Differentiation of Noisy, Nonsmooth Data](https://jasoncantarella.com/downloads/chartrand-2007-numerical.pdf)), and the closest applied paper to this project's own pipeline treats curvature and torsion estimation as a joint penalized-spline fitting problem precisely because post-hoc filtering of an already-differentiated signal performs worse ([arXiv:2203.02398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02398)). No source found treats a fixed-magnitude minimum threshold as an established technique for torsion or curvature specifically; the one superficially similar precedent, wavelet hard-thresholding, derives its cutoff per-scale from an estimated noise standard deviation rather than applying an arbitrary fixed constant, which is itself an argument against a hand-picked or eyeballed torsion cutoff.

The vascular-specific evidence sharpens this into a direct warning against transferability. A comparative study of two established centerline bend-detection algorithms on the internal carotid artery found they are "not comparable" to each other, with detected bend counts driven far more by fixed pipeline parameters, resampling length and smoothing factor, than by per-scan random noise (5 to 15 bends depending on resampling length alone; one algorithm's bend count held stable at 4 across smoothing settings where the other varied from 6-8) ([PMC8626959](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8626959/)). This is the vascular confirmation of what this project's earlier locked decision already flagged in general terms ([Scalar tortuosity metrics for CAD models.md](file:///zhome/e2/6/224426/project/ImageCAS-X/reports/Scalar%20tortuosity%20metrics%20for%20CAD%20models.md)): torsion's error is baked into the extraction pipeline's own choices, not randomly distributed around a stable true value. A magnitude floor calibrated against this project's own pipeline could not be assumed to transfer to a different pipeline, and more importantly, a threshold cannot cleanly separate "signal" from "noise" when a systematic bias can shift the estimator's whole output distribution into the same magnitude range the real signal would occupy. Thresholding indiscriminately removes small values; it does nothing about a biased estimator that produces confidently wrong large ones.

## No precedent, and one direct counter-example, for thresholding as a rescue

The strongest empirical argument against attempting this comes from a systematic search for precedent, which found none. Every coronary "significant tortuosity" definition traced to its origin, including the 737-patient LCx study this project already knows, is a fixed angular bend-count convention inherited by citation from Groves 2009 ("two consecutive 180-degree turns by visual estimation") and Eleid 2014, applied to a different disease (spontaneous coronary dissection) before being reused for atherosclerotic CAD ([PMC6335987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6335987/)). None of these thresholds were derived statistically from a reference population's normal range, and none was constructed as a rescue of a previously null continuous score; they were categorical from their first publication. The one same-study "expert consensus" threshold found, the Tortuosity Severity Index's 4-curvature cutoff, was set by clinician consensus at a single center with no independent validation and no stated derivation rationale ([PMC6303525](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6303525/)). Where the tortuosity literature does compare a continuous metric against a thresholded one head-to-head, the continuous version wins: a coronary study judged a continuous arc-chord tortuosity index more reliable than a categorical angle-based method for the same clinical question ([PMC10795752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10795752/)).

The direct counter-example matters most. A quadratic, non-linear association between a continuous biomarker (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio) and mortality risk "dissipated when [it] was dichotomized at its sample median" ([Journal of Nuclear Medicine 62(5):605](https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/62/5/605)); dichotomizing a real signal made it disappear. This is the general pattern the biostatistics literature describes for continuous-to-binary conversion: Royston, Altman and Sauerbrei's canonical paper finds dichotomization "equivalent to discarding approximately one-third of the data" in information terms and concludes dichotomizing continuous predictors in regression is "a bad idea" ([Statistics in Medicine 25:127-141, 2006](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sim.2331)). Applied to torsion, which already carries an estimator-instability problem on top of the ordinary information loss any dichotomization causes, this stacks two independent reasons for skepticism rather than one. Nothing in the literature suggests torsion is a special case where the general pattern would reverse, and no coronary or vascular geometry study has ever tested "torsion, thresholded, vs. torsion, continuous" against any outcome in either direction, so there is no existing evidence a threshold would help torsion specifically, only the uniform pattern that it does not help comparable metrics elsewhere.

## Denoising and outcome evidence are two separate, unresolved problems

Even setting aside whether a threshold could be built without leakage, a successful noise-floor calibration would only ever solve half of this project's original exclusion rationale. This project's own locked decision excluded torsion for two independent reasons: noise-sensitivity with a systematic pipeline-tied component, and the complete absence of any study linking scalar or per-point torsion to a clinical or hemodynamic outcome, compounded by the 2025 coronary-specific finding that the torsion-to-helical-flow mechanistic link fails Bonferroni-Holm correction ([PMC12324882](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324882/); see [Scalar tortuosity metrics for CAD models.md](file:///zhome/e2/6/224426/project/ImageCAS-X/reports/Scalar%20tortuosity%20metrics%20for%20CAD%20models.md)). A magnitude threshold, however well constructed, is purely a denoising operation; it touches only the first reason. Across every methods paper reviewed, denoising and outcome-validation are structurally separate literatures that never combine for torsion: numerical-differentiation and spline-regularization papers validate their methods against known analytical ground truth or simulated noise, never against a disease outcome, while the coronary tortuosity studies that do test outcome association start from an already-defined, manually-assessed bend-angle metric, not from a denoised or thresholded torsion signal. No study anywhere performs both steps in sequence for torsion. A perfectly denoised torsion signal would therefore still be an unvalidated predictor with zero outcome evidence behind it; the threshold does not manufacture the evidence this project already found missing, it can only, at best, produce a cleaner version of a metric that has never been shown to predict anything.

## The only defensible construction path, if attempted anyway

If the user chooses to attempt this despite the verdict above, exactly one construction path emerges from this research as methodologically sound, and it must satisfy three conditions together: the calibration source must be entirely independent of this cohort's outcome labels, the resulting threshold must be fixed before any model fitting touches outcome data, and the result must be reported as an explicitly labeled exploratory or sensitivity analysis outside the locked primary 8-parameter model, never folded into it.

The calibration itself should use synthetic, analytically-defined space curves with exactly known ground-truth torsion, run through this project's own extraction pipeline end to end (the same segmentation-to-skeletonization-to-smoothing-to-torsion code path used on real coronary centerlines), following the approach demonstrated for curvature and torsion estimation error under the Frenet-Serret framework using discretized analytic curves with simulated noise ([Axioms 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/axioms15050365)). Straight-line and planar test curves give exact ground-truth torsion of zero; discretizing them at this pipeline's actual voxel and point spacing and recording the distribution of nonzero torsion the pipeline reports for these known-zero cases characterizes the noise floor the estimator itself contributes, with zero patient data and zero outcome information entering the calculation at any point. A fixed rule derived purely from that distribution, for example a threshold set at some multiple of the standard deviation of pipeline-reported torsion on the known-zero synthetic curves, stated and justified in the methods before any outcome model is fit, would not trip PROBAST's signalling question on outcome-informed cutpoint selection, because it is never "based on the development dataset" in the sense that question flags ([PROBAST Explanation & Elaboration](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M18-1377)). This is the cheapest and safest calibration option identified, requiring no new scans, no repeat segmentation, and no repeat imaging, in explicit contrast to the coronary-specific reproducibility literature showing only 12-36% of standard radiomic features achieve acceptable reproducibility (ICC ≥ 0.75) under repeat segmentation of the same scans ([PMC12034606](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034606/)), a sobering real-world benchmark for how hard this bar is to clear even for better-behaved features than torsion.

Two structural guardrails follow directly. First, nested cross-validation is the correct machinery only if the threshold instead has to be tuned against outcome data, but it does not substitute for the synthetic-curve approach: nested CV corrects the leakage in the reported performance estimate, but the threshold search itself still consumes real effective degrees of freedom beyond a fixed constant, a cost the EPV/predictor-counting literature does not currently offer a numeric conversion factor for, and this project's Model 1 is already near the point where the safe EPV target doubles from 10 to 20 at 8 parameters ([Tortuosity feature count for CAD prediction.md](file:///zhome/e2/6/224426/project/ImageCAS-X/reports/Tortuosity%20feature%20count%20for%20CAD%20prediction.md); [van Smeden et al., PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)). An outcome-tuned threshold, even a nested-CV-validated one, should therefore be excluded from that 8-parameter budget altogether rather than charged a single slot. Second, the garden-of-forking-paths argument applies regardless of intent: the existence of defensible alternative choices, a different threshold, a different aggregation statistic, a different smoothing bandwidth, inflates the effective false-positive rate even if only one combination is ever reported ([Gelman & Loken](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/unpublished/p_hacking.pdf)), and Altman et al.'s quantified precedent shows outcome-driven cutpoint search alone can turn a nominal P=0.05 into a true error rate around 40% ([JNCI 86(11):829-835](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/86/11/829/955201)). The synthetic-curve path avoids this specific trap only because the threshold-construction rule never sees the outcome at all; any subsequent adjustment of that rule after looking at how it performs against CAD status in this cohort would silently reopen the same leakage the calibration was designed to prevent.

## Conclusion

The evidence assembled here converges from three independent directions on the same answer: the differential-geometry literature says thresholding is the wrong tool for this kind of estimator instability, the tortuosity-precedent literature shows no threshold has ever rescued a null continuous shape descriptor and one directly analogous attempt made a real signal disappear, and the forking-paths literature quantifies why any outcome-informed version of this maneuver would be actively dangerous for a budget already sitting at the EPV=20 boundary. What changes least from this round of research is the underlying verdict from this project's earlier locked decision; what changes most is the precision of why a fix does not exist: it is not merely that torsion is noisy, but that its noise is substantially the wrong kind for a magnitude floor to address, and that even a technically perfect floor would leave the metric exactly as unvalidated against CAD outcomes as it already was. The synthetic-curve calibration path is worth building only if the user wants a defensible answer to "how much of torsion's output is pipeline artifact," reported as a clearly bounded, outcome-blind sensitivity analysis; it should not be presented, and should not be expected, to convert torsion into a usable covariate for Model 1 or a usable channel for Model 2.
