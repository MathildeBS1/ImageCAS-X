# Collapse curvature, keep angle, cut torsion

For the whole-tree CAD-prediction comparison of Model 1 (logistic regression on averaged geometric scalars plus age and sex) against Model 2 (a small network reading a per-vessel curvature vector plus age and sex), the answer to all three questions points the same direction: **shrink the candidate list, do not grow it**. The Horton-Strahler bifurcation ratio should stay out of both models for now; coronary CTA segmentation resolves only 2-4 branch generations per named vessel against the 5+ consecutive orders the ratio needs to stabilize, and no study anywhere has computed it on a CTA-segmented human coronary tree or linked it to CAD, stenosis, or plaque ([Kassab et al., porcine coronary morphometry](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1394880/); [AHA 15-17 segment model](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Segmental-anatomy-of-the-coronary-arteries-after-a-modified-AHA-classification-1-RCA_fig1_7771780)). Bifurcation angle and coronary dominance both earn scalar-covariate slots, angle because it is the single most evidenced geometric feature this project has examined at all, dominance because it is a confound for all three major vessels, not only the RCA and LCx, and now demonstrably including the LAD ([Abdul Wahaab et al. 2018](https://applications.emro.who.int/imemrf/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med_2018_71_4_2944_2949.pdf)). Torsion should be excluded as a scalar covariate as well as a profile channel; the mechanistic case for it got weaker, not stronger, this round, with a 2025 coronary-specific study finding no significant torsion-to-helical-flow correlation after correction, directly undercutting the pathway the project's earlier locking exercise leaned on ([Shen et al. 2025, Royal Society Open Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324882/)). And of the curvature-family metrics, Model 2 should keep exactly one profile channel, mean absolute curvature, not two; mean absolute curvature and RMS curvature are a same-domain, overlapping pair, and the one paper that head-to-head tested them against a coronary hemodynamic outcome already recommends standardizing on the former ([Kashyap et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)). Applying this prioritization concretely, a defensible whole-tree Model 1 lands at eight predictor parameters, exactly the point at which the sample-size literature says the safe events-per-variable target doubles from 10 to 20, which makes the pruning decisions below load-bearing rather than cosmetic.

## Horton-Strahler fails the depth test the literature itself sets, twice

The Horton-Strahler bifurcation ratio is well-defined for any bifurcating tree, but every source that reports it as a stable, meaningful quantity does so across a run of 5 or more consecutive branch orders drawn from a full anatomical tree resolved down to near-capillary caliber. The classical porcine coronary corrosion-cast work (Kassab's diameter-defined Strahler system) reports a bifurcation ratio of roughly 3.30 held constant across orders 2 through 8, in a tree whose largest trunk order is 11 and whose diameter ratio of about 1.51 per order implies vessel caliber falls below typical CT resolution within five or six orders of the trunk ([Kassab et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1394880/); [A Computer Reconstruction of the Entire Coronary Arterial Tree](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-005-5758-z)). Clinical CTA and the AHA naming convention that structures every coronary paper's anatomy divides the whole visible tree into only 15 to 17 named segments across the RCA, LM, LAD, and LCx ([modified AHA classification](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Segmental-anatomy-of-the-coronary-arteries-after-a-modified-AHA-classification-1-RCA_fig1_7771780)), which caps any single named vessel at roughly 2 to 4 resolvable branch generations before the signal disappears into the segmentation's own resolution floor. Computing a segment-count ratio between consecutive orders at that depth produces a ratio of very small integers, 3 segments at one order against 1 at the next, with sampling variance that scales badly patient to patient. No study located in this pass computes a Horton-Strahler ratio, or any branch-order-count ratio, directly on a CTA-segmented, in vivo human coronary tree; every quantitative value for coronary Rb comes from ex vivo porcine casts that resolve far more orders than any current CT pipeline can reach.

Disease evidence compounds the problem: it is not merely thin, it is essentially absent for coronary arteries specifically. Every Rb value found in coronary literature comes from normal-anatomy morphometry, never a disease-comparison study. The one directly relevant disease-discrimination result for Strahler ordering comes from murine pulmonary hypertension, where Strahler order and tree depth (via topological data analysis) distinguished control from hypoxia-induced disease on micro-CT arterial trees ([TDA on murine pulmonary arterial trees](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0025556423000974)), a different organ, species, and imaging modality with far greater resolvable depth than clinical coronary CTA. The closest coronary-relevant analog is retinal and angiographic fractal dimension, a related but conceptually distinct metric computed by box-counting over a whole vascular image rather than by counting discrete segments per branch order. That literature is genuinely strong: a 14-year population study found participants in the lowest and highest quartiles of retinal vascular fractal dimension had **50% higher coronary heart disease mortality** than those in the middle quartiles after adjustment ([Eur Heart J](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/32/4/422/440005)), lower retinal main venular fractal dimension carried 7.2 to 8.2 times higher risk of prior CABG in the lowest two quartiles ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002191501731328X)), and box-counted fractal dimension on left coronary ramification angiograms could mathematically distinguish severe occlusive disease from normal trees ([OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20809615)). Citing this fractal-dimension evidence as if it validates the Horton-Strahler ratio would be citing a different metric; the two are related by classical theory (fractal dimension D can be derived from Horton's bifurcation and length ratios, D ~ log(Rb)/log(Rl)) but are not interchangeable, and that distinction should be stated explicitly rather than implied if Rb is ever discussed alongside fractal-dimension evidence.

By construction, Rb is a discrete, whole-subtree topological count with no mathematical dependence on centerline bending or junction angle, which makes it a genuinely different information domain from the curvature-family and angle-family metrics already in the candidate list ([definitional argument, general Strahler ordering](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4785968/)). That independence is reasoned from the metrics' definitions, not measured; no dataset anywhere reports the empirical correlation between Rb and curvature or angle in the same coronary cohort. This structural fact also answers part of the profile-channel question directly: because Rb is defined only over a whole subtree's segment counts, it has no per-point representation at all, so it was never a candidate for a dense channel the way curvature is; the classical literature computes one Rb (or a small set of order-pair ratios) per whole tree studied, and because the coronary circulation is anatomically two-to-three separate trees fed from two ostia rather than one, the standards-consistent construction, if this metric is ever revisited, would be one Rb per major named vessel (RCA-rooted, LAD-rooted, LCx-rooted), never a single pooled whole-patient number ([anatomical two-ostia structure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Segmental-anatomy-of-the-coronary-arteries-after-a-modified-AHA-classification-1-RCA_fig1_7771780)). Given the total absence of coronary-CTA precedent, zero coronary disease-association evidence, and a branch-depth mismatch that is a structural property of clinical imaging rather than a fixable engineering choice, the verdict is to leave the Horton-Strahler ratio out of both models' current design. If it is revisited later as an exploratory ablation, it must be computed per-vessel, reported with the caveat that its integer-ratio instability is likely worse per-vessel than pooled, and framed as untested rather than as a validated feature.

## Torsion's mechanistic case weakened further, not just its noise case

Two prior rounds of this project's research already excluded torsion from Model 2's per-point profile on noise-sensitivity grounds and flagged its wall-shear-stress link as "contested" in the WSS locking exercise, citing a torsion-helicity correlation of r=0.64 alongside a helicity-to-WSS anti-correlation of r=-0.91. This round's evidence moves the needle further against torsion on every front it touches, including as a plain scalar covariate. Torsion's error has two distinct components: a random component that spatial averaging would help, and a systematic component tied to the smoothing and resolution choices of the extraction pipeline itself, which averaging cannot fix because it is a property of the estimator, not of any one noisy sample ([Curvature and Torsion estimation of 3D functional data](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02398)). A directly analogous finding in vascular centerline geometry shows how severe this can be: the number of curvature-extrema bends detected in a carotid bifurcation ranged from 3 to 33 depending purely on centerline resolution, with the raw coordinate noise held constant ([Automated landmarking of bends in vascular structures](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-021-00957-6)). No study was found that computes coronary torsion at multiple explicit resolutions to test directly whether its scalar mean is stable across them, so this remains an inference from an adjacent quantity, but it is not a reassuring one: if coronary torsion behaves similarly, two different smoothing pipelines could each produce a highly reproducible but different mean torsion for the same vessel, meaning a scalar torsion covariate risks encoding pipeline choice rather than patient anatomy.

The profile-versus-mean question that this project's own curvature work answered decisively in favor of richer representation (Tello Ayala et al.'s per-point tortuosity profile beating a scalar mean, AUROC 0.67 versus 0.60, [PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)) has simply never been asked for torsion in any study, in any vascular bed. Every paper located that uses torsion at all collapses it to a scalar or segment-level aggregate before analysis: Kashyap et al.'s own 127-patient coronary WSS comparison excluded torsion entirely from its four-metric panel ([PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)); a 67-patient angiographic catalog computed only segment-averaged mean, maximum, and standard-deviation torsion, explicitly declining to correlate any of it with disease presence, severity, or outcome, framing that correlation as future work rather than something the paper itself attempted ([Zhu/Pao et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)); and a 2026 vessel-morphology classifier used only aggregated torsion features, never a per-point profile ([Zhong et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14195)). This pattern reads less like "a torsion profile was tried and lost to a torsion mean" and more like torsion being set aside as a covariate altogether, even in papers purpose-built to test scalar shape descriptors against outcomes. **No study anywhere, in coronary arteries or any other vascular bed, was found using a scalar or average torsion value as a predictor of a clinical or hemodynamic outcome with a reported effect size.** The closest positive precedents use scalar aggregates of related but distinct quantities: scalar average curvature predicting low-WSS area in the same Kashyap cohort, and scalar maximum flow helicity (a CFD output, not a centerline-geometry quantity) predicting coronary plaque progression with OR 1.44 (95% CI 1.07-1.93, p=0.016) in a 22-patient cohort ([Lv et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12858539/)). Neither transfers as direct torsion evidence.

The mechanistic backstop for torsion, that it drives helical flow which in turn shapes WSS, is what actually deteriorated this round. A 2025 study built specifically to test geometric factors against helicity in coronary arteries found **no statistically significant correlations between torsion and helical-flow-based factors after Bonferroni-Holm correction**, though some correlations reached uncorrected p<0.05 in diagonal branches ([Shen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324882/)). This is a coronary-specific, correction-aware, purpose-built test of exactly the link the project's earlier r=0.64 figure asserted, and that older figure itself could not be independently verified in this pass (CAPTCHA-blocked source) and may originate from a carotid rather than coronary cohort. Where the two conflict, the more recent, coronary-specific, correction-aware result should be weighted more heavily. Compounding this, the same 2025 paper shows the second link in the chain, helicity to WSS, is explicitly bidirectional: a helicity-related metric was positively correlated with high time-averaged shear stress area (rho=0.87, p=0.0004) and negatively correlated with low time-averaged shear stress area (rho=-0.89, p=0.0001) in the same stenosed segments, meaning helicity simultaneously suppresses one adverse WSS regime while promoting another. A monotonic "more torsion, more helicity, more protective WSS, lower CAD risk" story is not defensible even in principle once both links are this uncertain. Combining the noise-instability case (already established), the total absence of any torsion-outcome study, and now a directly falsified coronary link in the mechanistic chain, torsion should be excluded from Model 1 as a scalar covariate as well as from Model 2's profile, a stronger and more final verdict than the earlier "contested" framing supported.

## Bifurcation angle and dominance earn covariate slots; the case differs sharply between them

Bifurcation angle remains the single best-evidenced geometric feature this project has examined, on both trustworthiness and disease-association grounds, and nothing in this round changes that. Its stability was demonstrated on this project's own data (78.0-80.8 degrees across smoothing choices), and its CAD evidence is primary-source-verified rather than search-synthesized: an 80-degree angle threshold predicted significant stenosis at 100% sensitivity in a CCTA-CFD cohort, and a dual-source CT study of the LAD-LCx bifurcation found a 78-degree cutoff for significant left coronary stenosis with AUC 0.719 (0.805 restricted to non-calcified plaque) and independence on multivariate analysis, OR 1.423, p=0.002 ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0174352)). A separate 313-patient cohort found the same angle's association weakened to non-significance (p=0.064) after adjusting for sex and obesity ([PMC5431433](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5431433/)), so it is a real but not unconditionally independent predictor, which argues for keeping it as one scalar covariate per proximal landmark rather than treating it as a settled, standalone risk score. Across RCA, LAD, and LCx, the evidence concentrates on one or two proximal landmarks per vessel regardless of how many branch points exist anatomically, so it belongs as a scalar covariate, not a dense per-point channel, on every vessel, a conclusion this project's own earlier tree-wide work already reached and this round's material does not disturb.

Dominance is a genuinely harder case, and it should be included, but for a narrower reason than "it directly raises CAD risk." The confound argument is now stronger than previously understood: a dedicated 100-patient CTA study found dominance significantly changes LAD length and terminal course, not just RCA and LCx anatomy, with 76% of LADs wrapping the apex in left-dominant patients versus 42% in right-dominant patients (p=0.001) ([Abdul Wahaab et al. 2018](https://applications.emro.who.int/imemrf/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med/Egypt_J_Hosp_Med_2018_71_4_2944_2949.pdf)). Dominance is therefore a confound for all three major vessels, and any curvature or length-sensitive feature on any of them should be interpreted with dominance in hand. The direct CAD-risk question is where the evidence gets genuinely contradictory: a CTA cohort found left-dominant circulation independently associated with non-fatal MI and all-cause mortality, HR 3.20 (95% CI 1.67-6.13) ([EHJ](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/33/11/1367/548529)), and a 255,718-patient meta-analysis found left dominance increases ACS mortality ([Khan et al. 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ccd.26281)), while a more recent multivariable CTA study using severe CAD-RADS 4-5 burden as the outcome, the outcome type actually closest to this project's stated goal, found dominance was **not** independently associated with severe CAD after adjusting for age, sex, and standard risk factors ([PMC13363422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363422/)). The pattern that emerges is that dominance predicts adverse events (MI, mortality, mostly in ACS/PCI populations) more robustly than it predicts baseline CAD presence or severity, which is the outcome type this project's Model 1 and Model 2 are built around. The defensible framing is therefore to include dominance as a confound-adjustment covariate, not as a hypothesized independent CAD-severity risk factor in its own right, since the literature closest to this project's actual outcome argues against the latter.

Two practical gaps deserve explicit flagging rather than silent assumption. First, **no validated automated method exists to derive dominance from a 3D segmentation or centerline tree.** A dedicated coronary-tree-anatomy-labeling paper explicitly requires dominance as a pre-known human-supplied input and lists automatic dominance detection as unsolved future work ([PMC5677991](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5677991/)); the automated classifiers that do exist work on 2D angiographic video, not 3D CT segmentation ([PMC12110073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12110073/)). A rule-based geometric derivation (identify which system's branch continues past the crux/PDA territory) is plausible in principle given this project's own centerline tree, but it would be a novel, unvalidated build with no published implementation or accuracy figure to calibrate against. Until built and validated, dominance should be sourced from manual annotation (e.g., the Descriptors metadata this project already reads for stratified evaluation), not from an untested programmatic derivation presented as ground truth. Second, on encoding: the literature's default is a 3-level categorical variable (right/left/codominant), matching the clinical definition exactly and used in the CAD-RADS multivariable study ([PMC13363422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363422/)); a binary collapse (right-dominant versus left-or-codominant) also appears in outcomes studies on the rationale that left and codominant circulations share a "less balanced perfusion" mechanism ([Circulation: CQO 2012](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circoutcomes.111.964593)). No study compares model performance under different encodings, and no precedent exists for a continuous dominance proxy. The 3-level categorical is the standards-consistent default, but it costs 2 predictor parameters rather than 1, a detail that matters directly for the budget arithmetic below.

## One curvature channel, not two, and what the whole covariate list should look like

Mean absolute curvature and RMS curvature are a same-domain, mechanically related pair: both are aggregations of the identical per-point curvature signal along a centerline, differing only in how the aggregation is done (mean-absolute versus root-mean-square). The one study that tested them head-to-head against a coronary hemodynamic outcome, Kashyap et al.'s 127-patient CFD cohort, found mean absolute curvature the stronger predictor of low-TAWSS area (R^2 = 0.112-0.232 across vessels, p<0.001) with RMS curvature explaining a similar but somewhat smaller share (R^2 = 0.093-0.164), and the paper's own recommendation going forward is to standardize on average absolute curvature ([PMC8764056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)). Overlapping R^2 against the same outcome is circumstantial evidence the two metrics carry substantially shared information, consistent with the broader clinical-prediction-methodology guidance to exclude one member of any highly correlated pair before model development rather than rely on regularization to sort it out in a small sample: "not including both total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol" is the field's own worked example of exactly this move ([Shipe et al.](https://jtd.amegroups.org/article/view/26585/html)). No source gives a direct pairwise correlation coefficient between mean absolute and RMS curvature specifically, which remains an open gap, but the combination of Kashyap et al.'s own resolution of the redundancy in their own recommendations and Shipe et al.'s general pruning guidance is convergent, independent support for the same conclusion. For Model 2, this means keeping mean absolute curvature as the sole profile channel and dropping RMS curvature entirely, rather than encoding both as parallel per-point sequences; the Horton-Strahler ratio was never a candidate for this channel decision in the first place, since it has no per-point form at all, so the profile-channel question really was always a binary choice between the two curvature variants, and the evidence says collapse it to one.

This pruning decision is not merely tidy, it is what keeps the rest of the recommended additions inside a workable sample-size budget. Growing Model 1 from a small initial predictor set toward 7-9 predictors is exactly the transition where simulation evidence shows the required events-per-variable (EPV) doubles from 10 to 20 to control coefficient bias, and at the low end of that range (EPV 2-5), a meaningful fraction of resampled models fail to even converge ([van Smeden et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)). A worked sample-size table for a comparable prediction-model target shows the same doubling pattern in absolute terms, roughly 11.6 events required per predictor parameter at a fixed prevalence and shrinkage target, meaning a jump from 15 to 25 parameters raises the minimum required events from 174 to 290 ([PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)). Applying the prioritization this report and its predecessor notes converge on (collapse the curvature pair first, then add predictors from domains with zero existing representation, angle and dominance, ahead of any further same-domain geometric refinement, and treat torsion with the same collinearity suspicion as the curvature pair rather than adding it for free) gives a concrete whole-tree Model 1 covariate list:

| # | Covariate | Domain | Parameters | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Age | clinical | 1 | solid |
| 2 | Sex | clinical | 1 | solid |
| 3 | Mean absolute curvature, RCA | curvature | 1 | solid (RMS curvature dropped as redundant) |
| 4 | Mean absolute curvature, LAD | curvature | 1 | solid |
| 5 | Mean absolute curvature, LCx | curvature | 1 | solid |
| 6 | Bifurcation angle, LM (LAD-LCx take-off) | angle | 1 | solid, primary-verified |
| 7-8 | Coronary dominance (right/left/codominant) | topology/anatomy | 2 | includable, contested as a direct risk factor, sourced from manual annotation pending a validated automated derivation |

That is **8 predictor parameters**, landing squarely in the range the sample-size literature flags as requiring the doubled EPV=20 target, i.e. roughly 140-180 CAD-positive events for this exact list, not the ~30 events that might have been adequate for a 3-predictor design ([PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/); [PMC12106283](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/)). Adding both the Horton-Strahler ratio (per-vessel, 3 more parameters) and torsion (1 more) on top of this list would push it to 12 parameters, which is not a hypothetical edge case in the cited literature but its own explicit worst-case illustration: at EPV=2 in a 12-predictor model, only 7 of 1,000 simulated models even converged ([PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/)). This is the concrete, arithmetic version of why both metrics should stay out rather than be added "for completeness."

For Model 2, the same prioritization applies with more headroom: a late-fusion architecture concentrates most of a small network's parameters in the width of its first layer, so scalar covariates fused near the output (age, sex, dominance, bifurcation angle) add only a small, computable number of weights regardless of count, unlike a per-point profile channel, which multiplies its width through the entire preceding encoder ([GCondNet](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.06302v4)). This justification is architectural and directionally sound but not simulation-validated the way EPV is for Model 1; no neural-network sample-size heuristic found in this research (10x, 30x, or 50x the weight count) carries the same evidentiary weight as van Smeden's EPV simulations, so Model 2's greater tolerance for scalar covariates should be treated as real but qualitatively, not numerically, justified. The recommended Model 2 design is therefore: one curvature profile channel (mean absolute curvature, per vessel, shared-weight encoder with vessel-identity embedding, per this project's already-established whole-tree architecture), fused at the top with age, sex, bifurcation angle, and dominance, with no torsion channel or scalar and no Horton-Strahler feature of any form.

## Conclusion

The through-line across all three questions is the same discipline the project's own methodology notes already prescribe: prune same-domain redundancy before adding anything new, and require that anything new clear an actual evidence bar rather than a plausibility bar. Bifurcation angle and dominance clear that bar, though for different reasons and with different confidence, angle because it is genuinely well-evidenced and primary-verified, dominance because it is an unavoidable confound even though its direct CAD-risk case is contested and its automated derivation is unbuilt. Horton-Strahler and torsion do not clear it: one fails on a structural mismatch between what CTA can resolve and what the metric needs to be stable, the other has had its central mechanistic justification directly tested and found wanting in a 2025 coronary-specific study. What changes most concretely from this round of research is not the addition of new candidates but the removal of two: the curvature pair collapses to one metric, and torsion's status moves from "contested but locked" to "excluded," a genuine downgrade driven by new evidence rather than a reinterpretation of old evidence. The resulting 8-parameter Model 1 list is not a compromise forced by caution for its own sake; it is the list that the sample-size arithmetic says this cohort can actually support without manufacturing the appearance of discrimination that a larger, unpruned list would risk.
