# Integrate the frailty to turn segments into people

The "pooled" model is a **segment-level binary GLMM with a complementary log-log (cloglog) link and one person random intercept**: for person i and trunk segment j (up to 9), P(y_ij = 1 | b_i) = 1 − exp{−exp(x_ij'β + b_i)}, b_i ~ N(0, σ²). Its person-level output is not a separate model but an identity. Segments are independent only given b_i, so P(no new plaque in any segment | b) = exp(−e^b S_i) with S_i = Σ_j exp(x_ij'β), and the risk of any new plaque for a new person is **P_i = 1 − ∫ exp(−e^b S_i) φ(b; 0, σ̂²) db**, a one-dimensional integral evaluated by Gauss–Hermite quadrature. That formula is exact under the model; every error enters through the model's assumptions or through numerics. Two shortcuts that look natural are both wrong: setting b = 0 gives the risk of the *median* person, and multiplying marginal segment risks always *overestimates* P_i. Neither the cloglog-closure identity nor its use for person prediction has a verified precedent in coronary imaging; the algebra is borrowed from shared-frailty survival analysis, multilevel prediction for new clusters and noisy-OR multiple-instance learning. The model's main strength is statistical efficiency for geometry coefficients; its main weakness is that much of that extra information comes from *which* segments get plaque within a person, so a likelihood-ratio test of a geometry block can reject on "where" information while the thesis question is "who". For the thesis, the cloglog/hazard reading, the random-intercept GLMM, marginal vs conditional prediction, quadrature and the aggregation identity belong in the Technical Background; the segment set, covariates, coding of local features, fold rules, numerical settings and person-level evaluation belong in the Methodology. No cohort results appear here, and every own derivation and unverified citation is labelled.

Labels used throughout: **[derivation]** = standard algebra written out by the project, checkable by the reader but not quoted from a source; **[own sim]** = project simulation or quadrature on made-up parameters, never CGPS data; **[unverified]** = a reference recalled from background knowledge whose text or DOI was not opened in this research and must be checked before it enters `refs.bib`.

## A cloglog link makes each coefficient a log hazard ratio

The model is a generalised linear mixed model. Conditional on the person effect, each segment is a Bernoulli trial whose success probability passes through the inverse cloglog link H(v) = 1 − exp(−e^v); the random effects are normal with mean zero and are "summarized according to the unique elements of Σ, known as variance components" rather than estimated as parameters ([Stata, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)). Unlike logit and probit, the cloglog link is asymmetric: it approaches 1 sharply and 0 slowly, and the Stata manual describes it as the usual choice "when the positive (or negative) outcome is rare" ([Stata, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)). For small probabilities 1 − exp(−e^η) ≈ e^η, so cloglog, log and logit fits nearly coincide at low risk and diverge only at high risk [derivation].

The reason to prefer cloglog here is not fit but interpretation. Suppose new plaque in a segment arises as a Poisson process with cumulative hazard Λ over the follow-up window, and only "at least one onset" is observed. Then P(Y = 1) = 1 − exp(−Λ), and with proportional hazards Λ = Λ₀(t)·exp(x'β), so cloglog(p) = log Λ₀(t) + x'β [derivation]. **The intercept absorbs the baseline cumulative hazard and each β_k is a log hazard ratio**, invariant to window length while proportional hazards holds. This is the grouped-data proportional-hazards model of Prentice and Gloeckler (1978, Biometrics 34:57–67), whose interval hazard is cloglog-linear in covariates and is commonly fitted as a binomial GLM ([SAS PROC LOGISTIC](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/63962/HTML/default/statug_logistic_sect072.htm); [MetricGate summary](https://metricgate.com/docs/prentice-gloeckler-discrete-ph/); DOI 10.2307/2529588 seen only on an aggregator, [Synapse](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1994670953) [unverified]). A design with one follow-up scan per person is current-status (case-I interval-censored) data, and a free coefficient on log follow-up time corresponds to a Weibull-type baseline [derivation]. The logit alternative has a structural drawback: an odds ratio from a hazard process depends on the baseline risk and window, the non-collapsibility of the OR [derivation].

The same Poisson reading explains how **segment length** enters. If hazard per millimetre is homogeneous, Λ = λ·L and log L is an offset with coefficient fixed at 1; as a free covariate with coefficient γ, Λ ∝ L^γ, so γ = 1 means "plaque arises independently per mm", γ < 1 means risk concentrated at sites such as ostia or bifurcations [derivation]. No coronary study using segment length as an offset or exposure term was found; the ICONIC lesion analysis used distance from the ostium as a predictor, not as exposure ([Han et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8792800)). A free coefficient is the safer default, since AHA/SCCT segment boundaries are landmark-defined and length is confounded with position and vessel size; if position intercepts are in the model, γ is identified only from within-position length variation [derivation].

### The random intercept changes what β means

In η_ij = x_ij'β + b_i the coefficients are **conditional (subject-specific)**: they describe how risk changes for a fixed person. Averaging over b gives population-averaged risks that are no longer of cloglog form, and with a normal intercept there is no closed form, so the marginal model requires numerical integration ([Gory et al., marginally interpretable GLMMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01526)). Random-effects coefficients are larger in magnitude than marginal (GEE-type) ones ([Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/); [Liang & Zeger 1986](https://www.biostat.jhsph.edu/~jleek/teaching/2011/754/reading/liangandzeger.pdf)). The familiar attenuation factor 1/√(1 + c²σ²) with c = 16√3/(15π) is a **logit** result (Zeger, Liang & Albert 1988, Biometrics 44:1049–1060 [unverified]) and does not transfer to cloglog. For cloglog, a first-order expansion gives 1 − E[exp(−e^{η+b})] ≈ e^{η+σ²/2} at low risk, so marginal slopes are nearly unattenuated for rare outcomes and shrink as risk accumulates [derivation]; the project's quadrature put the marginal slope at σ² = 1.5 at 0.97 of the conditional slope at risk 0.02 and 0.45 at risk 0.96 [own sim]. Two frailty laws give closed forms: a gamma frailty, popular "mainly for inference simplicity due to conjugacy between Gamma distribution and the complementary-log-log link" ([marginalizable frailty model, arXiv 1403.6744](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.6744)), and a positive-stable frailty, under which the marginal model is again exactly cloglog with all coefficients multiplied by the stable index (Hougaard 2000 [unverified]). A frailty model is, in this sense, a random-intercept mixed model with a cloglog conditional link ([arXiv 1403.6744](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.6744)).

The size of σ² is hard to communicate. In the latent-variable form the cloglog residual is Gumbel with variance **π²/6**, versus π²/3 for logit ([Stata, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)), so a latent-scale ICC is σ²/(σ² + π²/6) (Nakagawa, Johnson & Schielzeth 2017, J R Soc Interface 14:20170213, abstract seen at [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28904005/); the per-link variance table in the full text was not opened). That ICC is not the correlation between two binary outcomes, and equal σ² values do not mean equal clustering across links [derivation]. A more readable summary is the **median hazard ratio**, MHR = exp(√(2σ²)·Φ⁻¹(0.75)) ≈ exp(0.954σ), the hazard-scale analogue of the median odds ratio ([Austin, Wagner & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5299617/)).

### Estimation needs adaptive quadrature, and tests need matched fits

The marginal likelihood is a product over persons of one-dimensional integrals, L(β, σ) = Π_i ∫ Π_j p_ij(b)^{y_ij}{1 − p_ij(b)}^{1−y_ij} φ(b; 0, σ²) db, with no closed form [derivation]. The Laplace approximation "is equivalent to mode-curvature adaptive Gaussian quadrature with one integration point" and "has been known to produce biased parameter estimates", mostly in the variance components ([Stata, mecloglog](https://www.stata.com/manuals/memecloglog.pdf)). Bias of Laplace estimators depends mainly on the discreteness of the response, is worse for binary data and falls with cluster size ([Joe 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/csdana/v52y2008i12p5066-5074.html)), though Capanu, Gönen and Begg found Laplace surprisingly good even for small binary clusters ([MSKCC paper 22](https://biostats.bepress.com/mskccbiostat/paper22)); the evidence is mixed. Quadrature centred at the conditional mode is accurate and efficient, whereas quadrature centred at the expected value is not (Pinheiro & Bates 1995, summarised at [GLMMadaptive](https://rdrr.io/cran/GLMMadaptive)), and non-adaptive Gauss–Hermite "gives valid results only when the number of quadrature points is high" ([Lesaffre & Spiessens 2001](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssc/v50y2001i3p325-335.html)). Even adaptive GH is less accurate than its original error rate suggested ([Jin & Andersson 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/biomet/v107y2020i3p737-744..html)). In software, `lme4::glmer` allows adaptive GH (nAGQ > 1) only for a single scalar random effect, exactly the person intercept here ([glmer docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/lme4/html/glmer.html)); `glmmTMB` uses Laplace ([Brooks et al. 2017](https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2017-066/RJ-2017-066.pdf)); `GLMMadaptive` and Stata's `mecloglog` use adaptive quadrature.

A nested block of k fixed effects is tested by LR = 2(ℓ₁ − ℓ₀) against χ²_k, with both models fitted by ML, on identical rows, with the same link and the same integration rule and node count; σ² is then a nuisance parameter present in both models, so no boundary correction applies [derivation]. Only a test of σ² = 0 itself sits on the boundary, where the LR follows a 50:50 mixture of χ²₀ and χ²₁ (Self & Liang 1987, [PDF copy](https://pages.stat.wisc.edu/~larget/Stat998/Fall2015/Self-Liang-1987.pdf)). That result is asymptotic, two of its special cases were later challenged ([arXiv 2509.00223](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.00223v1)), and with small binary clusters a parametric bootstrap is the safer check.

## The person risk is a frailty Laplace transform, exact under the model

The aggregation follows from three lines [derivation]. Given b, P(A_i = 0 | b) = Π_j (1 − p_ij(b)) = Π_j exp(−e^{η_ij + b}) = exp(−e^b S_i). So, **conditional on b, "any new plaque" is itself a cloglog model with linear predictor log S_i + b**, and marginally P_i = 1 − L_σ(S_i), where L_σ(s) = E[exp(−s·e^b)] is the Laplace transform of the lognormal frailty e^b. The integrand depends on the segments only through the scalar S_i, so the cost does not grow with the number of segments. This closure under the any-event aggregation is a property of cloglog (equivalently, of Poisson superposition); with logit the aggregation is still valid but needs the full product inside the integral and loses the sufficient-statistic form. No source was found that states this closure for GLMMs in these words, so it should be presented in the thesis as a derivation. The lognormal Laplace transform has no closed form, though a Lambert-W approximation exists ([Asmussen, Jensen & Rojas-Nandayapa 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.05878)); a gamma frailty with mean 1 and variance θ gives P_i = 1 − (1 + θS_i)^{−1/θ}, the negative-binomial zero probability [derivation]. In shared-frailty survival analysis a cluster's joint survival is likewise the frailty Laplace transform at the summed cumulative hazard (Duchateau & Janssen, *The Frailty Model*, Springer 2008, doi:10.1007/978-0-387-72835-3, [record](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/handle/1942/9641); Hougaard 2000 [unverified]), and the closest worked analogue is udder quarters clustered within cows, observed only at visits and modelled with a shared gamma frailty ([Goethals et al. 2009](https://research.itg.be/en/publications/modeling-interval-censored-clustered-cow-udder-quarter-infection-/)).

Log S_i is a log-sum-exp, a smoothed maximum of the segment linear predictors. Person risk is therefore dominated by the person's highest-hazard segments, and a covariate that raises hazard in one segment moves log S_i only by roughly that segment's share of S_i [derivation]. This dilution is the arithmetic behind small expected person-level gains from local geometry: a segment hazard ratio of 1.3 on a share w = 0.3 of the hazard multiplies the conditional person hazard by (1 − w) + w·1.3 ≈ 1.09 [derivation, w illustrative].

### Two shortcuts fail in opposite directions

Setting b = 0 is the most common error. Pavlou and co-authors note that marginal risks "are often obtained, incorrectly, using only estimates of the fixed effects"; in their logit simulations, b = 0 predictions had a calibration slope of 0.788 at ICC 0.30 against about 0.98 for integrated predictions, and the error was small at ICC 0.05 ([Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/)). Their critique targets Bouwmeester et al., whose marginal predictions set the random effects to zero ([Bouwmeester et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3658967/)). The mechanism is simple: P(A | b) increases in b and b has median 0, so **b = 0 gives the median person's risk, not the mean** [derivation]. With g(b) = exp(−S e^b), g''(0) changes sign at S = 1, so to second order the plug-in underestimates risk when the b = 0 risk is below 1 − e⁻¹ ≈ 0.632 and overestimates above it [derivation; a small-σ approximation, not an exact threshold]. Predictions come out too spread, matching Pavlou's slope below 1.

The naive product 1 − Π_j(1 − p̄_ij) of *marginal* segment risks is a different error. All factors 1 − p_ij(b) decrease in the same b, so they are positively associated, and by the Harris (Chebyshev) inequality E[Π(1 − p)] ≥ Π E[1 − p]: **the naive product always overestimates P_i**, with equality only at σ = 0 [derivation]. Martin and colleagues make the same point for multiple outcomes: multiplying separately modelled risks "is only valid if the outcomes are conditionally independent given the covariates" ([Martin et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.07624)). The two errors differ in one more respect. The b = 0 risk is a monotone function of S_i and leaves person-level C unchanged, but the naive product depends on how hazard is spread across segments and **can re-rank people**: at S = 0.6 and σ² = 1.5, naive risks were 0.661 with hazard spread over 9 segments and 0.550 with one segment, against a true 0.491 for both [own sim]. The magnitude of the naive overestimate depends strongly on σ² and the number of segments; earlier project figures of "about 20 percentage points" were inconsistent with their own stated σ² and should not be quoted [own sim].

### A held-out person is a new cluster

Under person-split cross-validation, each held-out person has no admissible information about b_i. The correct prediction for "a new randomly sampled cluster" integrates over the estimated random-effect distribution ([Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/); [Skrondal & Rabe-Hesketh 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v172y2009i3p659-687.html)), with β̂, σ̂ and every preprocessing constant taken from the training fold. An empirical-Bayes b̂_i uses the person's own follow-up segment outcomes, which define A_i, so it is target leakage [derivation]. Cross-validation by person is the cluster analogue of leave-centre-out validation in the multicentre literature, though no retrieved source prescribes grouped K-fold explicitly. Wynants and colleagues add the rule that frames the evaluation: "models should reflect the data structure, while the level of model validation should reflect the research question" ([Wynants et al. 2018](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/106551)).

Numerically, with nodes x_k and weights w_k for ∫e^{−x²}f(x)dx, E[f(b)] = π^{−1/2} Σ_k w_k f(√2·σ·x_k) [derivation]. The integrand is smooth, but its transition in b sharpens as σ·S grows, so non-adaptive nodes centred at 0 degrade at large σ. In the project's tests on synthetic data, 40 nodes agreed with 200 to ≤ 3×10⁻⁷ for σ ≤ 1.5, but 30 plain nodes in the *fitting* likelihood gave a log-likelihood error of −1.3 at σ = 2 and inflated the null LR size from 0.063 to 0.083 [own sim]. A Monte Carlo check of the prediction integral with 10⁵ draws has a standard error near 8×10⁻⁴, so a 10⁻⁴ agreement tolerance would fail a correct integral; a high-node GH or adaptive integrator is the right reference [own sim]. No package returns P(any segment) directly: `GLMMadaptive`'s "marginal" prediction gives marginal *segment* risks (the naive-product input) and its "mean_subject" prediction is the b = 0 answer ([GLMMadaptive vignette](https://drizopoulos.github.io/GLMMadaptive/articles/Methods_MixMod.html)); in `brms` the correct route is new levels with `sample_new_levels = "gaussian"` ([brms reference](https://paulbuerkner.com/brms/reference/prepare_predictions.html)).

### Person-level evaluation is ordinary binary-outcome validation

Once each person has one P̂_i and one A_i, persons are independent units and standard measures apply to out-of-fold predictions: C-statistic, calibration-in-the-large (intercept with logit P̂_i as offset, ideal 0), calibration slope (ideal 1), a flexible calibration curve and the Brier score ([Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/); [Van Calster et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6912996/)). Binary C, Brier and calibration suit current-status data scored at each person's own follow-up ([SAS PROC LOGISTIC](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/63962/HTML/default/statug_logistic_sect072.htm)). Three cautions follow from the aggregation [derivation]. **C is blind to the main aggregation errors**: for one σ, integrated risk, b = 0 risk and any monotone transform of Ŝ_i have identical C, so only calibration checks the aggregation itself. Calibration should be reported stratified by number of evaluable segments, since a person with more graded segments has both more chances and a larger S_i. And within-person C (van Klaveren et al. 2014, [PMC3897966](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3897966/)) measures "where" discrimination, not person risk, while a pooled segment-row C mixes the two. For intervals, a spread over CV repeats is split noise only: on mock data, repeat percentiles of ΔC were about 9 times narrower than a person bootstrap [own sim], consistent with CV's under-covering naive intervals ([Bates, Hastie & Tibshirani 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.00673)). After a prespecified coefficient test, testing ΔC again is redundant, since the "no improvement" nulls are equivalent to "the new predictor is not a risk factor" ([Pepe et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625503)).

## Five assumptions carry the efficiency gain, and each can fail silently

The segment GLMM's appeal is information. Collapsing a count of affected units to "any > 0" discards it: negative-binomial models gave confidence intervals 61–69% as wide as logistic regression on the dichotomised outcome in simulation ([Sroka & Nagaraja 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6195979/)), and eye-level models with a person effect beat person-level analysis in power ([Glynn & Rosner 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3454458)). The project's exact Fisher-information calculation at σ = 1.5 gave a person-level "any" model **0.672** of the segment model's information for an all-segment effect and **0.042** for an effect on 2 of 9 segments [own derivation]. The same Sroka and Nagaraja paper supplies the counterweight: fitting the wrong count distribution could bias estimates severely, even reversing sign, while the dichotomised logistic model stayed robust ([Sroka & Nagaraja 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6195979/)). The efficiency is bought with assumptions.

| Assumption | What breaks if it fails | Check or remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Segments independent given one b_i | Extra within-vessel dependence means a one-b model overestimates P(any) for the same marginal segment risks (Harris argument) [derivation] | Vessel-within-person intercept; still nested 1-D integrals under cloglog |
| Constant σ across persons | Person risk mis-scaled differently by risk group; calibration slope ≠ 1, invisible in segment fit [derivation] | Person-level calibration by subgroup |
| Normal b | Mainly affects tails | Compare lognormal and gamma on person calibration |
| S_i sums exactly the outcome-defining segments | Extra segments inflate S_i; omitted outcome segments make under-prediction by construction; informative cluster size biases intercepts and marginal predictions ([Pavlou, Ambler & Omar 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8254921/)) | One shared outcome definition; n_i as a tested predictor |
| b independent of covariates | A segment-varying covariate's single β blends within- and between-person effects, biased if b correlates with the person mean | Mundlak / within-between split, approximate in nonlinear models |

The within-between decomposition η_ij = β_W(x_ij − x̄_i) + β_B·x̄_i + … + b_i comes from Mundlak, who showed for linear models that once the dependence between regressors and effects is modelled, random and fixed effects give the same within estimate ([Mundlak 1978](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1978/01/01/pooling-time-series-and-cross-section-data)); Bell, Fairbrother and Jones argue the random-effects version "can provide everything that FE promises and more" ([Bell et al. 2019](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/ws/files/196855552/Bell2019_Article_FixedAndRandomEffectsModelsMak.pdf)). In cloglog the device is only approximate: there is no conditional likelihood to eliminate b (unlike conditional logistic regression), and it assumes E[b | x] linear in x̄_i [derivation]. A person mean over 2–9 segments is also a noisy estimate of latent geometry, which attenuates β_B; treating group means as perfectly reliable biases contextual effects ([Lüdtke et al. 2008](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/87423/the-multilevel-latent-covariate-model-a-new-more-reliable-approach-to-group-level-effects-in-contextual-studies)). A Wald test of β_W = β_B must compare coefficients on the same scale: with the mean z-scored by its own (smaller) SD, equal true effects were falsely rejected in 27.5% of simulations instead of 5% [own sim].

### The test of "who" can reject on "where"

This is the pitfall most likely to change an interpretation. Person risk depends on geometry only through log S_i, but the segment likelihood also uses *allocation*: which segments get plaque, given how many. For a feature coded locally on 2 of 9 segments, the project's exact calculation split the segment model's information into **6.2% from the count of positive segments and 93.8% from allocation given the count** [own derivation]. In a small simulation that held each person's S_i, and so P(any), fixed while a feature moved hazard toward the local segments, the segment LR test rejected in 12 of 12 replicates and a person-level logistic test, whose null was true, in 1 of 12 [own sim, 12 replicates, large HR; direction only]. The segment-model and person-model nulls coincide only if the feature truly has no effect outside the segments it is coded on. Since the thesis text currently treats only the "who" question, this matters directly: a geometry LR rejection in the pooled model is not by itself evidence that geometry predicts who, and needs the person-level benchmark or an out-of-fold person-risk shift beside it. Coding each person-level feature as a main effect on all segments plus a local contrast makes the "who" part testable on the main effects [derivation].

Two readouts that look natural also mislead. **The change in σ̂² is not a clean "who" measure**: the cloglog level-1 variance is fixed at π²/6, so explaining segment-to-segment variation rescales the person variance upward. In single-replicate simulations a within-only covariate raised σ̂² by 8–43% while a genuine all-segment person effect of HR 1.3 lowered it by only 5% [own sim]; Snijders and Bosker call the proportional reduction in variance components "not an attractive parameter" ([abstract](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/modeled-variance-in-two-level-models/)). And **coefficients from the segment cloglog GLMM and a person-level logistic model are not numerically comparable** by any constant: they differ in link, outcome, conditioning and dilution [derivation]. Finally, two fitting traps appeared on synthetic data. In a log σ parameterisation the score for log σ vanishes at σ = 0, so an optimiser started near the bound can stop there and report success, here 28–454 log-likelihood units below the optimum; and a zero-event covariate level produced β ≈ −15 with a "converged" flag [own sim]. A fit at σ̂ ≈ 0 reduces person risk to the independence product, which is a legitimate finding only if cold starts confirm it.

## Precedents exist for each piece, but not for the assembled model

The aggregation rule has three families of precedent. **Shared frailty** supplies the algebra, as above. **Multilevel clinical prediction** supplies the prediction target: Pavlou et al. on integrated marginal predictions ([2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/)), Wynants et al. on matching prediction type to validation level ([2018](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/106551)), and Falconieri et al., who found that with adequate samples conditional models were calibrated where marginal ones were not ([2020](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/developing-risk-models-for-multicenter-data-using-standard-logist/)); all are logit, none cloglog. **Multiple-instance learning** supplies the shape: a bag is positive if at least one instance is ([Dietterich et al. 1997](https://sci2s.ugr.es/keel/pdf/algorithm/articulo/1997%20-%20DietterichLathropLozano-Perez%20-%20AI.pdf)), and noisy-OR writes p(y = 1 | x) = 1 − Π[1 − σ(w'x_j)] ([Raykar et al. 2008](https://icml.cc/imls/conferences/2008/papers/587.pdf)); Liao et al. combine the top five lung nodules into a subject cancer probability with a leaky noisy-OR ([arXiv 1711.08324](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.08324)), and mixed-model aggregation of instance predictions appears in MixMIL ([AISTATS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02455)). Standard noisy-OR is exactly the naive product, since it assumes instances independent given features; the random intercept is the statistical fix for shared susceptibility, and the "leak" fixes something else (positives no instance explains) [derivation]. Unlike typical MIL, segment labels are observed here, so this is supervised multilevel modelling, not weak supervision.

Coronary imaging offers lesion- and segment-level designs but not this model. ICONIC handled within-patient dependence in lesion-level Cox models with **shared frailty**, finding culprit-lesion HRs of 2.71 for short distance from the ostium, 2.14 for bifurcation and 2.31 for tortuosity, in 116 matched ACS patients from a referral registry ([Han et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8792800)). PARADIGM reported per-lesion C of 0.895 against 0.825 per-patient, but the abstract does not say how clustering was handled, and a lesion-row C mixes between- and within-patient discrimination ([Lee et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/)). PREDICTION-related work used linear mixed models for continuous progression ([PMC10939719](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10939719/)). Every verified precedent is a referral or indicated-imaging cohort with prevalent disease, so none transfers effect sizes to plaque-free baselines. No study was found that integrated segment predictions over a random effect to a person risk, or that used a length offset; after about 19 searches this is weak evidence of a gap, not proof. The thesis should therefore justify the cloglog GLMM by the grouped-hazard and frailty arguments, not by field convention. Terminology deserves one sentence too: in hierarchical modelling "pooling" means shrinkage (complete, no and partial pooling; [Gelman 2004](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/463-663/week06/some%20nice%20papers/multi.pdf)), and "pooled" estimators often mean ignoring clustering, so the segment-to-person step reads more clearly as **aggregation**, with "pooled" defined once if the codebase name is kept.

## Conclusion

The pooled model is less a modelling choice than a consequence: once segments share a person frailty, there is one correct person-level functional, and cloglog makes it a Laplace transform of that frailty at the summed segment hazard. That turns the problem into a shared-frailty survival model observed at one time point, which is well understood, and it makes the tempting shortcuts recognisably wrong in a way the C-statistic cannot see. For the thesis this means person-level calibration is the one diagnostic that tests the aggregation, and it should carry weight in the Methodology rather than appear as a supplementary plot.

The sharper implication concerns the question the model answers. Its efficiency over a person-level model comes almost entirely from within-person allocation, the "where" information that the current thesis text sets aside. A geometry LR test in the pooled model is therefore strongest exactly where it is least about "who". The defensible position is to present the segment GLMM as the efficient engine if correctly specified, the direct person-level model as the robust benchmark a "who" claim must survive, and to code person-level geometry so that its "who" component is tested on its own. Several load-bearing numbers here, the allocation share, the σ̂² inflation and the quadrature errors, come from the project's own simulations on made-up parameters; they point consistently in one direction but should be reconfirmed on the fitted base model before the primary test is locked, and the unverified references (Zeger–Liang–Albert, Hougaard, Neuhaus–Kalbfleisch, Self–Liang DOI, Prentice–Gloeckler DOI, Nakagawa's link-specific variance table, Steyerberg) must be checked against the originals before they enter `refs.bib`.
