# Keep one geometry slope, test heterogeneity once

**No: the primary model should keep one shared coefficient per geometry feature, and segment-specific weights should enter only as one prespecified secondary test and as shrunken descriptive estimates.** Three findings drive this. First, no published coronary study, imaging, CFD or clinical, has estimated geometry–plaque slopes separately by artery or by proximal/mid/distal position and tested the difference; the large CCTA registries model artery as a main effect and give every other predictor one shared coefficient ([Bax et al. 2022](https://academic.oup.com/ehjcimaging/article/23/11/1482/6574367)), so there is nothing to prespecify segment-specific effects from. Second, the df cost is large next to the ~15-df person-level Riley budget. Curvature and taper by artery add 4 df, artery + position adds 8, and full 9-segment slopes add 16. If the truth is a common effect, spreading it over more df cuts the power of the confirmatory test sharply: 0.56 → 0.45 → 0.38 → 0.30 for 5, 9, 13 and 21 df at the same noncentrality [own calc]. Third, the thesis is currently "who"-only, and segment-specific slopes move the model's information further towards *which* segments develop plaque ("where"), not *who* does. They are therefore unlikely to change person-level discrimination, and they make a significant block test harder to read as a "who" result. Heterogeneity is mechanistically plausible: flow, diameter, branching and cardiac motion differ by segment. So the defensible design is a shared-slope primary LR test, one prespecified joint LR test of curvature and taper × artery (+4 df) reported as a finding but never used to choose the primary model, and per-segment-type slopes estimated with shrinkage and shown descriptively. LM features are already segment-specific by anatomical row restriction, which costs no df.

## The literature gives a direction for geometry effects but no segment-specific magnitudes

The mechanistic case for heterogeneity is real but qualitative. Geometry affects plaque through local low or oscillatory wall shear stress (WSS). Disturbed flow at branches and curvatures drives atherogenic endothelial gene expression in the same way in every vessel ([Chiu & Chien 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00047.2009)). The *amount* of low-WSS wall that a given bend or taper produces, however, depends on flow and diameter, which fall from proximal to distal and differ between arteries [derivation, from Poiseuille WSS ∝ Q/r³]. Taper creates low-WSS regions independently of curvature in idealised models ([Johnston et al., MODSIM 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)). In LAD/diagonal models the cardiac curvature radius mattered more than the bifurcation angle ([Chiastra et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2017.04.016)). The WSS–plaque relation itself depends on disease stage: it is strong where the vessel has enlarged to compensate and disappears once the lumen has narrowed ([Wahle et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2006.03.002)). The RCA has no main bifurcation at its origin and moves most with the heartbeat, so static CCTA curvature is plausibly a noisier proxy of disturbed flow there. This last point is a mechanistic argument with no quantitative source.

None of this yields numbers. The largest WSS study, PREDICTION, reports effects pooled over arteries ([Stone et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.112.096438)). The one study that looked at WSS by vessel had 41 vessels in 40 ACS patients. With vessel-specific tertiles, low WSS was linked to progression in the LAD and RCA but not the LCx; with study-wide thresholds, **only the RCA** showed it. The authors say they had too few vessels per type to compare them ([Hartman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8586146/)). Part of that apparent heterogeneity was a scale effect: median WSS was 0.56 Pa in the LAD against 0.92 Pa in the RCA, so one absolute threshold meant different things in different vessels. Geometry–plaque studies are small, cross-sectional and mostly symptomatic. Several are single-artery by design: RCA tortuosity in 38,691 angiograms, OR 1.05 per SD for CAD ([Tello Ayala et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacadv.2026.102829)), or LAD curvature on IVUS ([Iwami et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0002-9149(98)00340-3)). Tortuosity even changes sign across designs, protective in angiographic studies ([Li et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0300060518804723)) and a risk factor in others. A significant effect in one artery and none in another is not an interaction test, and no study ran one.

Position is different. Plaque clusters proximally, with a gradient that differs by artery: per 10 mm from the ostium, occlusion risk fell 13% in the RCA, 30% in the LAD and 26% in the LCx ([Wang et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.CIR.0000135468.67850.F4)). In PARADIGM, any plaque progression occurred in 77.4% of LADs, 52.7% of RCAs and 41.0% of LCxs ([Bax et al. 2022](https://academic.oup.com/ehjcimaging/article/23/11/1482/6574367)). These are differences in **baseline hazard**, which the thesis model already absorbs with segment dummies and log length. They are not evidence that the per-SD geometry effect differs. Precedent from other position-structured data points the same way. In multilevel periodontal models (sites within teeth within persons), tooth type enters as a main effect, and random slopes vary over persons, not over site types ([Tomasi, Leyland & Wennström 2007](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/15848)).

The one segment-specific structure the literature supports is **anatomical row restriction** for bifurcation features. Plaque at the LM bifurcation lies opposite the flow divider, and the ostial LAD was affected in 64% of cases in one MSCT series, where a wider LM angle went with LAD disease ([Rodriguez-Granillo et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10554-006-9144-1)). In CFD of LM bifurcations in people without significant atherosclerosis, LM–LAD geometry drove low WSS in the *proximal LAD* ([Malvè et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10439-014-1056-y)). The LM bifurcation is also the most variable coronary bifurcation (distal angle 80° ± 21°) ([Tu et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10554-011-9996-x)). The thesis already enters the LM angle only on the pLAD and pLCx rows. That is a segment-specific weight with the other seven segments' coefficients fixed at 0, and it costs no extra df. By the same reasoning, the LM→LAD diameter ratio would go on LAD rows (or pLAD only) and the LM→LCx ratio on LCx rows. This is a project proposal and must be fixed before outcomes are seen.

## Every extra slope is paid for in df and in power

The current shared design uses 5 geometry df: curvature and taper, each shared over the 9 trunk segments, plus the LM angle and the two LM diameter ratios. Prediction-model guidance is consistent about interactions. Harrell's strategy is to "specify relevant candidate predictors and possible interactions" in advance, to count interactions in the limiting sample size, and to "use a global test and either keep all or delete all interactions" ([Harrell, RMS ch. 4](https://hbiostat.org/rmsc/multivar)). The Riley criteria take "the number of candidate predictor parameters" as input, not the number of predictors ([pmsampsize manual](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pmsampsize/refman/pmsampsize.html)). In simulations of treatment-effect models, unpenalised all-interaction models overestimated heterogeneity and were overfit "even with doubled sample sizes"; a constant-effect model did best when there were no true interactions, and penalised effect models did best when there were ([van Klaveren et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7497896)).

| Coding of curvature and taper | Geometry df | Total with recalibrated SCORE2 base (≈2) | Total with refitted SCORE2 factors (≈8) | Power if truth common, NCP 7.85 / 10.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shared slope (current) | 5 | 7 | 13 | 0.56 / 0.70 |
| × artery (3 levels) | 9 | 11 | 17 | 0.45 / 0.59 |
| × position (3 levels) | 9 | 11 | 17 | 0.45 / 0.59 |
| Artery + position, additive | 13 | 15 | 21 | 0.38 / 0.51 |
| × segment type (9 levels) | 21 | 23 | 29 | 0.30 / 0.41 |

The df totals count only person-level base terms (recalibrated SCORE2 or the refitted factors, plus log follow-up) and geometry. The segment dummies, log length and dominance × vessel terms come on top. Power is for the whole block test at α = 0.05; NCP 7.85 is the noncentrality that gives a 1-df test 80% power [own calc]. The "additive" row is a project construct with no source: β_j = β + α_artery + γ_position with sum-to-zero constraints, 1 + 2 + 2 df per feature [derivation].

Two points follow from the table. **Which budget binds depends on the open base decision.** With the refitted SCORE2 factors, even the 4-df artery interaction puts the person-level model above ~15. With recalibrated SCORE2 it fits, and the additive coding lands exactly on the limit. Neither base leaves room for 9-segment slopes. The person-level budget is also arguably the wrong yardstick for segment-varying slopes, because those are identified mostly from within-person allocation, where the segment-level budget is larger (an on-disk estimate is 35–60 df). Even so, the person-level budget is the one that governs any claim about who develops plaque [on-disk, `~/Desktop/Speciale/CGPS_statistics-/reports/Segment plaque risk model nonlinearity.md`].

**The power trade is a bet on the shape of the heterogeneity.** With local information I_j per segment type and true slopes β_j, the shared 1-df test has noncentrality (Σ I_j β_j)² / Σ I_j, while the k-df segment-specific test has Σ I_j β_j² [derivation]. The k-df noncentrality is never smaller, but the k-df test has a higher critical value. When the effect sits in only one of three equally informative arteries, the shared test has power 0.47 against 0.78 for the 3-df artery-specific test [own calc]. When curvature is protective in one artery and harmful in another, the shared noncentrality can approach zero. When the effect is common, the shared test wins by the margins in the table. With no published segment-specific effect sizes and conflicting tortuosity signs, there is no evidence-based reason to bet on concentration. The bet also has to be placed before outcomes are seen.

Tests *of* the heterogeneity are weaker still. Gelman's rule of thumb is that estimating an interaction half the size of the main effect needs **16 times** the sample size ([Gelman 2018](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2018/03/15/need16/)). Brookes et al. found that interaction tests reached the power of the overall comparison only when the interaction was at least twice the overall effect ([Brookes et al. 2004](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/87531)). For the 4-df curvature/taper × artery test, power is 0.32 at heterogeneity noncentrality 4 and 0.60 at 8; the 16-df segment test has 0.17 and 0.35 [own calc]. A non-significant heterogeneity test is therefore **not evidence of a common slope**, and it has to be reported with interval estimates.

## Segment-specific slopes answer "where", not "who"

In the pooled model, person risk depends on the segments only through S_i = Σ_j exp(η_ij), and log S_i behaves like a smoothed maximum of the segment linear predictors. A covariate that acts in one segment type moves a person's hazard only by that segment's share of S_i: a segment HR of 1.3 on a 0.3 hazard share multiplies the person hazard by about 1.09 [on-disk derivation, `reports/Pooled segment GLMM background.md`]. The project's Fisher-information calculation makes the same point from the other side. For an effect confined to 2 of 9 segments, a person-level "any plaque" model has **0.042** of the segment model's information, against **0.672** for an effect on all segments, and 93.8% of the segment model's information on a 2-segment feature comes from *allocation* given the count [on-disk, same report]. A project simulation showed a segment-level LR test rejecting on allocation in 12 of 12 replicates while the person-level null was true in all of them [on-disk, same report].

Artery- or position-specific slopes concentrate geometry's effect on fewer segments, so they push the model further towards within-person allocation [derivation]. In a who-only thesis this matters twice. First, a significant 9- or 21-df block is harder to read as "geometry predicts who" than a shared 5-df block. Second, heterogeneity that averages out over a person's nine segments leaves Σ_j w_ij·HR_ij almost unchanged, so it is unlikely to move person-level ΔC even when it is real [derivation]. The shared slope over all trunk segments is the coding closest to a person-level effect. That is a reason to keep it as the confirmatory coding, separate from parsimony. The Mundlak within/between split answers a different question (confounding of the slope by person-level traits, not heterogeneity across segment types) and can be added independently at +1 df per segment-varying feature.

## Within-type z-scoring already builds in one kind of heterogeneity

Curvature and taper are z-scored within segment type, z_ij = (x_ij − m_j)/s_j, so one shared coefficient β on z implies a raw-unit log hazard ratio of **β/s_j** in segment type j [derivation]. A shared z-coefficient is therefore not "no heterogeneity". It encodes the specific assumption of an equal effect per within-type SD, and types with a small raw spread get larger per-unit effects. Centring by m_j changes nothing once segment dummies are in the model, but scaling by s_j does. Shared raw-unit and shared per-SD slopes are two different 1-df hypotheses. Both are valid under the null that all β_j = 0, and they differ only in power, depending on which describes the truth better [derivation]. This choice is defensible on two grounds. Hartman's vessel-specific tertiles, the method that recovered an LAD effect hidden by absolute thresholds, are a crude form of the same within-type rescaling ([Hartman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8586146/)). And curvature and torsion distributions differ systematically by position, for example highest distally in the LAD and lowest mid-RCA ([CinC 2005](https://www.cinc.org/old/Proceedings/2005/pdf/0105.pdf)). The thesis should state the assumption explicitly and report the implied raw-unit slopes β/s_j next to the per-SD HR. Under person-split CV, m_j and s_j must come from the training persons, which `pt.zscore(d, t, train)` already does. Per-type scaling is also the cheapest answer to the scale problem that otherwise motivates segment-specific slopes.

Random slopes by segment type, `(0 + curvature | segment type)`, look like a compromise but fail here. A variance from 9 levels is weakly identified, and from 3 levels it is essentially unidentified. The GLMM FAQ asks for "more than 5 or 6 at a minimum" and warns of "zero variance estimates" ([Bolker, GLMM FAQ](https://bbolker.github.io/mixedmodels-misc/glmmFAQ.html)), and multilevel texts advise against fewer than about 10 clusters ([Austin 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2949382)). A maximum-likelihood fit that lands on a singular (zero) variance silently collapses to the shared slope, and one that does not gives something close to full interaction, so it amounts to a hidden, outcome-driven model choice [derivation]. A random slope crossed with the person intercept also adds a second random-effect term, so the single-intercept Gauss–Hermite integration in `progression_who_pooled.py` no longer applies. Ridge on the deviations β_j − β̄, a normal hierarchical prior and an estimated random slope are the same model family. They are good *estimators* of heterogeneity: Dixon–Simon exchangeable interactions ([beanz docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/beanz/html/beanz-package.html)) and Bayesian subgroup shrinkage ([Jones et al. 2011](https://read.qxmd.com/read/21282293/bayesian-models-for-subgroup-analysis-in-clinical-trials)) both apply. But none of them gives an LR statistic with a known χ² reference, and Harrell advises against penalising the variable of interest when estimating its effect ([Harrell, RMS ch. 4](https://hbiostat.org/rmsc/multivar)).

## A three-tier plan fixes all choices before outcomes are seen

| Tier | Model | df beyond base | Role | What is reported |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Shared β for curvature and taper (z within type); LM angle on pLAD/pLCx rows; LM→LAD ratio on LAD rows, LM→LCx ratio on LCx rows | 5 | The single prespecified LR test of the geometry block | LR, df, p; per-SD HR with raw-unit β/s_j; ΔC and calibration estimated, not tested |
| Secondary | Primary + curvature × artery + taper × artery (sum-to-zero deviations) | +4 (joint) | One prespecified heterogeneity LR test, reported as a finding, never used to choose the primary model | LR (4 df), p; artery-specific HRs with CIs; explicit power caveat |
| Descriptive | Slope per segment type for curvature and taper, partially pooled towards the shared slope (hierarchical half-normal/half-Cauchy prior on the SD, or ridge with prespecified λ) | effective, not nominal | Shows where the effect sits; hypothesis-generating | Forest plot of 9 shrunken HRs per feature; no p-values |

Artery is the better grouping for the secondary test. It has the clearest mechanistic rationale (RCA motion and the lack of an origin bifurcation, differing LAD/LCx/RCA flow and absolute WSS) and the only empirical signal, weak as it is ([Hartman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8586146/)). Position is partly handled already, because within-type scaling gives proximal, mid and distal segments their own scale. Testing both artery and position would double the multiplicity for a test with power around 0.3–0.6. The LM split by branch (a separate LM-angle coefficient on pLAD and on pLCx, +1 df) is a reasonable add-on to the secondary block if it is written into the plan now. Not recommended at any tier: unpenalised 9-segment slopes as confirmatory (21 df, power 0.30 at NCP 7.85, over budget with either base), ML random slopes by segment type, and lasso or fused-lasso selection of interactions. All of these reintroduce data-driven model choice.

**A discrepancy with the code.** `CGPS_data/progression_who_pooled.py` currently fits G02 = B2 + mean T5 curvature (z within type) + LM angle on the pLAD/pCX rows, a **2-df** block. It has no taper and no LM diameter ratios. It runs on the Medis trees with progression (higher grade at FU) as the outcome, not on the plaque-free tracer cohort with incident plaque. The 5-df primary block above is therefore the planned design. Thesis methods text must describe the 2-df block until the code changes. The LM angle is also z-scored across people rather than within segment type, which is consistent, since it is a person-level feature.

Several things remain unknown. The ~1,100 segment events and the ~100 events per segment type are project estimates, not counts. The power figures assume independent per-type information and ignore the correlation the random intercept induces. No source extends the Riley criteria to clustered models. And no study anchors expected per-segment effect sizes in plaque-free people over ~10 years. The heterogeneity test and the shrunken estimates therefore have to be read as exploratory whatever they show.

## Conclusion

The question looks like a modelling choice, but it really concerns which estimand the thesis tests. A shared per-SD slope over all trunk segments is the coding that best expresses a person-level "who" hypothesis. Segment-specific slopes estimate something closer to "where", at a df price the cohort cannot pay for a confirmatory test. Two features of the current design already provide most of what segment-specific weights would add. Within-type z-scoring gives each segment type its own raw-unit slope through 1/s_j at no df cost, and anatomical row restriction makes the LM features local by construction. What remains, artery-level departures from a common per-SD effect, is worth one honest 4-df test and a shrunken forest plot. A null result there means "not detectable at this sample size", not "absent". If the where question is brought back, the trade reverses: segment-specific slopes become central there, and the same three-tier structure should be redrawn with the conditional logistic model as the primary.
