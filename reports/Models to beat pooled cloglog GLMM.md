# Flexible challengers rarely beat the pooled GLMM

No model is likely to clearly beat the pooled segment-level cloglog GLMM as a person risk of who developed plaque in the CGPS (Herlev–Østerbro) plaque-free cohort. The best challengers are the ones that keep its skeleton and only make the segment linear predictor more flexible. Two such models are a neural network that adds a zero-initialised residual to the GLMM's linear predictor and a GAMM with penalised splines. Both keep the cloglog link, the person random intercept and the pooling step, so each nests the GLMM and any gain can be attributed. The pooling step needs to be stated exactly, because it is easy to get wrong. The code does **not** combine segment risks as the naive product 1 − ∏(1 − p_s). It integrates the person's unknown random intercept out of a sum of segment hazards: **P_i = 1 − ∫ exp(−e^b Σ_j e^{η_ij}) φ(b; 0, σ²) db** ([progression_who_pooled.py](../CGPS_data/progression_who_pooled.py)). Every challenger has to reproduce that step, or it will overstate person risk. The data limit what any challenger can gain. About **452 of 1,076** plaque-free participants with graded follow-up developed trunk plaque (**433 of 1,041** complete cases), and for the who question the person is the unit of information, not the roughly 9,400 segment rows. The plan expects geometry itself to add only **ΔC 0.00–0.02**, and an assumed standard error of the C statistic is about 0.016. A non-linear gain on top of that would most likely fall within cross-validation noise. Challengers therefore belong in a prespecified secondary, estimation-only analysis. They are judged on paired, person-level, out-of-fold log loss, Brier score, C and calibration, on the same person folds, and they never replace the one LR test of the geometry block.

## The benchmark is a frailty model whose person risk integrates over the person

The decided who engine is a single cloglog GLMM over the nine trunk segments: proximal, mid and distal LAD, LCx and RCA. The left main and side branches are left out ([02b_segment_split.tex](../thesis/methodology/02b_segment_split.tex)). Each person has a normal random intercept, and the model is fitted by Gauss–Hermite quadrature with L-BFGS-B ([progression_who_pooled.py](../CGPS_data/progression_who_pooled.py)). Under the cloglog link the model is a grouped-data proportional hazards model, so exp(β) is a hazard ratio, and the random intercept is a lognormal shared frailty ([06_segment_glmm.tex](../thesis/technical_background/06_segment_glmm.tex)). One consequence matters for every challenger. **Person risk depends on the segments only through S_i = Σ_j exp(η_ij)**, the sum of the segment hazards. The code computes the person risk as `1 − Σ_k w_k exp(−e^{u_k} S_i)` over 40 Gauss–Hermite nodes. The segment-level marginal risks are used only for a per-segment AUC.

The plan rejects two shortcuts, and both are tempting in a flexible model. The first is the naive product 1 − ∏(1 − p̄_ij) of marginal segment risks. It ignores that segments in one person share a frailty, so it **always overestimates person risk**. In the plan's worked example, nine segments with σ² between 1.2 and 1.8 give about **0.63–0.69 instead of 0.49**. The second shortcut sets b = 0, which predicts the median person rather than the average one: about **0.45 instead of 0.49**. The C statistic does not detect this second error, so a person-level calibration plot is required (`~/Desktop/Speciale/CGPS_statistics-/experiments/who/pooled_score2/pooling.md`). External work agrees. Pavlou et al. found that marginal risks "are often obtained, incorrectly, using only estimates of the fixed effects". In their simulations, b = 0 predictions had a calibration slope of 0.788 at an ICC of 0.30, against about 0.98 for integrated predictions ([Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751/)). A held-out person is a new cluster. Integrating over b is the only valid out-of-fold prediction, because plugging in an estimated b for that person would leak their outcome.

The GLMM's efficiency rests on five assumptions:

- segments are independent given one b_i;
- σ is constant;
- b is normally distributed;
- S_i sums exactly the segments that define the outcome;
- b is independent of the covariates.

These come from the earlier background report ([Pooled segment GLMM background](Pooled%20segment%20GLMM%20background.md)). Four of the five are about how segments depend on each other, not about how geometry shapes risk. A challenger can therefore improve on the GLMM in two separate ways. It can use a more flexible η_ij, which is where neural networks and splines act. Or it can model the dependence between segments differently, through a gamma or nested frailty, which no neural network addresses.

The benchmark has not been fitted on the plaque-free cohort yet. The only implementation runs on the Medis trees with a progression outcome and baseline grade as a covariate, and its geometry is curvature (mean turning angle) and the LM bifurcation angle. It has no taper and no LM diameter ratios ([progression_who_pooled.py](../CGPS_data/progression_who_pooled.py)). The clean-cohort base model B2 is marked "Not run" in the plan, and `CGPS_data/thesis_final/` holds no pooled-model script. Four design choices also have to be settled before the benchmark exists:

- **Base.** Recalibrated SCORE2 or the refitted SCORE2 factors.
- **Follow-up time.** Log follow-up as an offset or as a free covariate. It is still open in [02_population_outcome.tex](../thesis/methodology/02_population_outcome.tex).
- **LM terms.** In the code they are coded only on the pLAD and pLCx rows. The plan warns that this turns about **94% of the "who" test's information into allocation**, i.e. which segment rather than who.
- **Missing geometry.** How missing baseline geometry is handled.

Running a challenger before these are fixed would compare it against a moving target.

## Four hundred and fifty people, not ten thousand rows, set the ceiling

The cohort numbers come from the files only. Of 1,801 participants, **1,077** were plaque-free at baseline. Of these, **483 (44.8%)** developed plaque anywhere and **452** developed it in a trunk segment. The trunk count is 42.0% of the 1,076 participants with graded follow-up. The trunk events are dominated by **minimal** grades: 261, or 57.7% ([00_cohort.tex](../thesis/results/00_cohort.tex)). The complete-case set has **1,041 people and 433 events** ([score2_honesty.md](../CGPS_data/score2_honesty.md)). Several numbers are not yet available: the segment-level event count on the new table, the Riley P_max, the image-quality exclusions and the follow-up distribution. They are left blank here rather than guessed.

The base model already discriminates moderately. In the person-level `score2_honesty` run, with 5 folds × 20 repeats, recalibrated SCORE2 reached an **out-of-fold C of 0.687**, with calibration slope 0.972 and log loss 0.6284. The nine refitted factors reached **0.686**, but with an apparent C of 0.699 and a **calibration slope of 0.866** ([score2_honesty.md](../CGPS_data/score2_honesty.md)). That run is person-level and is explicitly not the base decision. It still shows something that matters for challengers: even nine linear parameters overfit visibly at this sample size. The thesis budget assumes a C of about 0.70 under the Riley criteria and enters all five geometry terms linearly. It states that "a spline on every term would have needed [..] degrees of freedom, more than the model supports" ([03_model_size.tex](../thesis/methodology/03_model_size.tex)). The plan's working figure is **about 15–20 parameters**.

Neural networks and tree ensembles need far more data than this to be stable. In van der Ploeg et al.'s simulations, logistic regression stabilised at roughly 20–50 events per variable. Random forests, support vector machines and neural networks were still unstable above 200 ([van der Ploeg et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4289553)). Austin et al. reached the same conclusion for calibration. Penalised logistic regression was the least data-hungry, boosting was intermediate, and bagged trees and forests were the most data-hungry ([Austin, Lee & Wang 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539735)). The nine segment rows per person do not escape this limit. For person-level inputs (the SCORE2 factors, the LM angle and the two LM ratios) the information is bounded by the number of people. The rows mostly add information about **where** in a person plaque appears, not **who** develops it. By the standard design-effect formula, 1 + (m − 1)·ICC with m = 9 gives 1.8, 3.4 and 5.0 at ICC 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5 (own arithmetic). Even for segment-varying terms, the rows are therefore worth far fewer independent observations than their count suggests. The ICC for incident coronary plaque has not been published. It can be read from the fitted model as σ²/(σ² + π²/6) on the cloglog latent scale.

## The clinical case for non-linearity is mechanistic, not empirical

The clinical background v2 gives the reason to look for non-linear effects at all. Plaque forms and grows preferentially where wall shear stress is low or oscillatory. In a bend, the fastest blood is carried to the outer wall, which leaves low shear on the inner wall. In a coronary bend model, the inner-wall mean shear was about half the outer-wall value, and disturbed flow "grows stronger the tighter the bend and the higher the flow rate". At bifurcations, disturbed flow lies along the lateral walls, and its extent depends mainly on the share of parent flow entering the side branch ([03_wall_shear_stress.tex](../thesis/clinical_background_v2/03_wall_shear_stress.tex)). Dominance changes artery size: the proximal RCA is 3.9 mm in right dominance against 2.8 mm otherwise, and the LCx is 3.4 mm against 4.2 mm ([01_heart_anatomy.tex](../thesis/clinical_background_v2/01_heart_anatomy.tex)).

These facts suggest four specific shapes and interactions, each with a clear prior:

- **Curvature** acts through two opposing pathways: low inner-wall shear against protective helical flow. This argues for a two-sided curve.
- **Taper** would plausibly saturate.
- **Curvature × calibre or flow** is the Dean-type idea from the bend physics.
- The **LM angle and diameter ratios** are expected to act monotonically and possibly one-sidedly.

The shapes come from the project's non-linearity report (`~/Desktop/Speciale/CGPS_statistics-/reports/Segment plaque risk model nonlinearity.md`). Idealised simulations suggest that curvature and taper superpose rather than interact: curvature places the low shear and taper scales its level ([Taper and curvature interaction for WSS](Taper%20and%20curvature%20interaction%20for%20WSS.md)).

All of this evidence is mechanistic: CFD and idealised models. **No human study has tested the shape of any of the four geometry–plaque relations**, and none has fitted a taper × curvature term against plaque. Measurement noise works against detecting real shapes. Unsigned turning angle has a positive noise floor that depends on image quality, and therefore on BMI and heart rate. That noise flattens a true curvature shape toward linear. So a flexible model has specific shapes to look for, but the prior expectation that it will find them at this n is low. The project's own forecast is that "splines and linear terms give almost the same predictions".

## A neural network can nest the GLMM, and that is its only fair form

A neural network fits this question fairly only if it keeps three things: the cloglog likelihood on the segments, the person frailty, and the integrated pooling. The design that keeps all three is a **neural-residual cloglog GLMM**, here called model A:

**η_ij = x_ij′β + g_θ(x_ij, z_i, e_seg) + b_i**

- x_ij′β is the current linear predictor.
- g_θ is a small MLP with one hidden layer of 8–16 units. Its inputs are the segment's geometry, the person's SCORE2 inputs z_i and a learned embedding of segment position e_seg.
- The output weights of g_θ are initialised at zero.

The loss is the exact marginal likelihood per person: a 20–40-node Gauss–Hermite sum over b inside the loss, which is one-dimensional, differentiable and cheap. Person risk is computed by the same integral the GLMM uses. The design follows CANN ("Combined Actuarial Neural Network"). There, the GLM's linear predictor enters through a skip connection and the network learns only a correction, starting "exactly at the GLM" because its output weights begin at zero ([Wüthrich & Merz 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/66E8BEC373B5CCEF3BF3303D442D6B75/S0515036118000429a.pdf/editorial-yes-we-cann.pdf); [Wüthrich 2019 slides](https://www.zhaw.ch/storage/engineering/institute-zentren/iamp/upload/AI_conference_2019/Wuthrich_2019.pdf)). Holvoet et al. used the same construction to detect missing interactions in insurance data ([arXiv 2310.12671](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12671), **preprint**). The construction is close in spirit to DeepGLMM, which places a feed-forward network inside a GLMM and fits it variationally ([Tran et al., arXiv 1805.10157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.10157); journal venue not verified).

Because g ≡ 0 is exactly the GLMM, model A has three useful properties. Training starts at the benchmark. Heavy weight decay simply returns the benchmark. And the out-of-fold difference in person log-likelihood can be read directly as a "non-linearity gain". Weight decay and early stopping are tuned in inner folds grouped by person, and an ensemble of 5–10 seeds averages over initialisation. The whole model is about 150 lines of PyTorch, with nodes from `numpy.polynomial.hermite.hermgauss`.

A **neural additive version** (model B) restricts g to Σ_k g_k(x_k), one tiny network per geometry feature. Each feature's effect then stays visible as a shape function, which suits a thesis that asks which geometry matters. Neural Additive Models "perform similarly to existing state-of-the-art generalized additive models" ([Agarwal et al., NeurIPS 2021](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2021/hash/251bd0442dfcc53b5a761e050f8022b8-Abstract.html)). They do not outperform those models, so at around 1,000 people a spline GLMM will very likely match model B.

Graph or attention networks over the nine segments are a poor fit here. With nine fixed, named segments, permutation-invariant set pooling is the wrong structure, because segment identity is part of the base model. Message passing across nine nodes mainly learns dependence between segments within a person, which the random intercept already models as exchangeable. Segment-level neural models also face a structural limit. Most of the information in a segment model is about allocation within a person: the plan puts it at **93.8%** for local features coded on proximal rows ([Pooled segment GLMM background](Pooled%20segment%20GLMM%20background.md)). A flexible segment model will therefore mainly improve within-person ranking, which answers **where**, not **who**.

The published mixed-effects network packages are built for a different problem.

| Package | Limitation here |
|---|---|
| LMMNN | Its author calls binary classification a proof of concept ([GitHub issue #14](https://github.com/gsimchoni/lmmnn/issues/14)). |
| GLMMNet | Targets a high-cardinality categorical effect ([arXiv 2301.12710](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.12710)). |
| ARMED | Reports 2–9% relative gains on unseen clusters, against conventional models rather than a well-specified GLMM ([PMC10644386](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10644386/)). |
| MC-GMENN | Notes that earlier mixed-effects networks were "limited to regression and binary targets with only one clustering feature" ([IJCAI 2024](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/555)). |

None of these has a cloglog link. Several predict an unseen cluster with u = 0, which is the median-person error described above. No head-to-head against a GLMM with about 1,000 clusters of size 9 and a binary outcome was found.

A **person-level TabPFN** (model C) is useful only as a ceiling check, and it does not meet the brief. TabPFN v2 reports strong performance on tables of up to 10,000 rows ([Hollmann et al., Nature 2025](https://uni-freiburg.de/en/new-ai-model-tabpfn-enables-faster-and-more-accurate-predictions-on-small-tabular-data-sets/)), and v2.5 is described in a non-peer-reviewed technical report ([arXiv 2511.08667](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.08667)). But it treats rows as exchangeable and has no frailty or cloglog link. Fitted on segment rows, its probabilities could only be combined by the naive product. So it has to be fitted with one row per person, which drops the segment outcomes. Its role is diagnostic: if a black-box person-level learner cannot beat the pooled GLMM out of fold, a tuned segment network very likely cannot either.

## Non-neural challengers are cheaper and keep the test valid

The cheapest flexible model is the GLMM itself with **3-knot restricted cubic splines** on curvature, taper and the LM angle (+1 df each) and one curvature × taper product (+1 df). That is about 4 extra df, all fitted with the same likelihood. In `glmer`, adaptive quadrature works unchanged for a single scalar random effect ([glmer docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/lme4/html/glmer.html)). The model stays nested, so a chunk LR test of the non-linear terms is valid as a separate, prespecified secondary test, but only if the primary model stays linear whatever the result. Even 4 extra df may go beyond the Riley budget, so this model is a sensitivity analysis.

A **GAMM** in mgcv swaps these fixed splines for penalised smooths whose smoothness REML chooses. A person random intercept is just another smooth: `s(id, bs="re")` gives i.i.d. normal coefficients, "equivalent to an identity penalty matrix" ([mgcv docs](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/mgcv/html/smooth.construct.re.smooth.spec.html)). Within the budget, the smooths must be few and shared across segments: low k, at most one `ti()` tensor term, and no `by=segment` smooths, which would cost about 36 df. For prediction, the `s(id)` term is excluded and b is integrated over N(0, σ̂²) as before. Two drawbacks remain. The p-values for penalised smooths are approximate, and `gamm`'s PQL fit is biased for binary data in small clusters. So the GAMM is a predictive comparator, not a source of tests.

A **Bayesian cloglog GLMM** in brms covers the same ground with honest uncertainty. brms supports `bernoulli("cloglog")` ([brms families](https://paulbuerkner.com/brms/reference/brmsfamily.html)) and predicts for new persons with `sample_new_levels = "gaussian"` ([prepare_predictions](https://paulbuerkner.com/brms/reference/prepare_predictions.html)). It can add splines, a shrinkage prior on the geometry block, and geometry × segment slopes partially pooled over the nine positions. A slope that varies by person is not realistic here, because each person has at most nine segments and usually 0–2 events.

**GPBoost** is the strongest tree-based mixed model. It supports grouped random effects but only the `bernoulli_logit` and `bernoulli_probit` likelihoods; there is no cloglog ([GPBoost parameters](https://gpboost.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Main_parameters.html)). Its person risk must therefore be coded by hand as 1 − ∫∏_s[1 − F(f_is + b)]φ(b) db. Its broad benchmark is a **preprint** on continuous outcomes with 28,000 rows or more, which does not apply here ([Sigrist, arXiv 2307.02071](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.02071v1)). BiMM forest and GMERF report "similar or superior" or "comparable" performance to GLMMs, but only in their authors' own simulations ([Speiser et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169743918304362); [GMERF report](https://www.mate.polimi.it/biblioteca/add/qmox/36-2020.pdf)). Plain XGBoost on segment rows produces marginal segment risks whose naive product is biased upward. It would need a person-level recalibration fitted in the training folds, which restores calibration but not the ranking.

The last option changes the dependence between segments rather than the shape of η. A **gamma frailty** has the same number of parameters as the current model and a closed-form person risk, **P_i = 1 − (1 + θS_i)^(−1/θ)** ([arXiv 1403.6744](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.6744)). It is the cheapest check of whether the person calibration depends on the normal-frailty assumption. A **nested person + vessel frailty** adds one variance parameter but is weakly identified with 2–3 segments per vessel and rare events.

| Challenger | Keeps cloglog + integrated b | Extra df | Nested (LR possible) | Realism here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GLMM + RCS + curvature×taper | Yes | ~4 | Yes | High |
| GAMM (shared smooths, mgcv) | Yes | edf ~4–15 | Approximate | Moderate |
| Bayesian brms (splines, shrinkage, segment slopes) | Yes | many, shrunk | No (LOO) | Moderate, slow |
| A. Neural-residual cloglog GLMM | Yes (custom) | tuned net | No | Moderate; shrinks to GLMM |
| B. Neural additive cloglog GLMM | Yes (custom) | tuned subnets | No | Moderate; ≈ spline GLMM |
| Gamma / nested frailty | Yes | 0 / 1 | Yes | High / weak |
| GPBoost (logit/probit, shallow) | No (hand-coded integral) | tuned ensemble | No | Moderate |
| C. Person-level TabPFN | No (no segment outcomes) | none tuned | No | Ceiling check only |

## A fair comparison is paired, person-level and frozen in advance

The analysis would be split into three tiers:

1. **Primary (confirmatory).** The one LR test of the 5-df linear geometry block in the pooled GLMM. Nothing in this report changes it.
2. **Secondary (prespecified, estimation only).** A fixed list of challengers: the spline GLMM, one GAMM, model A and GPBoost.
3. **Exploratory.** Everything else, including profile-based or graph networks, labelled as hypothesis-generating.

The secondary tier rests on five rules.

**Same data.** Every model gets the same persons (or the same imputation), the same person outcome (any trunk plaque, minimal included) and the same base. The recalibrated-versus-refitted SCORE2 question must be settled in the pooled GLMM first, and the chosen base then reused by every challenger.

**Same folds.** The fold files are the plan's 10 person-grouped folds × 50 repeats, stratified on the person outcome, with recorded seeds. The CV text still has a red `\clarify` on folds and repeats ([11_cross_validation.tex](../thesis/methodology/11_cross_validation.tex)), and `score2_honesty` used 5 × 20, so the thesis text and the code must be aligned before running. Everything learned from data happens inside the training fold: z-constants, knots, imputation, σ̂², SCORE2 recalibration, and every hyperparameter. Hyperparameters are tuned by inner person-grouped CV on a small grid fixed in advance. Using the same CV for tuning and error estimation is optimistically biased; nested CV is nearly unbiased ([Varma & Simon 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1397873)).

**Same prediction.** For every model, the held-out person risk is the integrated marginal risk. One correction is needed here: the evaluation note drafted for this report proposed pooling 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) and predicting with b = 0, and **both are wrong for this model**.

**Metrics.** Discrimination and calibration are measured per person, never per segment, because segment AUC mixes who and where. The primary contrast is **Δ out-of-fold log loss**, the CV analogue of the LR statistic, together with Brier and scaled Brier. Alongside these come **ΔC**, the calibration intercept and slope, and a smoothed calibration curve, with person-level calibration also shown by the number of evaluable segments. Intervals come from a person-cluster bootstrap. ΔC is estimated and not tested: DeLong tests are invalid for nested models ([Pepe et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625503)). NRI is left out because its tests had false-positive rates up to 63% for null markers ([Pepe et al. 2014](https://edrn.cancer.gov/data-and-resources/publications/24681599-2296-net-risk-reclassification-p-values-valid-or-misleading)). Each challenger is compared with **both** the base B and the geometry GLMM G. Comparing it with G isolates the value of non-linearity. Comparing it with B alone would credit the network with what linear geometry already delivers.

**Stability.** The whole pipeline, including tuning, is re-run in at least 200 bootstrap samples that resample persons with all nine segments together. The report then shows the prediction instability plot and the mean absolute prediction error (MAPE) per person for every model, following Riley & Collins ([2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10952221/)). In their GUSTO-I example, a model built on 500 people kept a stable C of 0.82 while one person's predicted risk of 0.2 ranged from about 0 to 0.8 across bootstrap refits ([Riley et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10729337/)).

The decision rule should be written before outcome linkage, together with the tracer-method freeze. A challenger "adds" only if it is better on log loss and C, its interval excludes zero, its calibration is at least as good, and its MAPE is no worse. Otherwise the linear GLMM is retained. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI ([Collins et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11025451/)), and every model that was run is reported, not only the best.

The realistic expectation is a tie. In comparisons at low risk of bias, the difference in logit(AUC) between machine learning and logistic regression was **0.00 (−0.18 to 0.18)**. Apparent machine-learning gains came from comparisons at high risk of bias ([Christodoulou et al. 2019](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4167c905-5171-4fc7-b157-ff36d56a73db)). In PARADIGM, the closest coronary precedent, machine learning against logistic regression for rapid plaque progression gave **0.83 vs 0.81, P = 0.128** ([Han et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7335586)), and that was in a referral cohort with a different outcome. As a rough Hanley–McNeil estimate, the C statistic at about 450 events has a standard error near 0.016, and the paired ΔC has a somewhat smaller one. Gains below about 0.01–0.02 will therefore be indistinguishable from zero. That is the same size as the entire expected geometry effect. A large apparent gain for a flexible model should first prompt a check for leakage: a person split across folds, tuning outside the training fold, or an empirical-Bayes b.

## Conclusion

The question changes once the pooling is written out. The GLMM aggregates segments through a sum of hazards under a shared frailty that is integrated out, so a flexible model's only real room is in the shape of η_ij and in the dependence model. Neither is likely to move person-level discrimination much, because most of the information in segment-level flexibility is about where plaque appears, not who develops it. If non-linearity matters at all, it should show first as a better within-person ranking in an exploratory where-type check. The most useful challengers are therefore not the most flexible ones. They are the nested ones: the spline GLMM, the gamma frailty and the zero-initialised neural residual. A null result from them is informative, because it bounds how much structure the linear model leaves behind.

Two practical points follow. First, the benchmark does not exist on the plaque-free cohort yet. The fixed-effect design (base, follow-up term, LM coding, missing geometry) has to be settled and the GLMM fitted before any challenger can be run. Second, the neural network is best presented in the thesis as a robustness check on the linearity assumption, not as a competing model. A tie on held-out log loss and calibration would support the decision to enter geometry linearly. It would not say that geometry is unimportant.
