# Add diameter and angle, skip torsion

Model 2's ordered local tortuosity vector should be expanded, but only along one axis: add **luminal diameter** as a genuine second per-point channel, fold **bifurcation angle** in as a small number of event-based scalar covariates rather than a dense per-point channel, and leave **torsion out of the primary design**. This is not the conclusion the thesis's own anchor paper appears to license at first glance; Tello Ayala et al.'s transformer-versus-logistic-regression result (AUROC 0.67 vs 0.60) is evidence that reading a full profile beats collapsing it to a mean, a completely different design axis from how many channels that profile should carry, and no study anywhere in cardiology tests the channel-count question directly ([PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)). The recommendation instead rests on three independent lines of evidence that agree with each other: diameter is the one candidate channel with direct RCA-specific outcome evidence as an independent predictor, bifurcation angle is anatomically too sparse along the RCA's single trunk to serve as a dense per-point feature the way it plausibly could on the branch-rich LCA, and torsion is both the least outcome-validated candidate in the literature and the most numerically fragile to compute from an automated centerline. Architecturally, going from one to two or three channels costs a small network almost nothing in parameter count for the RNN/transformer families under consideration, so the overfitting risk argument that would justify staying curvature-only on parameter-budget grounds alone does not hold; the real risk is redundancy and RCA-specific confounding (chiefly coronary dominance), not raw parameter growth. Wherever this recommendation goes beyond what a cited study measured, that gap is flagged explicitly below, because the honest state of the literature is that this exact ablation, curvature-only versus multi-channel local profile for coronary CAD, has never been run.

## Tello Ayala's result licenses depth of one channel, not breadth across several

The single most important thing to get right before designing Model 2's input is what the 0.67-vs-0.60 AUROC gap in Tello Ayala et al. actually isolates. Both sides of that comparison use exactly one geometric domain, discrete curvature; the transformer wins by preserving where along the vessel a bend sits, while the logistic regression collapses the same information to its patient-level mean ([PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)). That is a profile-versus-scalar effect, and it is the correct justification for Model 2's sequence architecture in the first place, but it says nothing about whether a second, third, or fourth channel sampled at the same points would help, hurt, or do nothing. No source found in this research pass runs the specific three-way comparison that would answer the channel-count question cleanly: multivariable regression on several channels' summaries, sequence model on one channel's full profile, and sequence model on several channels' full profiles together. Citing Tello Ayala as support for channel expansion, without that caveat, would be a category error the notes explicitly warn against. The closest thing to a controlled channel-count ablation for coronary-adjacent geometry comes from a non-sequence random forest on carotid vessel morphology, which combined 13 curvature- and torsion-derived features and found Macro-F1 rising from 0.815 (one feature) to 0.921 (six features, after pruning seven redundant ones via a Spearman correlation cutoff of 0.95), but with a **non-monotonic** ceiling: backward ablation found the true optimum at just three features for the binary task (Macro-F1 0.926, better than the six-feature baseline) and five for the ternary task, meaning added channels stopped helping, and briefly reversed, past a small handful ([arXiv 2607.14195](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14195)). That pattern, real early gains from complementary channels, a low ceiling, and a penalty for redundant ones, is the best available proxy for what to expect from expanding Model 2's vector, and it argues for choosing channels deliberately rather than concatenating every available descriptor.

## The RCA's anatomy makes diameter the strongest addition and angle a structurally sparse one

Each candidate channel has to be evaluated against what the RCA specifically looks like, not against vascular geometry in general, and the four candidates are not equally well suited to a dense per-point representation. Diameter has the strongest direct RCA evidence: in an RCA-specific FFR-CT vessel-morphology study, RCAs with reduced functional flow (FFRCT ≤ 0.80) had smaller proximal diameter (3.9 ± 0.8 mm vs 4.6 ± 1.0 mm, p < 0.0001), smaller distal diameter (2.4 ± 0.5 mm vs 2.9 ± 0.6 mm, p < 0.0001), and a volume-to-length ratio that remained the strongest independent multivariable predictor (β = 0.48, p = 0.03), with proximal diameter itself also independently significant (β = 0.09, p = 0.04) ([PMC10873436](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10873436/)). Notably, that study used diameter as a standalone predictive feature rather than as a normalizing denominator for curvature, which is the same role Model 2 would give it as a second channel. Bifurcation angle is a different story specifically for the RCA. The RCA's named branches (conal, SA-nodal, atrial, acute marginal, and the terminal crux/PDA split) are concentrated at a small, fixed number of landmarks along an otherwise single trunk, in sharp contrast to the LCA, where a cadaveric study found bifurcation in 60.0% of hearts, trifurcation in 26.7%, and tetra- or pentafurcation in 6.7% each, essentially guaranteeing a branch point near the ostium ([Kenhub](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/anatomy/right-coronary-artery); [cadaveric dominance/branching study](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/17233)). Standardized CT protocols reflect this asymmetry directly, placing only three caliber-measurement stations along the entire RCA and defining its length in three segments bounded by just two internal landmarks ([Zhu et al., PMC2759354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)). A dense per-point bifurcation-angle channel sampled at the same arclength resolution as curvature would therefore be undefined or zero-padded at the overwhelming majority of RCA points, an event-sparse feature dressed up as a dense one. This sparsity is compounded by a genuine RCA-specific confound the LAD does not share: coronary dominance. Roughly 80-85% of hearts are right-dominant, 7-13% left-dominant, and 2-5% codominant, and dominance measurably changes RCA length, terminal branch topology, and where the vessel's identity changes near the crux; one cadaveric study found RCA length in the 8-14 cm range was dominant in 65% of cases, and dominance is independently documented as a confounder for other angle- and function-based coronary measurements ([PMC13363422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363422/); [RCA length/dominance table](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Right-coronary-artery-length-and-dominance_tbl1_320765447); [dominance-as-confound synthesis](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/17233)). Any terminal-segment feature, whether angle, curvature, or torsion, computed near the crux will covary with dominance for reasons unrelated to disease, which means dominance class needs to be a companion covariate wherever a terminal-RCA feature is used, exactly as age and sex already are. By contrast, there is direct RCA-specific evidence that a *single*, well-defined angle carries CAD signal: the RCA-to-aorta ostial takeoff angle was significantly smaller in a CAD group (79.07° ± 24.88°) than in a normal group (92.08° ± 19.51°, p = 0.001), with excellent inter-observer reproducibility (ICC = 0.999) ([PMC9917625](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9917625/)). That is a scalar covariate at a single anatomically fixed site, not a per-point profile entry, and it is the kind of angle feature the RCA's anatomy actually supports.

Torsion sits in the weakest position of the four candidates on two independent grounds. First, no RCA-specific or even general CT-coronary study was found reporting a quantitative reliability or reproducibility comparison between torsion and curvature computed from an automated, voxel-resolution centerline; the consistent qualitative pattern across the broader vessel-geometry literature is that torsion, requiring a third derivative of the centerline coordinates, is markedly more noise-sensitive than curvature, to the point that dedicated discrete-geometry and smoothing techniques exist specifically to stabilize its estimation ([discrete-geometry torsion stabilization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.04215); [hybrid morphology classification framework](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14195)). None of the studies using that stabilization worked from an automated segmentation-derived centerline comparable to this thesis's own pipeline; all used smoother, semi-manual or biplane-cineangiography reconstructions, so torsion computed from this project's centerlines could be noisier in practice than any published number would suggest. Second, on outcome evidence specifically, the one paper that reports RCA torsion values by segment (Zhu et al., 35 RCA vessels, angiographically *normal*, from biplane cineangiography) is a geometry-cataloguing study, not a disease-association study: it found a U-shaped along-course pattern (torsion lowest in the mid-segment) and torsion more than 30% higher in the LAD than the RCA overall (p < 0.0001), but never tested torsion against CAD presence ([PMC2759354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759354/)). No RCA-specific study linking torsion to CAD or stenosis was found anywhere in this search.

## Small networks absorb extra channels cheaply, but the sample-size floor is unmeasured, not zero

If the concern behind staying curvature-only is that a small network cannot support more inputs on a modest cohort, the architecture mechanics do not support that concern in the form usually assumed. In the RNN/GRU/LSTM and transformer families most plausible for Model 2, the number of input channels only multiplies the size of the first weight matrix (the input-to-hidden block, or the linear input embedding); moving from one to three or four channels adds a small fraction of the network's total parameter count, which is dominated by the recurrent or attention weights that do not depend on input width at all. A standard 1D-CNN is the one architecture where channel count multiplies the first convolutional layer's parameters directly, and even there, depthwise or grouped convolutions and channel dropout (Weighted Channel Dropout, spatial dropout) are established, low-cost techniques built specifically to stop channel-count growth from inflating overfitting risk, alongside simple pre-training correlation-based channel pruning as the cheapest safeguard of all ([WCD](https://liner.com/review/weighted-channel-dropout-for-regularization-deep-convolutional-neural-network); [multi-sensor 1D-CNN weight sharing survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03502)). So the "parameter budget" framing that would make classical-regression EPV logic transfer directly does not really apply here: no validated samples-per-parameter threshold exists for neural networks the way EPV ≥ 10-20 exists for logistic regression, and the double-descent literature shows raw parameter counts are a poor proxy for a network's effective capacity in the first place ([EPV extension work, PMC5045274](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045274/); [double descent / intrinsic dimension](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2019-03-14-overfit/)). What the literature does establish, and what matters more here than channel count, is that a cohort of a few hundred patients sits below where neural-network-family models have been shown to stabilize in the small handful of studies that measured this directly: a digital-mental-health benchmarking study found simple models (logistic regression, naive Bayes, SVM) converged around N ≈ 750, tree-based models around N ≈ 1,000, and neural networks required N ≈ 1,500, the highest of the families tested, with severe train/test gaps (up to 0.12 AUC) at N ≤ 300 and up to 70% of cross-validated results exceeding 0.61 AUC at N = 100 despite a genuinely useless underlying model ([PMC11655521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11655521/)). That threshold is about sample size in general, not specifically about how many channels the input carries, and TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST-AI, the reporting and risk-of-bias standards built for exactly this class of model, confirm there is no numeric channel-count rule to appeal to; they require the thesis to justify and report the sample size and predictor/channel count actually used, not to hit a fixed target ([TRIPOD+AI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11019967/); [TRIPOD-AI/PROBAST-AI protocol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8273461/)). The practical upshot is that expanding from one to two or three channels does not meaningfully move Model 2 further into overfitting risk than the cohort size already does on its own; the risk that channel expansion specifically adds is redundancy (correlated channels wasting model capacity and degrees of freedom) and RCA-specific confounding (dominance), both of which are addressed by pruning and covariate adjustment rather than by keeping the channel count at one.

## No published model has run this exact ablation, and the one close analog favors diameter and angle over torsion

Searching specifically for a channel-count ablation in a per-point sequence model predicting a coronary or vascular clinical outcome, curvature-only versus curvature-plus-others, returned nothing; this comparison has not been published for any vascular bed and any clinical outcome, coronary or otherwise ([direct-precedent search](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19030)). Multi-channel geometric profiles do exist in the literature, but almost entirely for predicting a physical or hemodynamic field (wall shear stress, pressure) rather than a disease label; a coronary WSS surrogate model combines vessel radius, bifurcation angles, and curvature as inputs, and a related geometric-deep-learning benchmark shows adding surface-normal and coordinate features measurably improves field-prediction accuracy, but neither targets a clinical outcome the way Model 2 does ([Frontiers WSS surrogate](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1360330/full); [GReAT benchmark](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19030)). The one genuine structural analog for a clinical-outcome sequence model reading multiple geometric channels per point comes from outside cardiology entirely: a retinal cardiovascular-risk framework's HVAAT module tokenizes each vessel segment as a four-channel vector, radius, bifurcation angle, segment length, and tortuosity, and passes the ordered sequence through a small transformer encoder (two layers, four heads, embedding size 64) to produce per-attribute interpretable risk components ([PMC13330239](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13330239/)). That paper's channel choice is informative precisely because of what it omits: torsion is absent, and every domain surveyed in this research, retinal, airway-tree, aneurysm, converges on the same short list when a multi-channel geometric profile is used for a learned model, radius/diameter, some angle measure, a length or position term, and a curvature-family quantity, with torsion essentially never appearing as a chosen channel anywhere outside classical differential-geometry feature engineering ([airway-tree COPD tree kernels](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1303.7390); [vessel centerline profile extraction](https://arxiv.org/html/1704.03743)). No paper reports an ablation isolating which of HVAAT's four attributes drives its performance, so this precedent establishes what practitioners choose to try, not what has been shown to work; but as a revealed preference across an entire literature, it lines up exactly with the RCA-feasibility analysis above, diameter and angle are the natural next channels, torsion is not.

## A concrete design: diameter as a channel, angle as covariates, torsion deferred, with correlation and dominance safeguards

Putting the three lines of evidence together yields a specific, implementable recommendation rather than a general lean. Add luminal diameter as a second per-point channel alongside curvature, using simple early-fusion concatenation into the same 1D-CNN, RNN, or transformer input; the two channels are already sampled at matched arclength positions along the same centerline, so the registration concern that makes early fusion risky in other multimodal settings does not apply here ([fusion-architecture review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10288577/)). Do not add bifurcation angle as a dense per-point channel; the RCA's anatomy makes it structurally sparse (only 2-4 named landmark sites on a typical single-trunk centerline, versus curvature's continuous definition at every point), so instead encode it as a small number of scalar covariates, most defensibly the RCA-to-aorta ostial takeoff angle, which already has direct RCA-specific CAD-association evidence ([PMC9917625](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9917625/)), fused at the top of the network alongside age and sex rather than injected into the sequence. Whatever terminal-segment feature is used, include coronary dominance class as a companion covariate, since it is derivable from whether the segmented tree shows RCA-to-PDA continuity and is a documented confound for exactly this anatomical region. Leave torsion out of the primary model: it lacks any RCA-specific or general coronary outcome-association evidence in either direction, and its known noise-sensitivity as a third-derivative quantity computed from an automated centerline is a real, currently unquantified risk rather than a theoretical one. If torsion is tested at all, it should be as an explicit, separately reported ablation arm, not folded silently into the default channel set, both because that is the only way to generate the evidence this literature search found missing everywhere, and because it keeps the reproducibility standard the project already holds itself to. Whichever channels are kept, apply the two safeguards the small-sample and multivariate-sequence literature both converge on: a correlation-based pruning pass before training (the Spearman-based elimination used in the carotid RF study is a directly transferable, near-zero-cost precedent) to catch any curvature-diameter redundancy before it wastes model capacity, and standard small-cohort regularization, early stopping, moderate dropout (including channel-level dropout if a third channel is added later), and nested or repeated cross-validation rather than a single held-out split, given that nested CV's bias correction is reported to matter most precisely in the sub-1,000-sample regime this project sits in ([nested CV for small samples, PMC10388213](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10388213/); [carotid RF correlation pruning, arXiv 2607.14195](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14195)).

## Conclusion

The literature does not contain a verdict on curvature-only versus multi-channel local vectors for coronary CAD prediction; it contains three separate, converging bodies of indirect evidence, RCA anatomy, small-network parameter mechanics, and revealed channel choices in the one genuine cross-domain analog, that all point the same direction without any of them directly measuring the effect. That convergence is what makes diameter-plus-angle-covariates a defensible design choice and torsion a deferred one, but it also means the thesis should present this design as its own contribution, not as a replication of established practice: no coronary or retinal study has published the curvature-only-vs-multi-channel ablation this thesis is positioned to run, and doing so with proper correlation pruning, dominance adjustment, and nested cross-validation would fill a gap the literature search confirms is genuinely open rather than merely under-cited. The one caution worth carrying forward past this report is that the biggest threat to Model 2's validity is not the channel-count decision at all; it is the same few-hundred-patient sample-size ceiling that already constrains Model 1, and no amount of careful channel selection changes the fact that neural-network-family models in the literature searched here have rarely been shown to stabilize below roughly a thousand samples.
