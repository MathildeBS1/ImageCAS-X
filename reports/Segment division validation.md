# Benchmark the segment rule against readers

The best available validation does not measure accuracy, because nothing in CGPS records where the visual reader put the segment boundaries. It measures **agreement with the reader's boundaries** in the Medis data, splits that agreement into the parts owed to the rule, to the tracer and to the outcome convention, and then shows whether the remaining disagreement changes the thesis result. In practice this means three comparisons, all in participants with plaque at baseline. (1) The rule on the Medis centerlines against the Medis reader labels isolates the rule. (2) The rule on the tracer arteries of the same participants, against the same labels, adds the tracer. (3) A plaque-contour check against the segments the visual analysis graded is the only step that touches the convention behind the outcome. Three changes would strengthen the current plan. The fractions should be fitted on all trees and judged by leave-one-out by person, not on a single random half. Results should be reported by boundary type (side-branch vs fixed fraction) and by dominance. Where possible, a second, blinded boundary placement on a subset of trees should supply a reader-vs-reader benchmark, since without one the millimetre figures cannot be judged large or small. Transport to the plaque-free cohort cannot be measured. It stays an explicit assumption, supported by label-free comparisons between the two populations and by a prespecified boundary-perturbation sensitivity analysis. The literature offers no direct precedent for any of these steps: no published study reports boundary distances in mm for coronary segment cuts, or checks a division against plaque location.

## The reference is a reader's choice, so report agreement next to a reader ceiling

When the reference standard is imperfect, the methodological literature recommends giving up the accuracy framework. The options are to build a composite reference, or to fall back on "clinical validation", meaning showing that the test serves its purpose ([Reitsma et al. 2009](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/a-review-of-solutions-for-diagnostic-accuracy-studies-with-an-imp/)). The Medis segment labels are exactly such a reference. Expert segment conventions differ even in the guidelines. In the ASCI-PT consensus, **only 64.7% of panellists agreed on the proximal–mid LAD end-point convention**, and these were panel votes, not measured reader agreement ([Kim et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10973734)). The SYNTAX/AHA definitions themselves fall back on fractions of length where no landmark exists: the proximal RCA runs to "one half the distance to the acute margin", and the mid LAD ends at half the distance from the first septal to the apex when its angle cannot be seen ([SYNTAX definitions](https://syntaxscore.org/index.php/tutorial/definitions/14-appendix-i-segment-definitions)). The thesis rule's fraction-based boundaries therefore follow the guidelines' own fallback, which makes them defensible on definitional grounds but not validated.

The closest precedent, and the template for interpretation, is Cao et al. 2017, from the Leiden group behind Medis. Their algorithm agreed with experts on **89.2% of segments in right-dominant and 83.6% in left-dominant trees**. They reported this next to the experts' agreement with each other, **97.6% and 87.6%**, and that inter-expert figure is the ceiling ([Cao et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5677991/)). Inter-expert overlap differed by only 1.0% (right-dominant) and 2.2% (left-dominant) on average. It was much worse in the LCx subtree, where one LPDA disagreement came from two different definitions rather than noise ([Cao et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5677991/)). Brandt et al. likewise set 94.4% algorithm overlap against 96.6% inter-reader overlap ([Brandt 2024](https://europepmc.org/search?query=DOI%3A%2210.1097%2Frti.0000000000000753%22)). The planned thesis design has no such ceiling. The strongest claim available without ground truth is that the rule disagrees with the reader about as much as a second reader would. That claim needs a second, blinded boundary placement on a random subset of Medis trees, for example 30–50 arteries per vessel. Without it, the mm figures should be presented as descriptive and called "boundary differences", not "errors". The reporting items of GRRAS for agreement studies apply: selection of trees, blinding, exclusions and confidence intervals ([Kottner et al. 2011](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/guidelines-for-reporting-reliability-and-agreement-studies-grras-/)).

Two properties of the reference weaken it further and should be stated rather than assumed away. First, how the Medis labels were produced is unknown. If they are QAngio's automatic labels, of the kind Cao et al. describe, and readers corrected them only near the contoured plaque, then boundaries far from plaque may be unchecked software output. In that case step 1 compares two algorithms. Second, agreement with the Medis readers' convention does not imply agreement with the CGPS visual reader who graded the outcome, since definitions differ between experts ([Cao et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5677991/)).

## Three comparisons separate rule, tracer and outcome convention

| Step | Applied to | Compared with | What it isolates | What it cannot show |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rule on Medis centerlines | Medis reader labels, same points | The rule alone | Tracer effects; the visual reader's convention |
| 1b | Rule on tracer arteries, mapped into Medis coordinates | Medis labels at the nearest point | Rule + tracer (the difference from step 1 is the tracer's part) | The visual reader's convention |
| 2 | Tracer division, with plaque contours placed by position | Segment graded with plaque in the visual analysis | Agreement with the outcome convention where plaque lies | Boundaries far from plaque; plaque-free arteries |

The current draft treats step 1 as validating the rule. The thesis, however, applies the rule to tracer arteries. Automatic centerlines lower labelling agreement consistently: Akinyemi reports **84.4% on manual vs 76.5% on automatically tracked centerlines** ([Akinyemi 2009](https://eurasip.org/Proceedings/Eusipco/Eusipco2009/contents/papers/1569187117.pdf)), and Yang reports 95/93% falling to 87/86% ([Yang et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254019616_Automatic_coronary_artery_tree_labeling_in_coronary_computed_tomographic_angiography_datasets)). Step 1b closes that gap at little cost, since the mapping into Medis coordinates already exists for the tracer–Medis comparison. It is the step most sensitive to the main tracer risk for fraction-based boundaries. If the tracer stops early or runs further distally than the reader did, every fraction-based cut shifts. Cao et al. show that a missed D1 or OM1 propagates downstream in the same way ([Cao et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5677991/)).

Step 2 deserves more weight than the draft gives it, and for a reason the draft does not state. The segment that "should contain the contour" is the segment graded with plaque in the visual analysis, so this is the only comparison against the convention that defines the outcome. It is also confined to where plaque lay, which mostly means the proximal segments, and to plaques large enough to contour, so it tells little about distal boundaries or minimal plaque. Reader agreement on segment-level plaque presence is only κ ≈ 0.73–0.88, and it mixes detection with location ([de Knegt et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378095); [Int J Cardiovasc Imaging 2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10554-008-9299-z)). That is why the reader-cut Medis baseline, attributing only the excess miss rate to the division, is the right control. A third comparator, the Medis centerline cut by the rule, separates the rule's contribution from the tracer's within this check. No published tolerance rule exists for contours that straddle a boundary, so the thesis should fix its own minimum overlap before running the check (the appendix's [x] mm). The draft already does this.

For metrics, the single "share of length with the same label" depends on artery length and is dominated by the long mid and distal stretches. A **length-weighted 3×3 confusion matrix per artery** gives the same number as its trace and shows which boundary fails. For comparability, Cao's per-label overlap should be reported as well ([Cao et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5677991/)). The most interpretable quantity is the **signed boundary difference in mm** per boundary. Bland–Altman limits of agreement apply only if the differences are roughly normal ([Bland & Altman 1986](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~mb55/meas/ba.htm)). Landmark boundaries will probably produce a mixture: near zero when rule and reader pick the same branch, and large when they pick different branches. The thesis should therefore show the histogram, report the median with 2.5–97.5 percentiles, and give the share of boundaries where a different branch was chosen. Results belong apart for branch-based and fraction-based boundaries. Registration in CT–IVUS work degrades with distance from the side-branch anchor ([PMC6824266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6824266/)), and nothing in the literature benchmarks a landmark-free coronary cut. They should also be split by dominance, where expert agreement itself drops ([Cao et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5677991/)).

## Leave-one-out by person replaces the single half split

A single random split gives "too pessimistic and highly uncertain" estimates ([Austin & Steyerberg 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5394463)), and the bootstrap and cross-validation are more efficient ([Steyerberg et al. 2001](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/74159)). The rule has two free fractions, each a median over hundreds of trees, so the optimism from fitting is negligible and the half split mainly halves precision. The better design fits the final fractions on all Medis trees and reports agreement with each tree's fractions re-estimated without that participant. Re-computing a median is trivial, and each person contributes one tree, so this is person-level cross-validation and consistent with the thesis rule. CIs come from a person-level bootstrap. If the half split is kept for simplicity, it should at least be repeated over many random splits and the spread reported. The fractions are fitted on Medis lengths, so they transfer only if tracer and Medis trees end at comparable distal points, which step 1b tests directly.

## Transport to plaque-free arteries remains an assumption with checks

Performance estimated in one case mix need not hold in another, a spectrum effect ([Mulherin & Miller 2002](https://catalogofbias.org/biases/spectrum-bias/)). No study has measured segment-labelling agreement separately in plaque-free and diseased arteries, and published labelling sets are selected; Cao et al. excluded severe proximal lesions ([Cao et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5677991/)). Side-branch origins do not depend on plaque. The traced length, however, does depend on image quality and stenosis, and these differ between the Medis participants and the plaque-free cohort. The defensible wording is that agreement was estimated in participants with plaque and assumed, not shown, to carry over.

Three checks can support or qualify that assumption without labels. The first is a within-Medis gradient: whether the boundary difference changes with burden (SIS, SSS or CACS, or plaque next to the boundary). Even a flat gradient leaves the step to zero burden an extrapolation. The second is a label-free comparison, as aggregates only, between the Medis participants and the plaque-free cohort: traced artery length, share of landmarks found, rule-derived segment lengths and ostium-to-boundary distances. The third is a small blinded visual audit of plausible versus implausible boundaries in a random sample of plaque-free trees. All three must be fixed, along with the rule, before the outcome is examined, since the tracer geometry of the clean cohort is still pending.

## A perturbation analysis turns millimetres into consequences

Agreement alone does not say whether the residual disagreement matters for the who question. The per-segment geometry comes from the rule, while follow-up plaque is assigned by the reader's convention, so boundary disagreement causes misclassification in both exposure and outcome location. Good practice in quantitative bias analysis draws bias parameters from validation data and prespecifies the analysis ([Lash et al. 2014](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/good-practices-for-quantitative-bias-analysis/)). It warns that transporting those parameters to another population is a known pitfall ([Fox, Lash & Bodnar](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7750925)), and that analyses tuned after the fact can mislead ([Lash et al. 2021](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/bias-analysis-gone-bad/)). The proposed design follows from this. In each of a few hundred iterations, every clean-cohort boundary is shifted by a draw from the step 1b distribution of signed differences, widened as a stress test because that distribution comes from diseased arteries. The features are recomputed, the pooled GLMM is refitted, and the spread of the LR statistic, ΔC and calibration is reported. A simple fixed grid of ±5 and ±10 mm should be reported alongside. This analysis complements the agreement steps and does not replace them: without step 1b the perturbation sizes would be arbitrary. Taper and curvature are not linear in stretch length, so the direction of bias must be simulated, not assumed. No coronary-geometry study with such an analysis was found, so the design is an adaptation rather than an established method.

## Recommended LaTeX paragraph

The paragraph keeps the reader's convention as the reference, states the transport assumption, and leaves the optional second reader and the sensitivity section as red notes until they are decided.

```latex
\subsection{Validation of the Division}
\label{sec:method-split-validation}

The visual analysis records plaque per segment but not where one segment ends and the next begins, so the boundaries were placed on the traced arteries by a rule.
Each artery was cut at the origins of the side branches labelled by the tracer (D1, D2, OM1, OM2 and PDA; Section~\ref{sec:method-tracer}).
Two boundaries have no such branch, the LAD mid--distal boundary when no second diagonal is present and the RCA acute margin, and these were placed at a fixed fraction of the artery length, set as the median position of the reader's boundary in the segment-labelled Medis centerlines (Section~\ref{sec:data-medis}).
The full rule is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-split}.

No boundary reference exists in the plaque-free cohort, so the division was compared with the reader's boundaries in the Medis data of participants with plaque at baseline, in three steps.
First, the rule was applied to the Medis centerlines, where a disagreement with the reader's labels on the same points comes from the rule alone.
Agreement was measured as the share of artery length given the same segment, per pair of segments, and as the signed distance along the artery between each boundary of the rule and the reader's, reported separately for branch-based and fraction-based boundaries and by dominance.
Each participant was evaluated with fractions estimated without that participant's tree.
Second, the rule was applied to the tracer arteries of the same participants in Medis coordinates (Section~\ref{sec:method-medis}) and compared with the reader's label at the nearest Medis point; the change from the first step is the contribution of the tracer, such as a different traced length that moves the fraction-based boundaries.
Third, the division was checked against the segments graded with plaque in the visual analysis: a segment graded with plaque at baseline and contoured in Medis should contain that contour on the tracer artery, placed by position only.
The same miss rate on the Medis centerlines cut at the reader's labels served as the baseline, and only the excess was attributed to the division.
Experts disagree on some segment boundaries, so the results are reported as agreement with the reader's boundaries, not as errors.
Agreement was estimated in arteries with plaque and was assumed, not shown, to hold in arteries without plaque; traced length, the share of landmarks found and the segment lengths were therefore compared between the Medis participants and the plaque-free cohort, and the effect of misplaced boundaries on the main analysis was assessed by shifting them by the observed differences (Section~[..]).
```

## Conclusion

The division can be shown to agree with the convention behind the outcome only through the plaque-contour check. That check covers only plaque large enough to contour, in participants who already had plaque. Every other step validates against a Medis convention whose origin is still unknown. The validation therefore earns two modest claims and no more: the rule reproduces the Medis readers' boundaries about as well as the reported distribution shows, and the thesis conclusions do or do not survive misplacement of that size. Settling how the Medis labels were produced (open in 01b) matters more for the strength of this section than any refinement of the metrics. If they turn out to be uncorrected software labels, step 1 shrinks to an algorithm-vs-algorithm comparison, and the plaque-contour check and the perturbation analysis carry the validation.
