# Keep taper and curvature additive, test their product second

Physics, clinical evidence and the parameter budget all point to the same encoding: taper and curvature should enter the primary geometry block of both the who and the where models as **two separate linear main effects**, and their product should be a **prespecified secondary test with a prespecified sign**, not part of the one confirmatory LR test. The best idealized coronary simulation shows that the two features largely **superpose** on wall shear stress (WSS). Curvature decides *where around the wall* WSS is low (the inner wall of each bend). Taper sets the axial *level* of WSS through the Poiseuille term Q/r³ ([Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)). Superposition means additivity on the log-WSS scale, which is exactly what two main effects on the log-odds scale encode. No human study has ever fitted a taper × curvature term against plaque. The only combination evidence is cross-sectional and comes from product indices or counts in diseased patients ([PMC10644144](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10644144); [ACC Journal Scan of Han 2022](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2022/02/04/18/53/Association-of-Plaque-Location)). This matches the decided CGPS plan, where the primary where test G01 holds curvature z and taper z plus the LM features coded on the proximal LAD and LCx rows, and curvature × taper sits in the exploratory G02–G04. Three refinements matter more than the interaction. In the conditional logistic model, the product should be formed from **within-person deviations**: a raw product mixes three different hypotheses. The **sign** of the curvature effect is genuinely uncertain at segment level: curvature also drives helical flow that raises segment-averaged WSS, and the local Medis progression analysis found more turning going with *less* progression. Finally, raw taper is a diluted proxy, since the physically relevant quantity is taper *beyond* what side-branch flow loss requires. Expect small effects. Segment-level summaries discard the circumferential inner-wall signal through which curvature acts.

## Curvature places low WSS, taper scales it

The cleanest evidence on how the two features combine comes from pulsatile simulations of rigid S-shaped coronary models that taper to an outlet radius of 78% of the inlet (2 mm to 1.56 mm, bend radius 3.5 cm, Dean number 54). During forward flow, every bend showed **high WSS on the outer wall and low WSS on the inner wall**. The positions of the consistently low-WSS points depended only on the planar geometry, and not on the inlet waveform or on whether the second bend was in plane. Taper changed the magnitude: WSS rose as the radius fell downstream. As a result, the **global WSS minimum moved from a bend in the uniform model to the straight proximal inlet in the tapered model**. The authors conclude that proximal low WSS in real arteries is "due, in part, to the overall taper in the artery and not only to the local curvature variation" ([Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)). The two low-WSS kinds also differ in character. At the taper-driven proximal minimum the oscillatory shear index (OSI) was only moderate, while at the curvature-driven points low WSS coincided with high OSI ([Johnston et al. 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)).

That picture fits the classical theory of curved pipes. In Dean's expansion, a secondary flow of two counter-rotating vortices appears at first order in the Dean number. At second order it pushes the axial velocity peak toward the outer wall, and at moderate Dean numbers "the wall shear stress is minimum on the inside of the bend" ([Imperial College BioFluids lecture](https://www.ma.ic.ac.uk/~ajm8/BioFluids/lec1117.pdf)). A compliant curved-tube model with coronary curvature and flow found mean WSS about 50% lower on the inside wall, with stronger oscillation there ([Qiu & Tarbell 2000](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.429629)). No closed-form inner-to-outer WSS ratio as a function of Dean number was found, and the inner-wall solution at high Dean numbers remains unresolved ([Imperial College BioFluids lecture](https://www.ma.ic.ac.uk/~ajm8/BioFluids/lec1117.pdf)).

A scaling argument, the researchers' own inference and not a published result, makes the superposition explicit. At fixed flow Q, Poiseuille WSS is τ = 4μQ/(πr³). Convergent taper therefore raises the axial WSS baseline as r⁻³. The Dean number Dn = Re·√(r/R_c) changes only slowly along a converging tube, roughly as r^(−1/2) at fixed Q and fixed bend radius. A bend placed distally therefore produces about the same *relative* inner-wall dip, sitting on a higher baseline. On the log-WSS scale the result is approximately log τ ≈ (taper/radius term) + (curvature term), a sum with no product. It follows that a **taper × curvature interaction is not what the physics predicts on the WSS scale**. An interaction on the log-odds scale could still arise if plaque responds to WSS through a threshold, so that the inner-wall dip only matters once the baseline is already low.

One configuration breaks the additivity. A *divergent* (widening) segment decelerates the core flow and sets up an adverse pressure gradient that favours separation on the inner wall of a bend. Low WSS from widening and low WSS from curvature would then compound in the same place. This too is an inference: no coronary CFD of a curved, divergent segment was found. It gives the secondary test a prespecified sign, however. If taper is coded positive for narrowing (−d ln r/ds), the curvature effect on plaque should be **strongest where taper is weak or negative**, which means a **negative product coefficient**.

## Raw taper carries only the part of WSS not explained by branch flow loss

Branch-free idealized models overstate the importance of taper. Along a real coronary trunk, flow leaves through side branches, so Q falls together with r. If the trunk obeyed Murray's law (Q ∝ r³), WSS would be constant along the vessel and taper would carry no WSS signal at all. Measured exponents are lower. Kassab's theory and experiments give 7/3 ([Kassab & Finet, EuroIntervention 2015](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/doi/10.4244/EIJV11SVA3)), and a **meta-analysis of 1,070 coronary trees pooled an exponent of 2.39** ([Taylor et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11380967/)). An empirical fit, Q = 1.43·d^2.55, is the standard flow-split rule in patient-specific CFD ([Frontiers Physiol 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.871912/full)). Boundary conditions matter: compared with an empirical flow–diameter law, Murray's law gave about 8% higher main-branch WSS and about 13% smaller low-WSS regions near bifurcations ([van der Giessen et al. 2011](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/the-influence-of-boundary-conditions-on-wall-shear-stress-distrib/)).

The arithmetic that follows is the researchers' own. With Q ∝ r^k and k ≈ 2.33, τ ∝ r^(k−3) ≈ r^(−0.67). A trunk that narrows from 4 to 3 mm in diameter at normal branch flow loss raises Poiseuille WSS by only about 21%. The same narrowing with no flow loss, as in branch-free models, raises it 2.4-fold. The WSS-relevant quantity is therefore the **mismatch between observed taper and the taper that branch flow loss requires**. A segment that stays wide while flow leaves through a large side branch should have locally low WSS. A segment that narrows faster than flow is lost should have high WSS. Raw taper mixes the flow-matched part, which carries no WSS change, with the mismatch part, so it is an attenuated proxy. No published "flow-matched taper residual" was found. Computing one requires side-branch diameters, and building it would replace the decided taper definition (a robust slope of log radius against arc length, masked about one diameter around side branches). It belongs in a sensitivity analysis, flagged as a change of feature definition, and not in the confirmatory block.

The same logic explains why the LM diameter ratios are the strongest geometric WSS term in the published CFD. In 127 CCTA-derived LM bifurcations without significant stenosis, **Finet's ratio alone explained R² = 0.71 of the low-TAESS area** (standardised β −0.84). LM, LAD and LCx curvature each explained 1% or less, and the bifurcation angles were not significant ([Gharleghi et al., arXiv 2401.12504](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.12504v1)). Finet's ratio is in effect a flow-matched diameter index: daughters that are wide relative to the parent's flow carry slow flow and low WSS. That simulation used uniform inlet velocity, not patient-specific flow, so the R² measures the geometric sensitivity of the model rather than in-vivo truth. Across patient-specific studies, diameter is also the most consistent single correlate of time-averaged WSS. In 39 left coronary trees, Diagonal diameter correlated with average TAESS (ρ = −0.85), while curvature and torsion did not correlate significantly ([Shen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06161)). Deep-learning WSS surrogates agree. Integrated gradients rank radius and inlet velocity as the most important inputs, and the importance of curvature was inconsistent ([Alamir et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1360330/full)).

## Curvature has a contested sign at segment scale

Curvature does two things at once. It lowers WSS on the inner wall, and it generates helical flow that raises WSS overall. In patient-specific coronary models, **more tortuous vessels had higher WSS**, which the authors attributed to helical flow ([Vorobtsova et al. 2016](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2770474)). In 30 swine coronaries, torsion dictated helical flow (r = 0.64), and helicity intensity was inversely associated with the surface area under unfavourable shear ([De Nisco et al. 2019](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2726967)). Among 127 LM geometries, average absolute curvature was the best curvature correlate of low TAWSS area, while the arc-to-chord tortuosity index showed no association (p = 0.86). Another study found the opposite trend, and the authors attribute the inconsistency to differing metrics ([Kashyap et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8764056/)).

The researchers infer that the sign of curvature depends on the summary. A segment-mean curvature averages over inner and outer walls and over the helical-flow gain, so it can move segment-averaged WSS *up* while it moves the inner-wall minimum *down*. The binary segment outcome (any new plaque in the segment) captures curvature only if the inner-wall dip changes *whether* plaque appears in the segment, not merely where around the circumference it sits. A segment-level curvature effect should therefore be attenuated, and its sign should not be fixed in advance.

Single-phase CCTA adds a further limit. In a moving-wall RCA model, rank correlations between static and dynamic simulations were **0.93 for TAWSS but 0.75 for OSI and 0.19 for transWSS** ([Kandangwa et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2022.962687/full)). Static geometry supports a time-averaged low-WSS hypothesis, which is what taper and the curvature-driven inner-wall dip both act on. The oscillatory component, which Johnston's model ties specifically to curvature-driven sites, is the part a single CCTA phase captures worst. Curvature measured from a single phase also shifts: it is higher at end-systole than at end-diastole ([van Zandwijk 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7165136/)).

## Clinical data support curvature and diameter separately, never together

Human evidence for curvature is cross-sectional but fairly consistent. In 73 patients, RCA curvature was 16.7% higher in stenotic segments, and calcified and mixed-plaque segments were more curved than plaque-free ones ([ESCR 2014 poster](https://epos.myesr.org/poster/escr/escr2014/458); [review PMC9497479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9497479/)). IVUS fused with angiography showed plaque preferring the inner curvature in most vessels, with the effect growing with curvature, and circumferential plaque thickness correlated more strongly with local curvature than with computed WSS ([Wahle 2006 abstract](https://user.engineering.uiowa.edu/~awahle/WahLIT/Abstracts/wahl06a-cfd.html)). In lesion-free autopsy hearts, branch angle and local curvature correlated with intimal thickness *before* overt disease, which is the result closest to a plaque-free baseline ([review PMC9497479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9497479/)). Global tortuosity is inconsistent. Higher tortuosity went with *lower* calcium volume, but tortuosity rises from proximal to distal while calcium falls, so position confounds the comparison ([Nannini et al. 2024](https://re.public.polimi.it/handle/11311/1276541)). In 1,010 angiography patients, tortuosity was inversely associated with CAD (OR 0.755) and positively with female sex and hypertension ([Li et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3164184/)).

Direct taper evidence is thin and exposed to reverse causation. Normal angiographic tapering averages about 0.22 mm per 10 mm, and about 10% of arteries taper in reverse ([Normal angiographic tapering](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11090643_Normal_angiographic_tapering_of_the_coronary_arteries)). IVUS shows the LAD tapering more steeply (0.33 mm per 10 mm) than the RCA or LCx (about 0.15) and shows that lumen taper partly reflects differential plaque accumulation ([Am J Cardiol, DOI 10.1016/s0002-9149(00)80072-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0002-9149(00)80072-7)). The steeper LAD taper confirms that taper is confounded with vessel. The decided within-segment-type z-score handles the mean difference, and position in the clogit base handles the rest. In CAD patients, lumens are diffusely 30–50% too small for their distal bed ([Seiler, abstract record](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1967018012)). A plaque-free CGPS baseline (SIS = SSS = 0) removes most of this problem, although subclinical, CT-invisible wall thickening can still bias the radius profile.

The few combination findings all go beyond main effects, but none is an interaction test. In the RCA, curvature and torsion together, with their cyclic excursion, explained 17–44% of wall-thickness variance, and no single parameter correlated ([Zhu & Friedman 2003, via PMC9497479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9497479/)). A product of proximal-LAD tortuosity and the cosine of the LM–LAD angle predicted severe proximal LAD stenosis better than either part (AUC 0.767 vs 0.634 for tortuosity alone), with cut-offs derived and tested in the same 133 patients ([Moon et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10644144)). Counting adverse geometric characteristics (distance from the ostium, bifurcation location, tortuosity) gave an **HR of 2.90 for one and 6.84 for two or more**, and lifted the AUC from 0.733 to 0.766 beyond plaque features. Distance from the ostium is itself one of the counted features, and tortuous lesions were rare (4.3% vs 1.4%) ([ACC Journal Scan of Han 2022](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2022/02/04/18/53/Association-of-Plaque-Location)).

The longitudinal case that low WSS drives plaque comes from invasive studies of already diseased arteries over 6–12 months. In PREDICTION, 506 ACS patients had three-vessel profiling, and **low endothelial shear stress and large plaque burden independently and additively predicted lumen loss** ([PubMed 22723305](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22723305/)). Low-ESS subsegments progressed in 33.3% vs 7.9% of other subsegments ([Stone 2007 abstract](https://user.engineering.uiowa.edu/~awahle/WahLIT/Abstracts/ston07-cfd.html)). The closest design analogue to the where question is PARADIGM radiomics: among **9,583 segments normal at baseline, 9.8% developed new plaque**, and texture features lifted the C-index from about 0.70 to 0.767 in the test set ([Yonsei repository](https://ir.ymlib.yonsei.ac.kr/handle/22282913/200058)). That is texture, not geometry. No study was found that relates baseline curvature or taper to new plaque in plaque-free segments, or that tests geometry beyond anatomical position, so the CGPS analysis has no prior effect size to lean on.

The local Medis analysis (`CGPS_data/progression_turning.md`) is the only in-house evidence, and it argues against a confident sign. Across 1,157 segments from 189 people, mostly with plaque at baseline (progression, not new plaque), single turning summaries gave **ORs per SD of 0.85–0.98**, so more turning went with *less* progression. Within-person LR p-values ranged from 0.08 to 0.98, within-person C did not improve (0.714 base, at most 0.714 with turning), and an 8-df test found no difference of the turning effect between segments (p = 0.62 for mean T5, 0.68 for mean T2.5). Two caveats limit how far this transfers. The population is diseased, and the Medis trees are a reference selection, not the tracer output the thesis will use. The direction nevertheless agrees with the helical-flow reading of curvature, and the six near-identical variants show how strongly the turning summaries are redundant.

## Encode the main effects in the block and the product outside it

The who and where models estimate different interactions, and the thesis should state this before fitting. In the who model, geometry enters as one value per person: the decided summary is the mean of within-segment-type z-scores over the measured trunk segments. A product of two person means is a **between-person** effect modification ("people whose arteries are both curved and weakly tapered"). In the conditional logistic where model, person-constant terms cancel, and only within-person contrasts identify coefficients ([Begg & Parides 2003, via Statalist](https://stata.com/statalist/archive/2003-11/msg00258.html)). A null result in one model does not contradict the other.

In clogit, the raw segment-level product is not a clean within-person interaction. Write x = x̄ᵢ + x̃ᵢⱼ and z = z̄ᵢ + z̃ᵢⱼ (person mean plus within-person deviation). Then x·z = x̃z̃ + x̃·z̄ᵢ + x̄ᵢ·z̃ + x̄ᵢz̄ᵢ. The last term is absorbed by the stratum, and one coefficient is forced onto the pure within-person interaction plus two cross-level terms of the form "the within-person taper effect depends on how curved this person's arteries are on average". This decomposition is the researchers' own algebra, built on the within/between split of Neuhaus & Kalbfleisch ([summary of Neuhaus & McCulloch 2006](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/442170); [Goetgeluk & Vansteelandt abstract](https://www.jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/IBC2006/Abstracts/pdf/666.pdf)), and the supervisor should check it. The recommended product is x̃·z̃: standardise each feature within segment type in the training fold, centre on the person's mean over their available trunk segments, then multiply. The main-effect coefficients do not change, since the person mean is absorbed. The within-person correlation of curvature and taper should be reported. If it is high, the product approaches a quadratic in one feature and cannot be separated.

The options under the single-LR-test rule are summarised below, with their effect on the decided design.

| Encoding | Where block df | Fits one-test rule? | Changes decided design? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main effects only in G01; within-person product x̃·z̃ as a secondary LR test with a prespecified negative sign | 5 (+1 secondary) | Yes; the secondary test is labelled exploratory | No: matches G01 and G02–G04, and only fixes the product's form and sign | **Recommended** |
| Product inside the confirmatory block | 6 | Yes | **Yes**: G01 grows from 5 to 6 df and the block's power drops if the interaction is null | Not recommended |
| 1-df physics composite (e.g. a flow-scaled Dean surrogate) replacing curvature and taper | 4 | Yes | **Yes**: it redefines what "geometry" means in the primary hypothesis | Sensitivity only |
| Tensor-product spline surface | about 7–8 extra | No | Exceeds the roughly 15–20 parameter budget | Descriptive figure only |
| Curvature effect stratified by segment or vessel | 2–8 extra per feature | No | Adds unbudgeted df; Medis found no heterogeneity (p ≈ 0.6) | Leave out |

The table rests on standard prediction-modelling advice. Harrell recommends prespecifying the df spent per predictor and testing complex interactions as pooled chunks, and his `%ia%` restricted interaction cuts the cost of a tensor surface without removing it ([useR! 2016 tutorial outline](https://user2016.r-project.org/tutorials/00.html)). Modelling interactions improves discrimination only when true interactions exist and overfits otherwise ([van Klaveren et al. 2019](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/117959)). Riley's criteria for binary outcomes replace events-per-variable rules ([Riley et al. 2020](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/457642)). They are not derived for conditional likelihoods, where the effective sample is the people with new plaque in some but not all of their graded trunk segments. In a plaque-free cohort that is a fraction of the people with any new plaque.

A 1-df composite is the most df-efficient choice only if its form is right, and the evidence does not pin the form down. The textbook Dean number needs flow, which CCTA does not measure. The researchers' inference is that a flow-scaled surrogate is computable from the tracer output: substituting Q ∝ d^k gives De(s) ∝ r(s)^(k−1)·√(κ(s)·r(s)). Two problems block it from confirmatory use. It fixes the sign of curvature, which the helical-flow evidence and the Medis results contradict. A composite also reads as "geometry" while hiding which component carries the signal. A cautionary precedent: the tortuosity index, a mechanistically appealing composite, did worse than plain mean absolute curvature against low TAWSS ([Kashyap et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8764056/)). The composite should be pre-registered as a sensitivity analysis, computed from tracer lumen and centerline only. The plan already lists a "Dean-type exposure (curvature × calibre)" among its exploratory contour-based features, so this adds no new thread.

The LM features need one explicit flag. A literal reading of a clogit over 9 trunk segments would drop the LM bifurcation angle and the two LM diameter ratios, since they are constant within a person. The decided G01 avoids this by coding them as interactions with the proximal LAD and proximal LCx rows, which turns them into allocation effects ("does a wide LM→LAD ratio shift plaque toward the proximal LAD within this person?"). That choice is correct, and given the Finet's-ratio evidence these are probably the geometry terms with the best physical prior. Any proposal to replace the block with a pure within-person taper-curvature composite, or to enter only segment-varying terms, would **remove the LM features from the where test** and change the decided design. The plan's open decision on person × position confounding applies with special force here. Age and sex shift proximal plaque, so age × proximal and sex × proximal terms belong in the base before the LM interactions are read.

Two summary choices matter more than the interaction. For curvature, mean absolute curvature after smoothing has the best support. Maximum and fraction-above-threshold summaries depend on single noisy points and on arbitrary cut-offs, and the decided plan already fixes smoothing (curvature w ≈ 2–3 mm) and a per-scan noise covariate. For taper, the sign convention must be fixed before outcomes are linked: −d ln r/ds, positive for narrowing, keeps "more taper → higher WSS → expected lower risk" readable. Keep log length in the base so that taper is not a length proxy. A pointwise product κ(s)·taper(s), averaged over the segment, is a 1-df alternative closer to the local physics than the product of two segment means, but it is a different feature and must be chosen before unblinding.

Reporting should follow the scale. Logistic and conditional logistic models estimate interaction on the odds-ratio scale, so report the product as a ratio of ORs with its CI, and the OR per SD of curvature at taper −1, 0 and +1 SD ([Knol & VanderWeele 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3324457)). For who, an additive-scale RERI for high/high versus low/low can be computed from the refitted model's absolute risks. For where it cannot, since the clogit conditions out baseline odds. A predicted log-odds surface over curvature and taper, with the data density overlaid and binned to meet MIN_CELL = 5, is the most transparent figure for either model.

## Conclusion

The question "how should taper and curvature interact" has a counter-intuitive answer: on the WSS scale they should mostly *not* interact, and that additivity is a physical prediction the thesis can state and test. Curvature places low WSS on the inner wall, and taper beyond branch flow loss shifts the whole WSS level. The confirmatory blocks should therefore stay as decided, and the only new commitment is a secondary within-person product test with a negative sign written down in advance. A significant negative product would indicate a threshold-like plaque response to WSS. A null product with significant main effects would be the result the physics predicts, not a failure.

The larger risk is attenuation, not misspecification. Segment means discard the circumferential signal through which curvature acts, raw taper mixes flow-matched narrowing with the WSS-relevant mismatch, and single-phase CCTA loses the oscillatory component. Each of these biases the geometry block toward the null. The most likely place for a positive where result is therefore the LM ratio terms on the proximal rows, which carry the strongest published WSS prior. The sharpest falsifiable claim the thesis can make about curvature is that its segment-level sign is uncertain, and the Medis progression data already lean toward protection.
