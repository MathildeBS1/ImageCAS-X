# Summarise the profile, keep the GLMM

**The best way to use pointwise curvature in this thesis is not a neural network. Compute curvature at every centerline sample on a fixed, outcome-blind scale of at least about 5 mm. Collapse each segment's profile into a small, prespecified set of profile functionals or outcome-blind functional principal component (FPC) scores. Enter them as a fixed-df block in the same pooled cloglog GLMM, so that the one likelihood-ratio test keeps a χ² reference.** The outcome is read visually once per SCCT segment, so pointwise geometry reaches the outcome only through some per-segment summary. The real choice is which summary to use, and who chooses it: the analyst before the outcome is seen, or a model fitted to it. Tello Ayala et al. is a weak template. It used 2D invasive angiograms of the RCA only, in a referral registry of 22,334 patients. Its outcome was stenosis read from the same image (cross-sectional, not incident plaque), and the transformer's AUROC gain (0.67 vs 0.60) had no adjustment for risk factors ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)). With about 450 events, a sequence network would be unstable, and it has no likelihood-ratio test with known df. Taper cannot be measured point by point from CCTA at all, so "pointwise" in practice applies to curvature, while taper stays a robust slope over the segment or trunk. No study was found that compares pointwise with segment-averaged geometry for predicting plaque development, in any modality. Whatever the thesis does here is untested ground and should be presented that way.

## Tello Ayala answered a different question on different data

The JACC: Advances paper is mainly a measurement study. A U-Net trained on an external dataset segments the RCA in one left anterior oblique (LAO) frame of a 2D angiogram, and the mask is skeletonised. The tortuosity score is the **mean absolute turning angle between consecutive skeleton points, divided by π**. It is hand-defined and label-free, and the main text describes no smoothing or resampling ([PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)). Only the secondary analysis is supervised: a local-attention transformer reads "the ordered vector of local tortuosity along the artery" together with age and sex, and classifies RCA stenosis of any grade read from the same angiogram. It reached **AUROC 0.67 (0.65–0.69), against 0.60 (0.58–0.63)** for logistic regression on mean tortuosity, age and sex. Neither model included hypertension, diabetes, lipids or smoking ([PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)). Input length, resampling, the data split and whether splits were by patient are in a supplement that could not be accessed. No code release was found.

Four features of the design limit what the 0.07 AUROC gap can show for CGPS. The design is **cross-sectional**: a stenosis can itself bend a 2D skeleton, so the local profile may partly encode the label. The cohort is a **referral catheterisation population** that included patients with prior PCI (28.9%) or CABG (15.5%). The comparator is a mean-only logistic model without risk factors, which is a weak baseline. And adjusted global tortuosity barely mattered: **OR 1.05 per SD** for CAD ([PMC13308244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13308244/)). Expert agreement was moderate (κ 0.52, recall 58% in the high-tortuosity bucket). The score correlated only weakly with the arc/chord index (r = 0.116), which suggests, as this report's inference, that unsmoothed pixel-to-pixel turning angles partly measure skeleton jitter. The paper's usable lesson is therefore narrow. An ordered profile can carry information that a mean discards, but this was shown only cross-sectionally, in 2D, on about 20 times more patients than CGPS has. The thesis curvature section already adapts the turning-angle idea in the opposite direction from the transformer, with 3D centerlines and a fixed 5 mm chord instead of raw skeleton steps (`thesis/methodology/06_curvature.tex`).

The wider literature shows no better precedent. Sequence and graph networks on coronary centerlines exist for detecting existing plaque from image intensities along the centerline, such as the recurrent CNN of Zreik et al. on 163 patients ([arXiv 1804.04360](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.04360)). They also exist for FFR surrogates trained on dense, simulated labels ([MICCAI 2023](https://conferences.miccai.org/2023/papers/135-Paper0259.html)). The longitudinal progression models use segment-level or patient-level summaries, not profiles. Examples are PARADIGM's rapid-progression model ([Han et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7335586)) and a multiparametric model in 187 patients with stable CAD ([Sakellarios et al. 2021](https://doaj.org/article/887844a1d32343c9bfc0ce0debfb3cc8)). No published model was found that feeds baseline pointwise curvature, torsion or radius profiles into a prediction of incident plaque.

## The segment read caps the resolution, and noise sets a 5 mm floor

Two limits squeeze "every point" from opposite sides. From above, the label is one visual grade per segment, and the read does not record which wall, or which millimetre, holds the plaque. The haemodynamic rationale works at a scale the outcome cannot see. Low shear stress sits on the inner wall of bends and on the lateral walls just downstream of bifurcations ([UNMC review](https://www.unmc.edu/intmed/_documents/cardiology/cbbl/2010-3.pdf)). Around lesions, the lowest shear stress lies **3–9 mm** from the minimal lumen area ([van den Hoogen 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10017098/)). The studies that link local shear stress to later change all followed patients who already had CAD, imaged invasively, for 6–12 months. Effects were modest: in PREDICTION, low shear stress gave an OR of about 2 for worsening lumen narrowing, against about 5 for large plaque burden ([TCTMD report of PREDICTION](https://www.tctmd.com/news/endothelial-shear-stress-works-prediction-tool)). Those are conference figures from a population with ACS. They motivate local geometry, but they are not evidence that it predicts plaque developing in a plaque-free wall over ten years.

From below, curvature is a second derivative of position, so its noise grows roughly as position error divided by the window length squared. This scaling is this report's reasoning, not a coronary source. Frenet curvature is known to be "highly sensitive to noise", and averaging it to suppress noise tends to underestimate true peaks ([Ann Biomed Eng](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10439-008-9590-0.pdf)). Systolic and diastolic curvature also differ systematically ([Springer Medizin](https://www.springermedizin.de/assessment-of-dynamic-change-of-coronary-artery-geometry-and-its/17405834)). **No scan–rescan or inter-algorithm reproducibility study of coronary CCTA curvature was found.** The thesis already sets its scale in an outcome-blind way. Mean curvature fell by 41% from a 2 to a 5 mm chord, but only by a further 13% from 5 to 8 mm, and so the chord was fixed at 5 mm (`06_curvature.tex`). That procedure resembles the bandwidth choice in the AneuRisk carotid study, which balanced fit against roughness and found results insensitive near the chosen bandwidth ([Sangalli et al. 2009](https://sangalli.faculty.polimi.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/2009_Sangalli-Secchi-Vantini-Veneziani-JASA_compresso.pdf)).

Taper is a harder case. Single-cross-section CCTA lumen diameter agrees with intravascular imaging only to **about ±0.8 mm** (95% limits of agreement) ([Monash Health](https://repository.monashhealth.org/monashhealthjspui/handle/1/52691)). A normal trunk tapers by about 0.25 mm of diameter per 10 mm, as stated in `09_taper.tex`. Local taper over a few millimetres is therefore below the measurement error. A Theil–Sen slope over at least 20 mm, as the taper section specifies, is the right representation, and a pointwise taper profile should not be attempted.

The two limits nearly meet, and this changes the question. Trunk segments are roughly 10–40 mm long (this report's estimate, not sourced). At a 5 mm scale, a segment therefore holds only about **2–8 effectively independent curvature values**, and the code already records a segment as missing when it has fewer than two turning angles. Sampling the 5 mm curvature every 0.5 mm with a sliding window gives a dense profile, but neighbouring values are strongly correlated, so it adds smoothness, not information. At this scale, the segment mean already uses every point. Between the mean and the maximum there is room for only a few distinct summaries. A neural network that "reads the profile" would read a handful of numbers per segment.

## A sequence network cannot be afforded at 450 events and cannot carry the test

The small-data evidence points one way. In 145 comparisons at low risk of bias, the logit(AUC) difference between machine learning and logistic regression was **0.00**. Machine learning came out ahead only where bias was likely ([Christodoulou et al. 2019](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4167c905-5171-4fc7-b157-ff36d56a73db/files/m0c822b1760523fd587b9a6769e616049)). In resampling simulations on clinical cohorts, logistic regression stabilised at about 20–50 events per variable. Neural networks "showed instability and a high optimism even with >200 events per variable" and "may need over 10 times as many" ([van der Ploeg et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4289553)). Small development samples also produce unstable individual predictions ([Riley et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10729337/)). With about 450 events, about 45–90 per outer fold, ΔC will be noisy for any model. A network adds variation from seeds and initialisation on top. Calibration, which this thesis estimates explicitly, is where flexible models most often fail ([Van Calster et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6912996)). These comparisons used tabular predictors, so their transfer to short 1D sequences is an extrapolation. Even so, nothing points the other way.

The design also already does what the network would add structurally. Attention-based multiple-instance learning (MIL) predicts a bag label from instance inputs with learned pooling ([Ilse et al. 2018](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/ilse18a.html)). Its small-sample successes came from clean synthetic signals, such as one target digit in MNIST-bags. The pooled GLMM combines segment hazards into one person risk through a fixed noisy-OR form: segment cloglog risks are pooled as one minus the product of the segment survival probabilities, integrated over the random intercept. That is a parametric version of the standard MIL assumption, and its pooling costs no parameters.

The decisive argument is inferential. A network fitted to the outcome turns the geometry block into a function rather than a parameter vector. The likelihood ratio then has no χ² reference with known df, so the prespecified test would have to become a sample-split or permutation test that retrains the network on every permutation, and the per-SD hazard ratios disappear. Semi-structured deep regression keeps the regression coefficients identifiable by orthogonalising the network part. That protects the SCORE2 coefficients, but not a test of geometry ([Rügamer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05777v4)). Successful CCTA deep learning uses the network as a measuring instrument and keeps prognosis in a regression on the measured quantity. An example is DL-quantified plaque volume entered in a regression in SCOT-HEART, with 41 MIs and a wide hazard ratio interval ([Lin et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9047317)). The in-house tracer already fills that role here.

The only form in which a network fits the design is outcome-blind. An autoencoder or self-supervised encoder is trained on curvature profiles without the outcome, frozen, and contributes k prespecified scores per profile to the GLMM. The LR test then keeps df = k, exactly as for engineered features. Given the 2–8 effective values per segment, such an encoder has little to compress that FPCA cannot, so it should be at most a secondary analysis.

## Three statistical routes use every point, and only two keep a fixed-df test

The statistical answer to "do not average, use the whole curve" is scalar-on-function regression. In it, the linear predictor includes ∫X(s)β(s)ds, with β(s) estimated as a penalised spline ([Goldsmith et al. 2011](https://core.ac.uk/download/61318164.pdf)). It runs in `refund::pfr` or in mgcv, which supports binary responses and random effects through `re()` and `bs="re"` ([refund pfr docs](https://rdrr.io/cran/refund/man/pfr.html); [mgcv linear functional terms](https://rdrr.io/pkg/mgcv/man/linear.functional.terms.html)). The catch is the test. With a penalised β(s), "no effect" is a boundary problem without a plain χ² null. The `refund` authors point to an ordinary ML LRT for inclusion ([rlrt.pfr docs](https://rdrr.io/cran/refund/man/rlrt.pfr.html)). McLean et al. instead use an exact LRT null distribution derived for linear mixed models ([arXiv 1310.5811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.5811)). Neither has been shown to hold for a cloglog GLMM with a person random intercept, and no source was found that combines a functional term, a cloglog link, a random intercept and an LR test.

| Route | What enters the cloglog GLMM | Geometry df (curvature, β shared over 9 segments) | One LR test with χ² reference? | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prespecified profile functional | One summary of the smoothed 5 mm profile per segment, e.g. 90th percentile, or fraction of length above a threshold fixed outcome-blind | 1 | Yes | Wrong functional chosen; the threshold must be frozen first |
| Outcome-blind FPCA scores | First K FPC scores of the within-segment profile on normalised arc length, K fixed in advance | K (e.g. 2–3) | Yes | Leading PCs capture variance, not necessarily risk; few points per segment |
| Penalised functional β(s) | ∫κ(s)β(s)ds via `pfr`/mgcv | Effective df chosen by REML, not fixed | No; nonstandard null | Unverified in a cloglog GLMM; the df of the block is data-driven |

Two routes keep the decided inference intact. A single **prespecified profile functional** replaces the mean at no df cost. On a smoothed curve, an upper quantile or the fraction of length above an outcome-blind threshold captures "is there a sharp bend in this segment" better than the mean. The maximum should be avoided: curvature is non-negative, so noise always adds to it, and the largest noise spike grows with the number of samples, which makes the maximum partly a measure of segment length. **FPCA scores** fixed in advance give a well-sized test. In simulations, the Kong–Staicu–Maity F-test on a fixed number of FPC scores performed as well as the best LRT at n = 500, and its authors recommended it at moderate n ([Tekbudak et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1710.05729)). That evidence is for continuous outcomes, not a clustered cloglog model. The penalised β(s) belongs in a descriptive, untested figure showing where along the segment the coefficient sits.

Within the cloglog model, the profile summary should keep the conventions already in place. Arc length is normalised within each segment, so the anatomical segment boundaries act as landmark registration, and segment length stays a separate covariate. The summary is z-scored within segment type on the training persons. One coefficient is shared over the nine segments. The df cost scales quickly if β varies: three FPC scores for curvature cost 3 df shared, 9 by artery and 27 per segment. Per-segment functional terms are out of reach at this event count, consistent with the earlier power calculation for segment-specific slopes. That calculation found the confirmatory block's power falling from 0.56 at 5 df to 0.30 at 21 df when the true effect is common. The nine segment rows already act as a coarse step-function β(s) along each artery. A functional term inside a segment only adds sub-segment resolution, which is exactly where tracer noise is largest.

One project fact needs care. `CGPS_data/progression_who_pooled.py` already compares ten turning-angle summaries as exploratory 1-df variants: mean, 90th percentile, fraction above the 75th percentile, maximum, total turning, bend count, and an upstream 20 mm window, among others. These runs use the Medis trees, where plaque was present at baseline, with progression as the outcome. Choosing the incident-plaque summary from those results would not touch the clean cohort's outcome. It would still be an outcome-informed choice made in a different population, and it should be described as such, or the choice should rest on reliability alone.

## Freeze the scale and the summary on agreement with Medis, not on the outcome

The scale and the summary have to be fixed before incident plaque is linked, and only outcome-free criteria are available for that. The cleanest is the planned Medis comparison, extended over candidate summaries. For each candidate (mean, 90th percentile, fraction above threshold, first FPC score) and each smoothing scale, compute tracer–Medis agreement on the full LAD, LCx and RCA, and choose the most reliable summary at the shortest scale where agreement plateaus. This fits the decided comparison design, which works on features rather than points. Two caveats belong in the text. Medis is a reference, not ground truth. And the 600 Medis trees come from people with plaque at baseline, so the reliability estimate lies outside the plaque-free cohort. No published coronary study of curvature reliability as a function of window length was found, so this comparison would itself fill a gap.

Reliability matters beyond the choice. Measurement error in the geometry attenuates the hazard ratio towards 1, whether the summary is a mean, a quantile or a set of FPC scores. Two noisy proxies in series, curvature for shear stress and segment plaque for where low shear stress acts, make a null result hard to read as absence. A reliability estimate from the Medis comparison would support a regression-calibration sensitivity analysis of the per-SD hazard ratio. More generally, pointwise summaries that measure *where in the segment* a bend lies mainly inform the allocation of plaque between a person's segments. In the decided design, that information is read descriptively, through the within-person C and the segment-level hazard ratios of the same pooled model, never through a separate model.

## Conclusion

The question assumes a contrast between averaging and evaluating every point that, at CCTA resolution, mostly collapses. Once curvature is computed at the roughly 5 mm scale where noise stops dominating, a trunk segment holds only a few independent values. The useful freedom is then the choice of summary, made in advance, rather than a model that learns it. That reframing also moves the risk. The danger is less that a mean misses a sharp bend, and more that a summary is picked after looking at the outcome, or that a flexible model hides an untestable geometry effect. A prespecified upper-quantile or FPC-score block keeps the single LR test, the per-SD hazard ratios and the pooled person risk, and costs one to three df.

A neural network such as Tello Ayala's transformer has no place in the confirmatory analysis. At most, an outcome-blind encoder could supply a few frozen scores, or a supervised model could run as a labelled exploratory comparison under nested person-split cross-validation, seed averaging, calibration and instability plots, with no test. No study has compared pointwise with segment-level geometry for plaque development, so a careful negative or null result from the CGPS cohort would be informative in itself, provided it is reported with the reliability of the summary and the power of the test beside it.
