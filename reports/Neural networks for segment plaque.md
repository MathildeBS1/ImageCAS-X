# Build a small tree network, test it against regression

**The best use of a neural network in the Herlev–Østerbro follow-up cohort is a small graph neural network (GNN) on each participant's own coronary tree, with segments as nodes, risk factors broadcast to every node, two task heads (incident plaque in baseline plaque-free segments, progression in plaque-positive segments), an unweighted likelihood loss and a seed ensemble. It should be judged only by whether it beats a regression and a boosted-tree model fitted to the same engineered features on identical participant-grouped folds.** No peer-reviewed study has trained a deep network, of any kind, to predict future plaque per segment from serial CCTA. Every published "future plaque" model uses hand-crafted features in Cox, logistic, random forest or XGBoost models. The closest precedent, 9,583 plaque-free segments in PARADIGM, reached a test C-index of 0.767 with clinical plus radiomic features ([Lee et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38378314/)). The network's only defensible advantage is structural. It can read the tree (what neighbouring segments look like and carry) and the ordered geometry along the centerline, which a per-segment table flattens. The data budget is the binding constraint. About 2,000 participants support roughly 25 parameters in a well-specified regression, and networks need far more events per parameter than that. So the network has to be tiny (thousands of weights, not millions), pretrained without outcomes where it learns representations, and reported as a structured ablation rather than as the primary model. A null result from that ablation is still informative: it would show that the tree adds nothing beyond baseline plaque, risk factors and four geometric summaries.

## No one has done this, and the precedents set a modest target

The literature has two separate halves that have not yet met. On one side, graph and tree networks on coronary centerlines work well for **anatomical labelling**, where the label is the segment's name. CPR-GCN reached F1 0.955 on 511 subjects with three graph-convolution blocks of 256 channels. On the same data, it beat the earlier bidirectional tree-LSTM, TreeLab-Net, at 0.871 ([Yang et al., CVPR 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.08560)). Hampe et al. labelled trees with a mean of 33 segments using a three-layer graph attention network with only 32 hidden features, trained on 79 scans ([J Med Imaging 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095121/)). The other strand is FFR surrogates. These reach r = 0.88 on CTA but depend on more than 6,000 synthetic training geometries, because a physics simulator can generate labels ([Xie et al., MICCAI 2023](https://conferences.miccai.org/2023/papers/135-Paper0259.html)). Plaque has no simulator, so a Herlev–Østerbro model must learn from about 2,000 real trees alone.

On the other side, every model of **future plaque** uses hand-crafted features. Patient-level rapid-progression models land between AUC 0.73 and 0.88 on held-out data. In PARADIGM's 1,083 patients, a machine-learning model with quantitative plaque reached 0.83, while a logistic model on the same inputs reached 0.81, not significantly different ([Han et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32089046/)). The screening cohort closest in design to Herlev–Østerbro is 664 Chinese adults with non-obstructive disease followed for 56 months; there a random forest reached 0.875 ([Su et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41349607/)). At segment level, the one large incident-plaque study defined new plaque as at least 1 mm³ at follow-up. In that study **9.8% of plaque-free segments developed plaque**, and the clinical model alone gave a C-index of 0.696 ([Lee et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38378314/)). Every one of these cohorts is clinically referred or selected, mostly Asian, with intervals of two to five years. None is a general population followed for ten years.

Three consequences follow for the thesis. First, a segment-level neural model of future plaque in a general-population cohort would be, as far as these searches show, the first of its kind. That claim should be phrased as "no study found", because MICCAI and arXiv were not searched exhaustively. Second, Han et al. show that a well-specified regression is the comparator that matters, not SCORE2 alone. Third, the realistic ceiling for discrimination is around a C-index of 0.75 to 0.80, and most of that comes from risk factors and existing plaque. Geometry and tree structure can only add an increment on top.

## Two thousand participants buy a network of thousands of weights, not millions

The participant is the unit of information. Segments of one person share risk factors, scanner, reader and interval. With about 15 segments per person, the design effect is 1 + ρ(m − 1) ([Killip et al. 2004](https://www.annfammed.org/content/2/3/204)). At an intraclass correlation of 0.1 or 0.3, the roughly 30,000 segments behave like about 12,500 or 5,800 independent observations. For SCORE2 variables, which are constant within a person, the relevant sample is the 2,000 participants. No published intraclass correlation for incident plaque exists, so it must be estimated from a null mixed-effects model in the cohort and reported.

The Riley criteria are the standard way to turn this into a parameter budget. In their worked example, a 3% event rate with 25 candidate predictors and an anticipated C of 0.74 needs 9,658 patients, and the authors note that machine-learning methods "will likely require an even larger sample size" ([Martin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12106283/); [Riley et al., BMJ 2020](https://www.bmj.com/content/368/bmj.m441)). Under the assumptions of the earlier model-choice analysis (35 to 50% of participants with new or progressing plaque, a conservative anticipated R²), 2,000 participants support about 25 regression parameters ([Riley et al., Stat Med 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519266)). In van der Ploeg et al.'s simulations, neural networks needed **more than 100 events per variable for a stable AUC**, against 20 to 50 for logistic regression ([PMC4289553](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4289553)). A network trained end to end on all inputs is therefore far outside the stable range. Small development sets also produce individual risk estimates that are unstable and miscalibrated in new data. Riley and Collins recommend about 1,000 bootstrap refits with prediction-instability plots and mean absolute prediction error to expose this ([Riley and Collins 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10952221/)).

The tabular benchmarks say the same thing from the machine-learning side. A review of 71 clinical prediction studies with a median n of 1,250 found a logit(AUC) difference of **0.00 between machine learning and logistic regression in low-risk-of-bias comparisons**, and 0.34 in favour of machine learning only where bias was likely ([Christodoulou et al. 2019](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4167c905-5171-4fc7-b157-ff36d56a73db)). On medium-sized tabular data, tree ensembles stay ahead of neural networks, which are hurt by uninformative features and irregular targets ([Grinsztajn et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08815v1)). In a 176-dataset benchmark, light tuning of a boosted model often mattered more than the choice of model class, and TabPFN had the best average performance on small datasets ([McElfresh et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02997v4)). Modern MLPs climb to the top of a living benchmark only with heavy tuning and ensembling ([TabArena preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16791)).

The conclusion is that a network fed the same per-segment numbers as the regression will, at best, tie it. The network needs to consume something the table cannot hold. That means the tree topology and the ordered profile along the centerline. The model also has to stay small. Published coronary GNNs are already in the right regime: two to three message-passing layers and 32 to 256 hidden units ([Hampe et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095121/); [Yang et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.08560)). By this report's own estimate, which is not taken from the papers, a three-layer, 32-unit network on about 20 inputs has 5,000 to 10,000 weights. A 3 × 256 network has about 140,000. At Herlev–Østerbro scale the first is plausible with heavy regularisation; the second is not.

## The recommended architecture: segments as nodes, risk factors broadcast, two heads

The design below follows the published coronary GNN recipe. It is adapted to the label unit (the SCCT segment), the patient-level covariates and the two biological processes. Each choice is labelled as either cited or this report's inference.

| Component | Recommendation | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Graph | Patient-specific tree, one node per SCCT segment that exists in that person; parent to child edges | Segment-as-node is standard ([Li et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.00386)); GCN was more robust than tree-LSTM to missing branches, 2.6% vs 6.7% loss ([Yang et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.08560)) |
| Node features | Segment type embedding, baseline plaque of that segment, curvature, arc length from the ostium, lumen calibre at a plaque-free reference | Location features gave the largest ablation drop in labelling, F1 to 0.59 ([Hampe et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095121/)) |
| Edge or child features | Bifurcation angle and daughter ratio stored on the bifurcation edge or copied to the child node | Inference: these are properties of a branching, not of a segment |
| Patient features | SCORE2 inputs, baseline plaque burden (for example segment involvement score), lumen volume to myocardial mass, scanner or protocol | Concatenated to every node (early fusion) |
| Message passing | 2 layers of GraphSAGE or GAT, about 32 hidden units, dropout, weight decay | More than three blocks gave no gain on 511 subjects ([Yang et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.08560)); GraphSAGE was best of four on 141 patients ([Li et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.00386)) |
| Optional profile encoder | Small 1D CNN over each segment's curvature and radius profile, pretrained without outcomes and frozen or last-layer tuned | CNN plus sequence encoders per branch feeding a GCN is the CPR-GCN pattern ([Yang et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.08560)) |
| Heads | Head A: incident plaque in plaque-free segments; Head B: progression in plaque-positive segments; losses masked to each head's denominator | Splitting by baseline status follows PARADIGM practice ([Lee et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/)) |
| Output link | Complementary log-log with a log(interval) offset | Inference carried over from the regression design so segment hazards sum to a participant risk |
| Loss | Unweighted binary likelihood; no resampling, class weights or focal loss | Imbalance corrections worsened calibration without improving discrimination ([van den Goorbergh et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9382395)) |
| Ensemble | 5 to 10 seeds per outer fold, mean as prediction, spread as uncertainty | Deep ensembles gave the best simple uncertainty and held up under shift ([Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2017/hash/9ef2ed4b7fd2c810847ffa5fa85bce38-Abstract.html); [Ovadia et al. 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/8558cb408c1d76621371888657d2eb1d-Abstract.html)) |

**Segments as nodes, not centerline points or mesh vertices.** The outcome is graded per segment, so the node should match the label. Point-cloud and mesh networks were built for vertex-level outputs such as wall shear stress, where Suk et al. reached a 7.6% approximation error on the artery wall ([Suk et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.05023v1)). With about 15 labels per person they are over-parameterised unless used as frozen feature extractors. Structurally absent segments, such as a missing ramus or a dominance-dependent PDA, are simply not nodes. Present but non-evaluable segments stay as nodes so that they pass messages. Their outcome is masked out of the loss, and missing inputs are imputed inside the training folds with an indicator.

**Patient covariates by concatenation, not by conditioning layers.** FiLM and DAFT were designed to inject tabular data into high-dimensional convolutional feature maps. In DAFT's own comparison on Alzheimer data, FiLM was "overall less effective" ([Pölsterl et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05990)). When the segment branch is itself five to ten numbers, a concatenation followed by a small MLP can already express every patient by segment interaction. A hierarchical patient encoder with FiLM is worth one ablation at most.

**No patient-ID embedding and no free random effect at prediction time.** Mixed-effects networks exist. LMMNN treats clusters as random effects ([Simchoni and Rosset, JMLR 2023](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/22-0501.html)), and ARMED adds an adversary that forces cluster-invariant features ([Nguyen et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10644386/)). LMMNN's classification support is described as a proof of concept, because the non-Gaussian likelihood is intractable ([LMMNN discussion](https://github.com/gsimchoni/lmmnn/issues/14)). More fundamentally, a new participant has no follow-up data, so their random intercept cannot be estimated and prediction falls back to the fixed part. The deterministic substitute for "patient susceptibility" is to feed the network what is known at baseline: the number of plaque-positive segments and total burden. A free patient embedding would leak identity under any split that is not grouped by participant.

**Condition on baseline plaque; do not predict change.** Vickers showed that analysis of covariance, with baseline as a covariate, has more power than change scores, and that percentage change performs worst ([Vickers 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC34605)). In prediction terms, the network should model follow-up status given baseline status. That handles regression to the mean and lets the baseline effect be nonlinear. A single head over all segments, with baseline plaque as an input, would be dominated by "plaque stays plaque". Its AUC would look excellent and hide the geometry signal, so it belongs only in a sensitivity analysis. Head A is where geometry is least contaminated by existing disease, and it is the primary test. If Head B uses ordered categories such as burden tertiles, CORAL gives rank-consistent ordinal outputs with shared weights ([Cao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07884v5)). Plaque composition is not a monotone severity scale and should not be coded ordinally.

## Message passing has to earn its place against hand-crafted neighbours

The case for a graph model rests on spatial dependence. That dependence is real. At pathology, **92% of thin-cap fibroatheromas and ruptured plaques clustered within two adjacent 20 mm segments** ([Am J Med 2009](https://www.amjmed.com/article/S0002-9343(08)01017-6/fulltext)). In PARADIGM, baseline plaque anywhere raised the odds of a new lesion (OR 1.84) ([Tantawy et al., EHJ-CVI 2026 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/ehjcimaging/article/27/Supplement_1/jeaf367.317/8446296)). In human flow models, including daughter branches improved wall shear stress prediction of progression over main-vessel-only reconstructions ([Sakellarios et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28606882/); [Ramasamy et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42131920/)). In labelling tasks, cutting the information flow between neighbouring nodes dropped F1 to 0.72 ([Hampe et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11095121/)). In airway trees, a GNN raised segment accuracy from 83.8% to 91.2% over a branch-only CNN ([Xie et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.04532)).

None of this shows that neighbours add value for future plaque beyond what patient-level burden already carries. A coronary tree of 15 to 20 segments is tiny, and two hops already span most of a main vessel. Much of the "neighbour" signal is likely the person-level tendency to form plaque, which a burden term captures directly. The decisive comparison is therefore a per-segment MLP that receives explicit neighbour features: plaque and geometry of the parent, children and sibling, plus patient burden excluding the segment itself. That model is a hand-crafted one-hop message pass. If the GNN does not beat it, the graph has learned nothing a feature table could not hold. This rung mirrors the "no information flow" ablation in the labelling literature, and it is the most important rung in the ladder.

Lee et al. 2020 give a reason for cautious optimism about local information. In PARADIGM, adding **per-lesion plaque features raised the C-statistic from 0.825 to 0.895** over per-patient features for progression to obstructive disease ([Lee et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32779077/)). Local information can therefore beat aggregates. Whether learned neighbour aggregation beats hand-built local features is the open question this thesis can answer.

## Pretraining is the only route to a learned geometry encoder

If the network is to learn from the curvature and radius profile, and not only from the four summaries, the encoder has to be trained mostly without outcomes. The data budget makes an outcome-trained profile encoder unstable. Self-supervised pretraining reduces label requirements in medical data ([Krishnan et al. 2022](https://dbmi.hms.harvard.edu/publication/self-supervised-learning-medicine-healthcare)). On graphs, though, naive node-level or graph-level pretraining often caused **negative transfer**. Combining node-level and graph-level objectives avoided it and gave up to 9.4 points of ROC-AUC on molecular benchmarks ([Hu et al., ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12265v2)). Reasonable pretext tasks on coronary trees follow from this. They are masked reconstruction of a segment's curvature or radius (local) and prediction of segment type or tree topology (global). No study has pretrained on coronary centerline trees and transferred to plaque, so this step would itself be novel. Its benefit has to be shown by ablation: pretrained against from scratch, and frozen against fine-tuned.

ImageCAS-X supplies about 800 curated trees for this. The Herlev–Østerbro baseline trees can also be used without touching follow-up outcomes, which has the advantage of matching the scanner and population. Two cautions apply. ImageCAS-X comes from a different population and scanner, and under dataset shift calibration degrades even with post-hoc temperature scaling ([Ovadia et al. 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/8558cb408c1d76621371888657d2eb1d-Abstract.html)). Any component transferred from it should therefore be recalibrated inside the inner folds. Second, any self-supervised use of Herlev–Østerbro scans must happen inside the outer folds or exclude test participants.

The case for geometry over image intensity is strongest here. Over ten years, scanners, reconstruction kernels and software change. Reconstruction algorithms alter volumetric and radiomic plaque parameters ([Kolossváry et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30447949/)). The short-interval reproducibility studies used the same scanner over days to months ([Schuhbaeck et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24962824/); [Lee et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30342980/)). A CNN on raw Hounsfield units would absorb scanner drift as signal. Lumen and centerline geometry is far less sensitive to it. This is the main reason not to add a per-segment image encoder in the style of CPR-GCN, even though it helped labelling (F1 0.934 without image conditions, 0.955 with them) ([Yang et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.08560)).

## Validation decides whether any gain is real

Christodoulou's review is a direct warning: machine-learning gains appeared almost only in studies with biased validation. Leakage has reversed apparent superiority over logistic regression in other fields ([Kapoor and Narayanan 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856)). Tuning and evaluating on the same cross-validation can make chance-level classifiers look good ([Varma and Simon 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1397873)). Ignoring group structure in cross-validation "results in serious underestimation of predictive error" ([Roberts et al. 2017](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/roberts-et-al-2017-ecography.pdf)). The protocol therefore follows from the evidence, not from preference.

All splits are by participant. The outer loop is repeated 5-fold, grouped by participant and stratified by participant-level outcome. The inner grouped loop handles tuning and early stopping, with the same tuning budget for every model class. Every preprocessing step (standardisation, imputation, centerline smoothing parameters) is fitted inside the folds or fixed on ImageCAS-X before outcomes are opened. Confidence intervals come from a participant-level bootstrap that resamples people with all of their segments.

The metrics have to separate "who" from "where". Segment-level AUC is inflated by between-person differences in risk. A model that only ranks people well will look good on it while localising nothing. The report should therefore give three numbers: segment-level discrimination, participant-level discrimination for any new plaque, and within-participant discrimination, which is AUC computed within each person and averaged. Only the third shows whether geometry or tree structure localises plaque. No standard within-patient metric exists for coronary segments, so the thesis must define and justify its choice. Calibration (intercept, slope and a flexible curve) is required, because complexity relative to sample size is the main threat to it ([Van Calster et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6912996)). Incremental value is estimated as paired differences in log-likelihood, Brier score and AUC, not tested with NRI. NRI gave false-positive conclusions in 18.8% to 34.4% of test-data simulations, against about 5% for the likelihood-ratio test ([Pepe et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4615606)).

The ablation ladder runs on identical participant folds and seeds:

| Rung | Model | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Regression: SCORE2 inputs + segment type + baseline plaque (segment and burden) | Reference for everything |
| 1 | Rung 0 + four geometric features, as regression, shallow XGBoost and TabPFN | Do the summaries add, and is the regression missing nonlinearity? |
| 2 | Per-segment MLP on rung 1 inputs, no graph | Does a network on the same table match the regression? |
| 3 | Rung 2 + hand-crafted parent, child and sibling features | Explicit one-hop context |
| 4 | GNN on the tree, same node inputs | Does learned message passing beat rung 3? |
| 5 | Rung 4 + pretrained profile encoder, frozen, then last-layer tuned | Does the ordered profile carry information beyond the summaries? |
| 6 | Rung 5 with shuffled profiles; rung 4 with permuted edges | Controls: order and topology, not just model capacity |
| 7 | Rung 4 single head over all segments | Sensitivity: how much "plaque stays plaque" inflates performance |

Attribution goes by refitting without each feature family, never by attention weights or marginal SHAP alone. Kernel SHAP assumes independent features and "may be very misleading" when features are correlated ([Aas et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.10464v3)). Geometry is correlated with segment type, sex and body size, which is why segment type sits in rung 0. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI and TRIPOD-Cluster, with PROBAST+AI as a self-audit ([Collins et al. 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:414e5fb8-5575-4b67-b94d-a0833a7cc58a/files/smp48sf448); [PROBAST+AI](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:8b15c087-459b-43fa-ad03-1f66ed6ba370)).

Two design questions sit outside the network but decide what it estimates. First, participants who die or are revascularised between scans do not have a comparable follow-up. The natural estimand is "partly conditional": plaque at follow-up among those alive and scanned ([Kurland and Heagerty, as summarised in PMC2812934](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2812934)). Inverse-probability-of-censoring weights in the loss are a sensitivity analysis compatible with any network. A revascularised segment can be counted as an event or excluded, and both should be run. Second, the outcome threshold must respect measurement error. Lee et al.'s 1 mm³ threshold sits below reported limits of agreement for small plaque ([Meah et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33423941/)). Sakellarios et al. instead tied progression to twice the intra-observer variability ([Sakellarios et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26985077/)). Apparent plaque regression should be counted and reported, not merged silently, since much of it will be reader variability.

## Conclusion

The question "how best to use a neural network" turns out to be a question about which structure the network may legitimately exploit. A network fed the same per-segment table as the regression is a different learner, not a different hypothesis, and the evidence says it will tie at best. A network that reads the tree and the ordered profile tests two hypotheses no published study has tested: that plaque in a segment depends on its neighbours beyond what the person's total burden implies, and that the shape of the vessel along its length matters beyond four numbers. Framed that way, the GNN is a measurement instrument whose rungs 3 to 6 isolate those two hypotheses. It is not a competitor to the regression's estimate of whether geometry adds to risk factors.

The most likely outcome, given the precedent, is that baseline plaque and risk factors carry most of the participant-level signal, and that the GNN improves within-participant discrimination slightly or not at all over hand-crafted neighbours. Both results are worth reporting. A gain confined to within-participant discrimination would mean that tree context explains where plaque forms but not who develops it, which is the same split the regression's within/between decomposition makes. Agreement between the two approaches on that split would be stronger evidence than either alone. The open quantities that set the real budget (ten-year segment event rate, intraclass correlation, the rate of non-evaluable segments) are unknown until the cohort is opened. So the architecture and ladder should be fixed, and the pipeline debugged on ImageCAS-X with synthetic outcomes, before the follow-up labels are seen.
