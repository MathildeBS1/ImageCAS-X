# Oscillatory shear rarely links geometry to plaque

None of the thesis geometry features has a well-supported link to oscillatory wall shear stress (WSS) in human coronaries. The evidence for "oscillatory shear makes coronary sites plaque-prone, independently of low time-averaged WSS (TAWSS)" is weaker still. Only one patient-specific coronary dataset relates the features directly to the oscillatory shear index (OSI), relative residence time (RRT) and the topological shear variation index (TSVI): **Gharleghi et al. 2025, 127 CAD-free left main bifurcations**. It is almost certainly the same Auckland cohort as Kashyap 2022, so it is not independent of the thesis's main curvature source. Its results cut against the usual story. **More curvature went with less high-OSI area (β −0.37 to −0.52) but more TSVI (β +0.25 to +0.38)**. The bifurcation angles had no association with any oscillatory metric. Finet's ratio predicted low TAWSS and RRT strongly but OSI only weakly, and that link vanished after adjustment. For taper and normal-range lumen size, no coronary study reports OSI at all. "Widening raises OSI" holds only in the carotid bulb and in coronary aneurysms. On the plaque side, the only systematic review calls the low/oscillatory theory "less robust than commonly assumed". In the one human serial study that tested OSI against plaque size, low-and-oscillatory sectors *regressed* (Timmins 2017) or showed no change in total plaque area (Kok 2019, same 20 patients). In pigs, OSI predicted plaque no better than low TAWSS. For the thesis, the safe position is that geometry is motivated through **low** WSS. Oscillatory or disturbed flow is often present at the same sites, but it is not an established separate pathway in human coronaries.

## Curvature: less OSI, more multidirectional shear

Curvature is the only feature with a quantitative coronary oscillatory result, and that result runs in a direction many readers would not expect. Kashyap et al. 2022 is the thesis's cited curvature source. It **computed no OSI, RRT or multidirectional metric**: its only shear output is percentage wall area with TAWSS < 0.4 Pa, from pulsatile, rigid-wall CFD in 127 patients without obstruction and with zero calcium score. Average absolute curvature had adjusted R² 0.196 (left main, LM), 0.232 (LAD) and 0.061 (LCx) against low-TAWSS area. The tortuosity index was null everywhere (whole bifurcation, p = 0.865) ([Kashyap 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8764056/)).

The OSI result comes from Gharleghi et al. 2025 in Sci Rep, read in full text. It used 127 patients with suspected CAD and no stenosis or calcification, from the same Auckland group and with the same pulsatile, rigid, Carreau–Yasuda pipeline. Analysis was restricted to 10 mm around the LM bifurcation. "Tortuosity" there means mean centreline curvature, not the tortuosity index. Curvature was associated with **less high-OSI area (OSI > 0.1): standardised β LM −0.367, LAD −0.484, LCx −0.519, all p < 0.001**. It was also associated with less high-RRT area in the LAD (−0.269) and LCx (−0.368), but with **more mean TSVI (LM +0.249, LAD +0.376, LCx +0.333)** ([Gharleghi 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11697190/)). Curvature had no significant association with low-TAWSS area (< 0.5 Pa) in that analysis. Kashyap's positive curvature–low-TAWSS link in the same patients used a wider region and a 0.4 Pa threshold, so the two papers show how much region and threshold choices matter. Two further caveats apply. Women had smaller and more curved arteries, so curvature and diameter are confounded, and diameter itself was strongly associated with high OSI (β ≈ +0.7). The multivariable coefficients (Table 3) could not be read, so it is unconfirmed whether curvature survives adjustment for diameter. The two papers must count as **one cohort, not two replications**. The shared cohort is inferred from identical n, group and pipeline; neither paper states it.

An idealised parametric study points the same way. In population-based LAD–diagonal models, a tighter bend (smaller curvature radius) gave more low-TAWSS area but **less high-OSI and high-RRT area** ([Chiastra 2017, abstract](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/handle/11583/2677971/158355/SB3C2017_Abstract_IdealizedCoronary.pdf)). The idealised coronary S-bend of Johnston & Johnston 2009 did compute OSI. It found low WSS and high OSI coinciding on the inner bend, with OSI values only about 0.04–0.13 at the worst points ([Johnston & Johnston 2009, MODSIM proceedings](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)). One of the research notes could not identify this paper; the taper notes located it as a conference paper, not a journal article. Myers et al. 2001 had already noted in a realistic right coronary model that OSI "was very small and was attributed to flow reversal in the waveform, rather than separation" ([Myers 2001, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.1349703)).

### Reconciling the two Zhang versions

Zhang et al. used 39 ASOCA left coronary trees (20 non-stenosed, 19 stenosed). The two research notes read different versions. The curvature notes read the **published R Soc Open Sci 2024 full text**, the version the thesis cites. In it, %HighOSI@0.1 was higher in stenosed trees for the whole tree and for non-bifurcating segments (p < 0.001) but not at bifurcations. TSVI was higher everywhere (whole tree 186 ± 40 vs 137 ± 25, p < 0.001). ROC AUCs were TSVI 0.876, %HighOSI 0.813 (cut-off 0.6% of wall area) and curvature 0.711 ([Zhang 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11416812/)). The bifurcation notes read the **arXiv preprint 2312.00257**. It reports no TSVI, highlights the null bifurcation OSI difference (p = 0.116) and the null angle and Finet differences, warns that OSI values were very small, and gives a negative region-level correlation between %HighOSI and %LowTAESS (r = −0.413) ([Zhang preprint](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.00257)). The bifurcation results agree between the two versions; the emphasis differs. **The published version takes precedence.** The OSI–low-TAESS correlation and the preprint p-values for angle and Finet's ratio should not be cited unless they are confirmed in the published paper or its appendix. In neither version is curvature correlated with OSI. The study compares curvature by stenosis group and OSI by stenosis group separately, cross-sectionally, with no adjustment for the large sex imbalance (60% female non-stenosed vs 14–17% stenosed). Stenosis can also alter both curvature and flow.

## Torsion and the tortuosity index: helicity, not oscillation

For torsion, the coronary evidence runs through helical flow. In 30 swine coronaries, torsion dictated helical flow (r = 0.64), and helicity intensity was strongly inversely associated with "unfavourable" WSS (r = −0.91). Whether that composite included OSI was not verified, because the full text was inaccessible ([De Nisco 2019, abstract](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2726967)). In humans, the only direct torsion–oscillatory number is Shen et al. 2025 (R Soc Open Sci, same 39 ASOCA trees). LAD torsion was correlated with RRT% at ρ = −0.46 (p = 0.049), which became **Holm-adjusted p = 1.0**, and no curvature- or torsion–OSI correlation survived correction ([Shen 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12324882/fullTextXML)). This Shen 2025 paper is distinct from the excluded Shen 2026 arXiv geometry paper. Vorobtsova et al. 2016 showed that zero-torsion idealised models and 23 patient-based tortuous arteries reach opposite conclusions: in real arteries, tortuosity raised helicity and WSS ([Vorobtsova 2016, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10439-015-1492-3)). Idealised curved-tube OSI results therefore should not be carried over to real coronaries. For the tortuosity index (arc/chord), no coronary study reporting OSI was found. In the carotid, more tortuosity *reduced* disturbed-flow area ([Bijari 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3371282)).

## Bifurcation geometry: the angle is null, the diameter split acts through low shear

The claim that a wide bifurcation angle raises OSI at the lateral walls rests on idealised models and secondary citations. In Gharleghi 2025, the **LM–LAD angle (124 ± 20°), the LAD–LCx angle (79 ± 19°) and the inflow angle were not associated with high OSI, high RRT, TSVI or low TAESS (all |β| < 0.1, adjusted p > 0.3)** ([Gharleghi 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11697190/)). Two idealised parametric studies call the angle effect "minor" ([Beier 2016 record](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/96776); [Chiastra 2017](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/99510)). Both are abstract-only, and Beier's "adverse" metrics were not verified. The positive "wider angle, more OSI" evidence comes from Zheng et al. 2016: integrated OSI rose 1.07 → 1.33 → 1.53 (normalised) from 70° to 110°, but only in **idealised, Newtonian, stented** LM models with no native geometry ([Zheng 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5061990/fullTextXML)). The early Chaichana models reported low WSS and WSS gradient at wide angles; no OSI was seen in what was read ([Chaichana 2011 record](https://hira.hope.ac.uk/id/eprint/1505/)).

The diameter split matters more, but mainly for low TAWSS. In Gharleghi 2025, **Finet's ratio predicted %lowTAESS (β −0.842) and %highRRT (β −0.488) strongly, but %highOSI only weakly (β −0.187, adjusted p = 0.247)**. Absolute diameters showed the reverse pattern: strongly associated with high OSI (β ≈ 0.69–0.74), not with low TAESS ([Gharleghi 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11697190/)). The diameter–OSI link may partly be built in, because inflow was scaled from LM diameter with a generic waveform, so larger arteries get higher Reynolds and Womersley numbers. That is an inference, not the authors' claim. Garcha & Grande Gutiérrez 2025 is the thesis's cited source for the LAD/LCx split. It **computed no OSI, RRT, transverse WSS or TSVI**, only low- and high-TAWSS area, in 230 synthetic rigid trees ([Garcha 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11770140/)). Its large split effect (low-TAWSS area 1.7% at a neutral ratio vs 6.8% and 16.7% at skewed ratios) cannot be cited for oscillation. Its direction labelling is also inconsistent between Methods and Results, so the figures should be checked. In idealised Murray-law work, the size of the low-WSS region depends on the expansion ratio and not on the daughter-to-daughter ratio; no OSI result is given in the abstract ([Doutel 2016](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/handle/10216/97837?mode=full)). No study regresses OSI on the LM→LAD or LM→LCx ratio as the thesis defines them.

## Taper and lumen size: only carotid and aneurysm evidence

No coronary study relates normal-range taper or lumen diameter to OSI, RRT or transverse WSS in non-diseased arteries. Johnston & Johnston 2009 is the only cited taper source that computed OSI, and its result **does not support "taper creates oscillation"**. Taper moved the low-WSS minimum into the straight proximal part, but OSI there was only "moderate". The authors attribute it to the inlet waveform, since "there are no eddies there". The low-WSS finding itself partly reflects a flat inlet profile and developing flow ([Johnston & Johnston 2009](https://www.mssanz.org.au/modsim09/B4/johnston_bm.pdf)). The idea that normal taper suppresses separation is fluid-mechanics reasoning. The explicit claim that it "limits flow separation" was found only in a patent ([USPTO 6605053](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6605053)), and in canine femoral simulations the effect of taper on reverse shear depended on the assumed inlet profile ([Banerjee 1995, abstract](https://content.iospress.com/articles/biorheology/bir32-6-10)).

The "widening raises oscillation" evidence lies elsewhere. In 50 normal carotid bifurcations (patient-specific, rigid, Newtonian), bulb flare increased disturbed-flow area. **OSI was the metric flare explained least: adjusted R² 0.388, vs 0.549 for RRT** ([Bijari 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3371282)). In coronaries, OSI rises only with pathological dilation: from 0.01 to 0.30 in idealised fusiform aneurysms at 1× to 5× diameter, in a non-specialist journal ([Wang 2020, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/hin/complx/8507273.html)). In 34 patients with coronary ectasia, OSI, RRT and transverse WSS were higher in thrombosed ectasia but were not regressed on diameter ([Sun 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12669196/)). Neither result transfers to between-person variation in taper within plaque-free arteries.

## OSI as an independent plaque driver is unproven in coronaries

OSI started as a carotid autopsy correlate ([Ku 1985, abstract](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/Pulsatile-flow-and-atherosclerosis-in-the-human-carotid-bifurcation-Positive-correlation-between-plaque-location-and-low-oscillating-shear-stress-/f34bbc20-c572-41c2-82f5-d3b861a0f760)). There, the oscillation index (r = 0.82) correlated with intimal thickness no better than 1/mean shear (r = 0.82). OSI has TAWSS magnitude in its denominator, so "low" and "oscillatory" are partly the same signal by construction. The only systematic review (Peiffer, Sherwin & Weinberg 2013, 27 CFD studies) found that **every point-by-point WSS–wall-thickness comparison failed**, and concluded the evidence is "less robust than commonly assumed" ([Peiffer 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3695746)). Nearly all of that evidence is cross-sectional co-location with lesions already present.

Human serial evidence for OSI is small, short and in diseased arteries. In 20 Emory patients with stable CAD on atorvastatin 80 mg, followed with VH-IVUS for 6 months, **low-and-oscillatory sectors showed plaque regression and low non-oscillatory sectors showed progression (p < 0.001)** ([Timmins 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28148771)). The same cohort's OSI showed no total-plaque-area effect, only composition shifts toward necrotic core and calcium ([Kok 2019](https://eurointervention.pcronline.com/article/the-influence-of-multidirectional-shear-stress-on-plaque-progression-and-composition-changes-in-human-coronary-arteries)). These are one cohort, not two studies. The thesis's Clinical Background also cites Samady 2011, which was not retrieved; it may belong to the same Emory programme. In ACS non-culprit arteries, high TSVI went with more atheroma-volume progression at one year, presented as an addition to low TAWSS. That evidence is abstract-only and from one group ([De Nisco 2024](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2988982)). In familial-hypercholesterolaemic pigs (10 animals, 3,648 sectors), low TAWSS co-located with high OSI in 63% of sectors. PPV for plaque was 48% for low TAWSS vs 45% for high OSI, and **the authors concluded low TAWSS remains the strongest driver and predictor** ([Hoogendoorn 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7177495)). In rabbit aortas, transverse WSS, not low or oscillatory WSS, matched lesion location ([Mohamied 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4286626)). The 2019 ESC consensus describes the mechanism as "low and/or oscillatory" but cites clinical evidence only for low (and high) TAWSS. The large human prospective study it relies on (PREDICTION, n = 506 ACS) tested low WSS only ([Gijsen 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/153395/1/ehz551.pdf)). OSI is also fragile as a modelled quantity. Between static and moving RCA models, TAWSS agreed at r = 0.89–0.95 but OSI only at r = 0.38–0.60 ([Bristol record](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-dynamic-vessel-motion-on-haemodynamic-parameters-in/)). High-OSI area covered only 3.08 ± 2.36% of the LM bifurcation wall in Gharleghi 2025, and Gharleghi and Zhang both ask readers to interpret OSI cautiously. No study tests any WSS metric against incident plaque in plaque-free coronaries, and none uses CCTA with long follow-up.

## Conclusion

No thesis feature can be argued, on current evidence, to act *through oscillatory shear* rather than low shear. Curvature is the closest candidate, and only in the multidirectional sense: in the one cohort, more curvature meant more TSVI but less high-OSI area. So "curvature raises oscillatory shear" would be wrong. "Curvature is associated with more multidirectional shear in one simulation cohort" is defensible, with the TSVI and single-cohort caveats. The bifurcation angles have a direct null for OSI. The LM→LAD and LM→LCx ratios act, as far as the evidence goes, through low TAWSS and RRT, and RRT largely tracks low TAWSS. For taper, any oscillatory argument is a carotid analogy. The thesis's two cited simulation sources for curvature and the diameter split, Kashyap and Garcha, report no OSI, so they cannot support an oscillatory claim.

For wording, the Clinical Background sentence "Plaque forms and grows preferentially where the WSS is low or oscillatory" (citing Chatzizisis 2007, Samady 2011 and Yamamoto 2017) overstates the oscillatory part as human coronary evidence. A safer version: *"Plaque develops preferentially where WSS is low; at bends and bifurcations, the flow there is often also disturbed, and the WSS can change direction over the cardiac cycle \cite{chatzizisis2007ess}, although whether this oscillation adds to the effect of low WSS in human coronary arteries has not been established \cite{peiffer2013}."* This keeps Chatzizisis for the mechanism and Peiffer for the limits of the evidence. In the literature review, keep the premise sentence as it is now: geometry → **low** WSS → plaque. Do not add OSI as a second pathway for any feature. The Chatzizisis 2007 wording itself was not re-read in these notes and should be checked before it is quoted.
