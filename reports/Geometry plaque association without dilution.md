# One test, read on two scales

The best presentation keeps the segment-level result as the main result and shows the person-level result as a translation of the same fitted model, not as a second analysis. The main result is the one prespecified likelihood ratio (LR) test of the geometry block and the geometry hazard ratios per SD with 95% CI, conditional on the person random intercept. The person-level result is then a set of model-based contrasts in predicted person risk, integrated over the random intercept, for explicitly named shifts: "all segments one SD higher" and "one named segment one SD higher". The "dilution" is not a loss of information. Conditional on the person, the pooled risk is again a complementary log-log (cloglog) model whose hazard is the sum of the nine segment hazards, so a shift in all segments keeps the hazard ratio e^β, while a shift in one segment multiplies the person hazard by only 1 + w(e^β − 1), where w is that segment's share of the person's hazard. With nine similar segments, a segment hazard ratio of 1.30 becomes about 1.03 at person level. This formula is the researcher's own derivation and no source was found for it, so it must be checked numerically on the fitted model. There is exactly one test. ΔC and calibration stay secondary estimates, and a small person-level ΔC next to a clear segment-level hazard ratio is the expected pattern, not a contradiction.

Evidence labels used below: **established method** (published methodology), **guideline** (reporting guideline or consensus), **applied practice** (what individual papers did), **opinion** (blog or slides) and **own derivation** (algebra or argument made in the research notes or in this report, not taken from a source). Several sources were read only as abstracts or through secondary sources; these are named in the limitations section.

## The single LR test is already the test of the pooled person risk

The design has one test: the LR test that compares the pooled segment-level cloglog GLMM with the six risk factors against the same model with the geometry block added. No second, person-level test is needed, and none should be added. Three facts make the LR test a person-level-pooled test already. These are **own derivations** from the model definition, not statements from a source.

First, the person is the independent unit of the likelihood. The likelihood is a product over persons, and each person's contribution is the probability of that person's nine segment outcomes together, integrated over the person random intercept. The segments are never treated as independent observations.

Second, the person risk has no parameters of its own. Conditional on the person intercept u, and under the model's assumption that segments are independent given u, the probability that at least one segment develops plaque is

1 − ∏(1 − p_s) = 1 − exp(−e^u · Σ_s H_s), with H_s = exp(η_s),

so cloglog(person risk | u) = u + log Σ_s exp(η_s). The person-level model is the same cloglog model with the segment hazards summed. Pooling is a calculation on the fitted coefficients; nothing is re-estimated.

Third, the null hypothesis is the same on both scales. If all geometry coefficients are zero, no segment hazard depends on geometry, and so neither does the summed person hazard nor the person risk. If a coefficient is not zero, the person risk depends on geometry. "The geometry block adds nothing at segment level" and "geometry adds nothing to the pooled person risk beyond the six risk factors" are the same hypothesis in this model.

One limit on this claim must be stated. The LR test is **not** a test computed on the person-level yes/no outcome. It uses which segments developed plaque, not only whether the person did. That is why it keeps the information a person-level-only analysis would lose, and it is also why the thesis must not describe it as "a person-level test of geometry". The accurate wording is "the test of the geometry block in the pooled model, from which the person risk is computed".

The choice of the LR test as the main inferential result has support, at different levels of authority. PROGRESS 2 (**guideline**, read in the source) asks prognostic factor studies for multivariable analyses of a factor's value over existing factors, effect sizes with confidence intervals and a prespecified analysis plan, and makes no recommendation about the C statistic ([Riley et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1001380)). Harrell calls the LR χ² test the "gold standard" for added value and comparing two C indexes "a low-power procedure" (**opinion**, a blog post that is not peer reviewed) ([Harrell](https://www.fharrell.com/post/addvalue/)).

## The segment-level result is conditional, blended and scale-dependent, and the table must say so

The geometry hazard ratios are the undiluted result. Three properties decide how they can be worded.

**They are conditional on the person intercept.** A hazard ratio from the GLMM compares two segments of persons with the same risk factors and the same random intercept. It is not a population-averaged effect. For the logit link there is a known approximate conversion, β_marginal ≈ β_conditional/√(1 + 0.346σ²) (**established method**, read in Austin and Merlo and in Pavlou et al., who attribute it to Zeger et al. 1988, which was not read) ([Austin & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471); [Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751)). **That constant belongs to the logistic distribution and must not be applied to the cloglog model.** For the cloglog link with a normal random intercept, one preprint states that no closed-form adjustment exists and numerical integration is needed ([arXiv:1610.01526](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1610.01526)); no source giving a constant was found. The same preprint states that with a log link the slopes have both a conditional and a marginal reading. Since the cloglog link is close to the log link when risks are small, the notes infer that attenuation of the segment-level coefficients is small when segment risks are low. That is an analogy (**own derivation**), not a published cloglog result. The practical consequence: report the conditional hazard ratio as the estimate, label it conditional, and give marginal quantities as integrated predicted risks, not as a rescaled coefficient.

**For curvature and taper the single coefficient is a blend.** In a random-intercept model the coefficient of a covariate that varies within the cluster is a weighted average of its between-cluster and within-cluster associations (**established method**, but read only at abstract level) ([Neuhaus & Kalbfleisch 1998](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/between-and-within-cluster-covariate-effects-in-the-analysis-of-clustered-data/812074010140475393-922)). The hazard ratio for curvature therefore describes "a segment with one SD higher curvature, same segment position, same risk factors and same person intercept", whether the difference lies between two people or between two segments of one person. "Within the same person" overstates it, and so does "between persons". This matches the decision that the where reading is descriptive and that β mixes both kinds of information.

**Terms that are constant within a person are the weakest under conditional wording.** Austin and Merlo recommend population-averaged estimates for cluster-level covariates, since a cluster-specific ratio for a variable that never varies within a cluster compares states that no cluster is observed in (**established method**, read in the source) ([Austin & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575471)). The bifurcation angle and the diameter ratios are of this kind. Their hazard ratios still belong in the main table, but their interpretation should lean on the predicted person risks of the next section.

**"Per SD" needs a stated SD.** No guideline was found that fixes the scaling; PROGRESS 2 asks only that continuous factors stay continuous and that non-linearity is examined. The notes argue (**own derivation**, arithmetic plus an argument about the model) that the model has segment intercepts, so the variation that identifies β is the variation within a segment position. A hazard ratio per pooled SD over all nine segments would then express β over a contrast, such as proximal versus distal curvature, that the model does not use, and would look larger than the contrast the data support. The within-position SD is the scaling most consistent with the model. This should be verified by decomposing the variance of curvature and taper into between-position, between-person and residual parts. Whatever is chosen, the table should give each SD in physical units and a per-unit hazard ratio in a second column, so that a reader can rescale. Changing the SD changes the number, not the model and not the LR test.

**A yardstick that pooling cannot dilute.** The median hazard ratio, MHR = exp(√(2σ²)·0.6745) = exp(0.954σ), was defined for Cox frailty models with normal random effects, with the advice to set it next to the covariate hazard ratios (**established method**, read in the source) ([Austin, Wagner & Merlo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5299617)). The cloglog coefficients and the person intercept are on the log-hazard scale, so the same formula carries over by the same argument. That transfer is an **own derivation**; no source defines the MHR for a cloglog GLMM. It can be checked by drawing many pairs of intercepts from the fitted normal distribution and taking the median of the larger-to-smaller hazard ratio. Its use is one sentence: the typical hazard ratio between two people with the same covariates is X, and the geometry hazard ratio per SD is Y. Harrell's fraction of new information, 1 − LR_base/LR_full (**opinion**), is a second such measure; it sits directly on the prespecified test and does not depend on pooling, on the conditional or marginal choice, or on the SD.

REMARK items 16 and 17 (**guideline**, read through a search summary quoting the items; written for tumour markers) ask for effect estimates with confidence intervals for the marker and all other variables in the model, whatever their significance ([Altman et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362085)). The main table should therefore show all ten covariates, not geometry alone.

## The person-level result is the same β seen through a hazard share

Everything in this section follows from the summed-hazard form above. The algebra is the researcher's **own derivation**. The notes describe it as the grouped-time proportional hazards property applied across segments instead of time intervals, but the standard references for that property (Prentice and Gloeckler 1978, Allison 1982) were not read, and no source was found that writes out this pooling across anatomical sites.

A covariate that takes the same value in all nine rows of a person factors out of the sum. Its person-level hazard ratio, conditional on u, equals the segment-level hazard ratio exactly. This holds for the six risk factors. It holds for a geometry term only if the term enters all nine rows. A covariate that is raised in only some segments multiplies the person hazard by

1 + W(e^β − 1), where W is the summed hazard share of the shifted segments, w_s = H_s/Σ_t H_t.

The table shows what this does to a segment hazard ratio of 1.30. The numbers are arithmetic illustrations, not results.

| Contrast | Hazard share W | Person-level hazard ratio (conditional on u) |
|---|---|---|
| One segment shifted, nine similar segments | 1/9 | 1.03 |
| One segment shifted, a segment with a quarter of the person's hazard | 0.25 | 1.08 |
| All nine segments shifted | 1 | 1.30 |

On the risk scale the same contrast looks like this, for a person whose conditional risk is 40%: one segment shifted gives 41.0%, all segments shifted gives 48.5%. Again an illustration with made-up inputs.

Two things follow. The "dilution" is a property of the chosen contrast, so both contrasts must be shown and each must be named. And the share w_s is larger in segments where plaque is common, so the same local difference in curvature counts for more at person level in a high-hazard segment than in a low-hazard one. The one-segment contrast should therefore be shown for a named segment, for instance the proximal LAD, and not for "a segment".

**Person risks must be integrated over the random intercept.** The marginal person risk is 1 − E_u[exp(−e^u Σ_s H_s)], a one-dimensional integral. Two shortcuts are wrong. Setting u = 0 gives the risk of a median person, not the average risk; in simulations for the logit link this gave calibration slopes below 1 (0.788 at an intraclass correlation of 0.30), and the authors state that integrating over the random effects is the correct way (**established method**, read in the source, logit link only) ([Pavlou et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4525751)). Pooling marginal segment risks as 1 − ∏(1 − p̄_s) is also wrong, since segments of one person share u; this overstates person risk (**own derivation**). The thesis should state which type of prediction is shown: for a new person drawn from the population, with the intercept integrated out ([Skrondal & Rabe-Hesketh 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v172y2009i3p659-687.html), abstract only).

**After integration, even the all-segments contrast is no longer exactly e^β.** The hazard ratio e^β holds conditional on u. The marginal risks of two groups do not differ by exactly that hazard ratio, and with a normal intercept there is no closed form. This is the reason to show the person-level result as predicted risks and risk differences, and to keep the hazard ratio for the conditional statement.

The person-level measures to show are then, all computed from the one fitted model: the average predicted person risk with a geometry term set one SD below and one SD above its mean (or at the 10th and 90th percentile), other covariates as observed, integrated over u, with the difference; this for "all segments shifted" and for "one named segment shifted" in the case of curvature and taper; and a curve of predicted person risk against each geometry term. These are model-based predictions for groups of people who differ in geometry. They are not effects of changing a person's geometry and they are not risks for use in a clinic. Intervals can come from a bootstrap that resamples persons or from parametric simulation; no source read prescribes one.

**ΔC and calibration.** Curvature and taper enter the person risk only through the hazard-weighted sum over segments, which is dominated by the high-hazard segments. Person-level discrimination therefore uses mostly the between-person part of these covariates (**own derivation**). The literature also explains why ΔC is small for a real factor: an odds ratio of 3 still gives a poor classifier (Pepe et al., **established method**, abstract and secondary source) ([Pepe et al. 2004](https://edrn.nci.nih.gov/data-and-resources/publications/15105181-919-limitations-of-the-odds-ratio-in-gauging-the-performance-of-a-diagnostic-prognostic-or-screening-marker)), and the AUC is described as insensitive for model comparison ([Cook 2007](https://ncook.bwh.harvard.edu/), search summary). This cuts both ways. A small ΔC does not show that geometry is unimportant, and a clear hazard ratio does not show that geometry improves prediction. Reporting ΔC with an interval and without a p-value agrees with the decision already made; lecture slides by Kerr call p-values for ΔAUC invalid (**opinion**) ([Kerr](https://si.biostat.washington.edu/sites/default/files/modules/Kerr_SISCER2023mod5_part3_update12July2023.pdf)). C and calibration should be computed from cross-validated, u-integrated predictions, at person level and at segment level, with a within-person C as the descriptive where reading. Harrell's back-to-back histograms of predicted risk under the base and the full model show the added spread in person risk more directly than ΔC does (**opinion**).

## Proposed layout

**Main table (segment level, the main result).** Ten rows: the six risk factors and the four geometry covariates. Columns: level of measurement (person, artery or segment, and which rows of the model the term enters); the SD used, in physical units; hazard ratio per SD with 95% CI; hazard ratio per physical unit. Below the rows: the LR test of the geometry block (χ², df, p), marked as the one prespecified test; the SD of the person intercept with its median hazard ratio; the fraction of new information. Table note: "Hazard ratios are conditional on the person random intercept. For curvature and taper the hazard ratio combines differences between persons and between segments of one person."

**Figure 1 (segment level).** Forest plot of the four geometry hazard ratios per SD, with the six risk factors for scale and the median hazard ratio as a reference line.

**Person-level table (the translation).** One row per geometry term and contrast. For curvature and taper: "all segments one SD higher" and "the proximal LAD one SD higher". For each term that enters only some rows: the contrast over exactly those rows. Columns: the hazard share W of the shifted segments (mean and range over persons); the person-level hazard ratio conditional on u, from 1 + W(e^β − 1); the average predicted person risk, integrated over u, at the low and the high value; the risk difference with interval. No p-values in this table.

**Figure 2 (person level).** Predicted person risk, integrated over u, against each geometry term, with a band. For curvature and taper two curves: all segments shifted and one named segment shifted.

**Secondary table.** C, calibration slope and calibration-in-the-large for the base and the full model, at person level and at segment level, with ΔC and an interval; within-person C; no p-values. Calibration plots at both levels from cross-validated, u-integrated predictions. Optionally the back-to-back histograms of predicted person risk.

**Optional descriptive display.** The variance of curvature and taper split into between-position, between-person and residual parts. This supports the SD choice and shows how much of each covariate can act between persons.

**Example sentences** (X, Y and Z stand for the fitted numbers):

> Adding the four geometry covariates to the six established risk factors improved the fit of the pooled model (likelihood ratio χ² = X, 4 df, p = Y). This is the only test of geometry in the thesis; the person-level results below are computed from the same fitted model.

> A segment with one SD higher curvature (X mm⁻¹, the SD within segment position) had a hazard ratio for incident coronary plaque of Y (95% CI ... to ...), comparing segments at the same position in persons with the same risk factors and the same random intercept.

> The pooled person risk depends on the sum of the nine segment hazards. One SD higher curvature in all nine segments therefore corresponds to the same hazard ratio, Y, for developing plaque in any segment, whereas one SD higher curvature in the proximal LAD alone corresponds to a person-level hazard ratio of Z, since that segment carries on average W of a person's hazard.

> Averaged over the random intercept, the predicted proportion who developed plaque was A% with curvature one SD below the mean in all segments and B% with curvature one SD above. These are model-based predictions for groups that differ in geometry, not the effect of changing geometry in a person.

> The hazard ratio between two persons with the same covariates was typically X (median hazard ratio); the geometry hazard ratios were smaller/larger than this.

> The C statistic of the pooled person risk changed by X (interval ... to ...) when geometry was added. A change of this size is expected for a factor with a hazard ratio of this magnitude and was not tested.

**Statements to avoid.**

| Avoid | Reason |
|---|---|
| "A person-level test confirmed..." or any second p-value for the person-level result | There is one test; the person-level numbers are estimates from the same model |
| "The association was weaker at person level" | The coefficient is the same; only the one-segment contrast is smaller |
| "Within the same person, a segment with higher curvature..." | β blends between-person and within-person information |
| Reading the conditional hazard ratio as a population-averaged effect | It is conditional on the person intercept |
| Applying the logit constant 0.346 to convert the hazard ratios | It belongs to the logistic model |
| "Geometry increases, raises or leads to plaque", "independent of confounders", risk-change wording for the predicted risks | Adjusted associations, not causal estimates |
| "Geometry improves prediction", on the strength of a hazard ratio | A hazard ratio does not show classification ability |
| "Geometry is unimportant", on the strength of a small ΔC | ΔC is insensitive |
| Person risks pooled with u = 0, or from marginal segment risks multiplied as if independent | Both give wrong person risks |
| A one-segment or all-segment contrast without saying which it is | The two differ by the hazard share |
| Comparing per-SD hazard ratios with other studies without the SD in units | The SD is cohort- and definition-specific |
| Presenting the predicted person risk as a tool, or claiming TRIPOD-Cluster compliance | The thesis is a prognostic factor study, not a risk model |

## Limitations of this report

**Derivations to verify on the fitted model.** The hazard-share formula 1 + W(e^β − 1): compute the conditional person hazard directly with one segment shifted and compare. The median hazard ratio for a cloglog GLMM: simulate pairs of intercepts. The scaling argument that the within-position SD is the right one: decompose the variance. The claim that segment-level attenuation is small: integrate the segment risks over u and compare the implied marginal contrast with e^β. The claim that person-level ΔC reflects mostly between-person information: compare person-level and within-person C. None of these was found in a source.

**Sources not read in the original.** Read only as abstract, search summary or through a secondary source: Zeger, Liang and Albert 1988; Neuhaus and Kalbfleisch 1998; Begg and Parides 2003; Wang and Louis 2003; Coull et al. 2006; Skrondal and Rabe-Hesketh 2009; Hedeker et al. 2000; Pepe et al. 2004; Cook 2007; Pencina et al. 2008 and 2012; REMARK (items quoted in a search summary); TRIPOD-Cluster; the ophthalmology tutorials by Ying et al. and Glynn and Rosner. The statement on the cloglog link with normal random effects comes from a preprint (arXiv:1610.01526), whose author list the notes mark as unchecked. Nakagawa et al. 2017 was read as a bioRxiv version. Harrell and Kerr are a blog and lecture slides. The PROSPECT numbers in the notes come from a news report, not the NEJM article, and are not used here.

**Applied practice is thin.** Coronary papers such as PROSPECT show lesion-level and patient-level predictors as separate blocks of one table (**applied practice**, secondary source), which is compatible with the main table proposed here but is not a recommendation. No paper was found that pools site-level risks to a person risk by 1 − ∏(1 − p_s) and reports both levels, and no guideline was found that says whether the conditional or the marginal estimate should lead in a prognostic factor study.

**Topics not covered.** Not read: Prentice and Gloeckler 1978, Allison 1982, Singer and Willett; Neuhaus et al. 1991 and Neuhaus and Jewell 1993; the non-collapsibility literature; Larsen and Merlo 2005 on the median odds ratio; Greenland et al. 1986 on standardised coefficients; Harrell's book on interquartile-range effects; TRIPOD 2015, TRIPOD+AI, STROBE, QUIPS, CHARMS, PROGRESS 1 and 3; the marginal standardisation literature; the debate on R² for binary GLMMs. Latent-scale R² and variance partition with the cloglog constant π²/6 ([Nakagawa et al. 2017](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/095851v4.full)) are possible but were left out of the layout: the latent variable is asymmetric and the quantity is not a share of variance in observed plaque. A refit with person-mean and deviation terms for curvature and taper is an accepted technique for separating the blend; it is mentioned here only as a possible labelled sensitivity display and is not part of the fixed design.

**The current code differs from the decided design.** A check of `CGPS_data/progression_who_pooled.py` showed the following, which affects what can be written. The person risk is already integrated over the random intercept by Gauss–Hermite quadrature, and curvature is already standardised within segment type, both in line with this report. The geometry block in the script holds two terms (mean turning and the bifurcation angle), not four. The bifurcation angle enters only the proximal LAD and proximal LCx rows, so its person-level hazard ratio is not e^β but 1 + (w_pLAD + w_pLCx)(e^β − 1); the same question (which rows) must be settled for the diameter ratios before their person-level contrast is defined. The base still defaults to log SCORE2 and holds log follow-up, dominance, log length and baseline grade. The interval reported for ΔC is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile over cross-validation repeats, which describes variation between repeats and is not a confidence interval; it should be labelled as such or replaced by a person-level bootstrap interval.

## Conclusion

The worry about dilution dissolves once the two results are seen as one model on two scales. The coefficient is never diluted; what shrinks is a specific contrast, one segment out of nine, and it shrinks by a factor that can be computed and shown. The honest presentation therefore states the segment-level hazard ratio once, as conditional and blended, and then says exactly which shift in geometry each person-level number refers to.

The open work is numerical, not conceptual. Before any of this is written into the thesis, the share formula, the median hazard ratio and the SD choice should be checked on the fitted model, and the rows that each geometry term enters should be fixed, since that alone decides whether a term's person-level hazard ratio equals its segment-level one.
