\subsection{Turning-Angle Curvature}
\label{sec:method-curvature}

The curvature of each trunk (Section~\ref{sec:method-split}) was estimated from the turning angle at every centerline point (Figure~\ref{fig:method-turning}).
For a point $\mathbf{P}_i$, the points $\mathbf{B}_i$ before it and $\mathbf{F}_i$ after it were placed on the centerline at a straight-line distance of exactly $\ell = 5$\,mm, by linear interpolation between the two centerline points on either side of that distance, and the turning angle between the arriving and the leaving chord was computed as
\begin{equation}
  \theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}\bigl(\lVert \mathbf{a}_i \times \mathbf{b}_i \rVert,\; \mathbf{a}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_i\bigr),
  \qquad \mathbf{a}_i = \mathbf{P}_i - \mathbf{B}_i,\quad \mathbf{b}_i = \mathbf{F}_i - \mathbf{P}_i .
  \label{eq:method-turn}
\end{equation}
The chords have the same length at every point, so every angle spans the same distance along the vessel although the centerline points are unevenly spaced, and centring the chords on each point makes the angles independent of where along the artery the measurement starts.
The two-argument arctangent remains accurate for nearly straight chords, where the cosine of the angle is close to 1, and the angle is unsigned, between $0$ and $\pi$, as each bend of a 3D vessel lies in its own plane.
A positional error of size $\sigma$ in the centerline points tilts a chord of length $\ell$ by about $\sigma/\ell$, so the false turning per millimeter it causes grows as $\sigma/\ell^2$, and the chord of 5\,mm, the value adopted for coronary curvature on CCTA by van Zandwijk et al.\ \cite{vanzandwijk2019curvature}, suppresses this noise at the cost of not resolving bends shorter than a few millimeters.
No smoothing was applied beyond the chord.
Points closer than 5\,mm to either end of the trunk lack one of the chords and were given no value.

Dividing each angle by the chord length gives the curvature profile
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_i = \frac{\theta_i}{\ell},
  \label{eq:method-profile}
\end{equation}
the turning per millimeter at arc length $s_i$ along the trunk, in rad/mm.
The profile was computed on the whole trunk and then divided at the segment boundaries, so that an angle near a boundary uses chords that reach into the neighboring segment.
The feature of a segment $s$ is its mean curvature, the profile averaged over the arc length it covers,
\begin{equation}
  T_s = \frac{1}{s_{\mathrm{last}} - s_{\mathrm{first}}} \int_{s_{\mathrm{first}}}^{s_{\mathrm{last}}} \kappa(s)\,\mathrm{d}s ,
  \label{eq:method-tl}
\end{equation}
where $s_{\mathrm{first}}$ and $s_{\mathrm{last}}$ are the arc lengths of the first and last profile point in the segment, and the integral was evaluated with the trapezoid rule over the profile points.
Weighting by arc length rather than by point prevents densely sampled stretches from dominating, and keeps $T_s$ in rad/mm.
A segment with fewer than two profile points was recorded as missing.
Mean curvature was preferred to the tortuosity index, as it was the measure most closely related to low wall shear stress in patients without coronary artery disease, whereas the tortuosity index showed no correlation \cite{kashyap2022tortuosity}.

The feature was adapted from the turning-angle score of Tello Ayala et al.\ \cite{telloayala2026tortuosity}, which averaged the angles between consecutive points of 2D angiographic skeletons and divided them by $\pi$; here the centerlines are 3D, every angle spans a fixed 5\,mm chord, and the turning is divided by the length covered, so that $T_s$ retains its unit of rad/mm.
