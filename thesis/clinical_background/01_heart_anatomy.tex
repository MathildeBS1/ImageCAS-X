\section{Heart anatomy}
\label{sec:anatomy}

\subsection{The coronary circulation}

A thesis about the coronary arteries has to start with the heart itself, because the way those
arteries behave --- filling while the heart relaxes, compressed while it contracts --- only makes
sense once the pump they sit on has been described.

The heart drives the circulation that delivers oxygen to every tissue and carries away metabolic
waste \cite{shah2009heart}.
It has four chambers, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:heart-chambers}: two atria, which receive returning
blood, and two ventricles, whose thicker muscular walls pump it onward \cite{weinhaus2024anatomy}.
The right and left sides work as two pumps in series.
The right atrium receives deoxygenated blood returning from the tissues and passes it to the right
ventricle, which drives it through the lungs to be oxygenated.
The left atrium then receives that oxygenated blood and passes it to the left ventricle, which
ejects it into the aorta \cite{weinhaus2024anatomy}.

Blood only moves in that one direction because four valves make it.
The tricuspid and mitral valves, on the right and left respectively, separate each atrium from its
ventricle, while the pulmonary and aortic valves guard the outlet of each ventricle into the artery
it supplies \cite{weinhaus2024anatomy}.

The blood passing through these chambers does not perfuse the heart muscle itself.
The \emph{myocardium}, the muscular layer responsible for contraction, is supplied by the coronary
arteries, which arise from the aorta immediately
above the aortic valve, course over the \emph{epicardial} surface, the outer surface of the heart,
and send penetrating branches inward \cite{devos2024coronary}.
Coronary artery disease is a consequence of this dependence on a dedicated supply, and the arterial
tree that provides it is the structure this thesis measures.

Two properties of that supply explain why a narrowing matters.
First, contraction compresses the penetrating branches, most severely in the left ventricle, so
coronary inflow falls during \emph{systole}, the contractile phase, and rises during
\emph{diastole}, the phase of relaxation; coronary perfusion is predominantly a diastolic event
\cite{duncker2015regulation}.
Second, the myocardium already extracts 60--80\% of the oxygen delivered to it at rest, so a rise in
demand has to be met by a rise in flow rather than by extracting more from the flow already there.
A vessel that cannot deliver it produces \emph{ischemia}, a state in which muscle works without
sufficient oxygen, which is how a narrowing, or \emph{stenosis}, becomes symptomatic
\cite{duncker2015regulation}.
Diastole is also the phase in which the arteries are most nearly stationary, and it is therefore the
phase in which coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) images them
(Section~\ref{sec:ccta}).

\subsection{The coronary arterial tree}
\label{sec:tree}

Along a vessel, \emph{proximal} means near its origin at the aorta and \emph{distal} means far from
it, toward the small branches.
The coronary arteries arise from two of the three bulges in the aortic wall known as the aortic
sinuses \cite{devos2024coronary}.
The opening of each artery is its \emph{ostium}, and each ostium is the root of a tree: the left
coronary ostium opens from the left sinus and the right from the right sinus, which is why the two
systems are described separately here.

The left coronary artery begins as the left main artery (LM), a short trunk that divides into the
left anterior descending artery (LAD) and the left circumflex artery (LCX)
\cite{devos2024coronary}.
The LAD descends the anterior surface of the heart within the anterior interventricular
\emph{sulcus}, the groove between the two ventricles, supplying the anterior wall of both and the
\emph{interventricular septum} dividing them \cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.
It reaches the septum through septal perforating branches and the anterior wall through a variable
number of diagonal branches \cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.
The LCX courses around the left side of the heart within the left atrioventricular sulcus, the
groove separating the left atrium from the left ventricle, supplying the lateral wall through one or
more obtuse marginal branches \cite{devos2024coronary}.

Both sets of branches are numbered by the order in which they leave the parent vessel rather than by
any fixed identity: D1 and D2 are the first and second diagonal branches off the LAD, and OM1
and OM2 the first and second obtuse marginal branches off the LCX \cite{devos2024coronary}.
A label therefore names a position in a sequence, not the same vessel in every heart, and
prevalence is expected to fall with position.

The right coronary artery (RCA) courses within the right atrioventricular sulcus, giving off
branches to the right ventricle along its course
\cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.
It usually terminates on the inferior surface of the heart as the posterior descending artery (PDA),
which supplies the inferior third of the interventricular septum, and one or more posterior lateral
arteries (PLA) \cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.
Those proximal right-sided branches are given no individual name here and are not distinguished
further.

\subsection{Coronary dominance}
\label{sec:dominance}

The two systems differ in which of them reaches the \emph{crux}, the point on the underside of the
heart where the atrioventricular and interventricular sulci meet.
That variation is \emph{dominance}, defined by the origin of the PDA
\cite{allwork1987applied}: a heart is right-dominant when the PDA arises from the RCA,
left-dominant when it arises from the LCX, and codominant when the posterior territory is shared.
Dominance is the largest source of normal topological variation in the coronary tree, and therefore
the variable by which any description of coronary anatomy is stratified.

Approximately 85\% of individuals are right-dominant, 8\% left-dominant and 7\% codominant
\cite{wu2024dominance}.
These proportions vary systematically with ancestry: among patients undergoing CCTA, right dominance
was found in 96\% of East Asian, 87\% of South Asian and 87\% of European individuals
\cite{wu2024dominance}, so a cohort figure is interpretable only alongside the population from
which it was drawn.


\subsection{Ramus intermedius}

When the left main trifurcates rather than bifurcates, the additional vessel arising between
the LAD and the LCX is the ramus intermedius, abbreviated IM
\cite{obrien2007anatomy}.
It supplies myocardial territory that would otherwise be served by a diagonal or an obtuse
marginal branch.

A meta-analysis over 46 cadaveric, angiographic and CCTA studies covering 25,602 hearts places
the aggregate prevalence at 25.5\%; restricted to CCTA cohorts it is 26.5\%, with a study
range of 19\% to 68.8\% \cite{dai2025ramus}.
The wide study range in the pooled estimate reflects both genuine population differences and
the threshold at which an observer records a third branch as a ramus intermedius rather than a
proximal diagonal \cite{dai2025ramus}.
