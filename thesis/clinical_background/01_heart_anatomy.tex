\section{Heart anatomy}
\label{sec:anatomy}

\subsection{The coronary circulation}

The heart drives the circulation that delivers oxygen to every tissue and carries away metabolic
waste \cite{shah2009heart}.
It has four chambers, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:heart-chambers}: the \textbf{right atrium (RA)} and
\textbf{left atrium (LA)}, which receive blood arriving at the heart, and the
\textbf{right ventricle (RV)} and \textbf{left ventricle (LV)}, whose thicker muscular walls pump it
back out \cite{weinhaus2024anatomy}.

The right and left sides work as two pumps in series. Deoxygenated blood returns from the tissues into the RA and passes to the RV, which drives it
through the lungs to take up oxygen.
It comes back to the LA, passes to the LV, and the LV ejects it into the \emph{aorta}, the largest
artery in the body, for distribution to every organ \cite{weinhaus2024anatomy}.
Four valves hold it to that direction: the \emph{tricuspid valve} and \emph{mitral valve} inside
the right and left sides, and the \emph{pulmonary valve} and \emph{aortic valve} at each
ventricle's outlet \cite{weinhaus2024anatomy}.
The path and the valves are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:heart-chambers}. Filling and emptying repeat as a cycle of two phases: \emph{diastole}, in which the muscle relaxes
and the chambers fill, and \emph{systole}, in which it contracts and ejects what it holds
\cite{weinhaus2024anatomy}.

The blood passing through these chambers does not perfuse the heart muscle itself.
The \emph{myocardium}, the muscular layer responsible for contraction, is supplied by the coronary
arteries, which arise from the aorta immediately
above the aortic valve, run across the \emph{epicardium}, the
outer surface of the heart, and send small branches down into the muscle from there
\cite{devos2024coronary}.
Coronary artery disease is a consequence of this dependence on a dedicated supply, and the arterial tree that provides it is the structure this thesis measures.

\subsection{The coronary arterial tree}
\label{sec:tree}

The coronary arteries leave the aorta through two of the three bulges in its wall known as the
aortic sinuses \cite{devos2024coronary}.
The opening through which an artery leaves is its \emph{ostium}, and each ostium roots a tree of its
own: the left artery from the left sinus, the right from the right sinus.
Position along a vessel is given relative to that root: \emph{proximal} means near the ostium,
\emph{distal} far from it.

The left tree begins as the \textbf{left main artery (LM)}, a short vessel, bifurcates into the
\textbf{left anterior descending artery (LAD)} and the \textbf{left circumflex artery (LCX)}.
The LAD runs down the front of the heart in the anterior
interventricular groove, supplying the front wall of both ventricles and the
\emph{interventricular septum}, the wall dividing them \cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.
The LCX turns the other way along the left atrioventricular
groove, supplying the side wall of the left ventricle \cite{obrien2007anatomy,allwork1987applied}.
Both are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:coronary-arteries}.

Each reaches the muscle through side branches.
The LAD sends septal perforators into the septum and diagonal branches across the front wall, the
first two being the \textbf{first diagonal branch (D1)} and the \textbf{second diagonal branch (D2)}
\cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.
The LCX sends obtuse marginal branches over the side wall, the first two being the
\textbf{first obtuse marginal branch (OM1)} and the \textbf{second obtuse marginal branch (OM2)}
\cite{obrien2007anatomy}.
Both series are numbered by order of origin, so a label names a position rather than the same vessel
in every heart, later positions are occupied in fewer hearts, and branches past the second are not
named individually \cite{devos2024coronary}.

The left main sometimes divides into three rather than two.
The extra vessel, arising between the LAD and the LCX, is the \textbf{ramus intermedius (IM)}, and
it supplies muscle that a diagonal or an obtuse marginal branch would otherwise reach
\cite{obrien2007anatomy}.

The right tree begins with the \textbf{right coronary artery (RCA)} \cite{obrien2007anatomy}.
It leaves the right ostium and follows the right atrioventricular groove around the other side of
the heart, on the way supplying the RV through acute marginal branches and the
heartbeat-conducting tissue through small nodal arteries; none of these proximal branches is named
individually \cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.

Underneath the heart the RCA usually ends as two further sets of branches: the
\textbf{posterior descending artery (PDA)}, which runs forward in the posterior interventricular
groove and supplies the lower third of the septum, and the \textbf{posterior lateral arteries (PLA)},
which supply the underside of the left ventricle \cite{devos2024coronary,obrien2007anatomy}.
Either tree can give rise to them, so each carries the prefix of its parent.
From the RCA they are the \textbf{right posterior descending artery (R-PDA)} and the
\textbf{right posterior lateral arteries (R-PLA)}; from the LCX, the
\textbf{left posterior descending artery (L-PDA)} and the
\textbf{left posterior lateral arteries (L-PLA)} \cite{allwork1987applied}.

\subsection{Coronary dominance}
\label{sec:dominance}

The largest source of normal variation in the shape of the coronary tree is which of the two systems
reaches the underside of the heart.
The \emph{crux} is the point on that underside where the atrioventricular and interventricular
grooves meet \cite{allwork1987applied}.
\textbf{Dominance} names the system that crosses it, and is defined by where the PDA arises
\cite{allwork1987applied}.
A heart is right-dominant when the PDA comes from the RCA and left-dominant when it comes from the
LCX; it is codominant when the PDA comes from the RCA but the PLA from the LCX
\cite{devos2024coronary,allwork1987applied}.

Dominance changes which arteries a tree contains rather than how large they are, since the posterior
vessels follow the dominant system.
Any feature that counts branches or endpoints therefore compares like with like only within a
dominance group, and every population statistic in this thesis is stratified accordingly
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset-dominance}).
