\section{Anatomical variants}
\label{sec:variants}

A method that characterizes coronary topology must represent these variants rather than discard
them, because each changes how the tree branches rather than whether it is diseased.

In most hearts the left artery leaves the aorta as a single trunk, the LM (Section~\ref{sec:tree}),
before splitting into the LAD and the LCX.
In the \emph{absent left main} variant that split happens at the aorta itself: the LAD and LCX
arise from two separate openings in the left sinus instead of from one common trunk, and no LM
exists to label.
Topaz et al.\ found it in 83 of 20{,}332 consecutive patients undergoing cardiac catheterization, or
0.4\%, and reported two things that travel with it: left coronary dominance is more common in these
hearts, and myocardial bridging, a length of artery that dips under the muscle instead of running
over it, occurs in 6\% of them against an otherwise-typical 0.5--1.5\% \cite{topaz1991absentlm}.
It is a variant of connectivity, not of caliber, and it means a method that assumes one root per
side will either merge two independent trees into one or drop the smaller of them; the graph this
thesis builds instead roots each side at however many ostia it actually has
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset-dominance}).

The ramus intermedius (IM), already introduced as an extra vessel some left main arteries send
between the LAD and the LCX (Section~\ref{sec:tree}), is common enough that its presence or absence
is itself a source of normal variation rather than an anomaly.
A meta-analysis of the published coronary computed tomography angiography literature puts its
pooled prevalence at 26.5\% \cite{dai2025ramus}.
Because it changes how many branches a left tree carries without changing whether the tree is
diseased, any feature that counts branches has to treat its presence as a variable to condition on,
the same way dominance already is (Section~\ref{sec:dominance}).
