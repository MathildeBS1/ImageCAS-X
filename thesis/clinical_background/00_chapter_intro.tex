\chapter{Clinical Background}
\label{ch:clinical}

The coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself, and the shape of the tree they form is what
this thesis sets out to measure.
Four things have to be in place before such a measurement means anything.
The first is what a normal coronary tree looks like, and the second is how much it varies between
healthy people.
The third is what computed tomography can and cannot resolve of it.
The fourth is why the shape of the tree should have any bearing on disease at all, and this chapter
only starts that argument: Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} is where it is carried all the way to a clinical
quantity.

The chapter follows that order.
Sections~\ref{sec:anatomy} and~\ref{sec:variants} describe the normal arterial tree, coronary
dominance, and the variants that change how the tree is connected.
Sections~\ref{sec:cad} and~\ref{sec:ccta} cover the disease and the imaging used to see it.
Section~\ref{sec:hemodynamics} covers wall shear stress and fractional flow reserve, the two
measurements that describe what flowing blood does to a vessel; how that mechanism links a vessel's
shape to where disease forms in it is Chapter~\ref{ch:sota}'s question.

No cardiology background is assumed.
Every term is defined where it first appears, set in bold there with its abbreviation in
parentheses, and every abbreviation used in this thesis is collected in the front matter.
