\section{Coronary artery disease}
\label{sec:cad}

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for
approximately 20.5 million deaths in 2021 \cite{lindstrom2022global}.
Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the largest component of that total.

CAD is the manifestation of atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries, and atherosclerosis is a
slow process rather than an event.
It begins with injury to the \emph{endothelium}, the single cell layer lining the arterial lumen,
which allows circulating lipid to enter the vessel wall and be retained there
\cite{libby2019atherosclerosis}.
Inflammatory cells are recruited to the site and take up that lipid, forming the fatty deposit that
is the earliest recognizable lesion \cite{libby2019atherosclerosis}.
Over years the lesion grows: smooth muscle cells migrate across it to build a fibrous cap, and
calcium is deposited within the body of the plaque
\cite{libby2019atherosclerosis,rampidis2022geometry}.
The plaque that results may encroach on the lumen and restrict flow.

A plaque threatens the myocardium by two distinct routes.
The first is gradual.
The plaque grows until the vessel can no longer raise flow to meet demand, and the muscle it
supplies becomes ischemic whenever that demand rises --- the mechanism set out in
Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}.
The second is abrupt.
The plaque ruptures, exposing thrombogenic material to the bloodstream; a clot, or \emph{thrombus},
forms on the exposed surface and occludes the vessel \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
The myocardium beyond the occlusion is cut off from oxygen and dies, which is a \emph{myocardial
infarction}, or heart attack.

Because the acute event is usually a rupture rather than the gradual closure of a lumen
\cite{rampidis2022geometry}, the character of a plaque matters as much as its size.
Calcified, non-calcified and mixed plaques differ in their risk of rupture, and the non-calcified
plaques that are least conspicuous on imaging are the more dangerous \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.

A separate distinction runs between anatomical stenosis and functional ischemia: a narrowing that
appears severe on an image does not necessarily restrict flow sufficiently to render the myocardium
ischemic, which is why the diagnostic pathway distinguishes anatomical from functional testing
\cite{knuuti2019esc}; the functional measurement is defined in Section~\ref{sec:ffr}.

Compounding both, CAD can progress through long, clinically silent periods even as the underlying
atherosclerosis advances \cite{knuuti2019esc}.
A patient may therefore reach a substantial plaque burden without ever having had a symptom to
prompt investigation, which is why the diagnostic pathway begins with a non-invasive anatomical test
rather than an invasive one, reserving invasive coronary angiography for patients in whom clinical
likelihood or symptoms already justify it \cite{knuuti2019esc}; Section~\ref{sec:ccta} treats the
non-invasive modality used for that first step.

Taken together, these are the reasons the location and character of plaque, rather than the degree
of narrowing alone, predict clinical events, and it is by this route that vessel geometry becomes
prognostically relevant (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}).
