\section{Coronary artery disease}
\label{sec:cad}

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for
approximately 20.5 million deaths in 2021 \cite{lindstrom2022global}.
Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the largest component of that total.

CAD is the manifestation of atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries.
Atherosclerosis begins with injury to the endothelium, the single cell layer lining the
arterial lumen, followed by accumulation of lipid, inflammatory cells and eventually calcium
within the vessel wall \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
The resulting plaque may narrow the lumen and restrict flow.

Two distinctions matter for what follows.
The first is between plaque composition types, since calcified, non-calcified and mixed
plaques differ in their risk of rupture, and the non-calcified plaques that are hardest to
see are the more dangerous \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
The second is between anatomical stenosis and functional ischemia: a narrowing that appears
severe on an image does not necessarily reduce flow enough to starve the myocardium, which is
why the diagnostic pathway distinguishes anatomical from functional testing
\cite{knuuti2019esc}.

The acute event that ends this process is usually not the gradual closure of a lumen but the
rupture of a plaque, exposing thrombogenic material and forming a clot that occludes the
vessel abruptly \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
The myocardium distal to the occlusion is deprived of oxygen and infarcts.
This is why the location and character of plaque, rather than the degree of narrowing alone,
predict clinical events, and it is the opening through which vessel geometry becomes
prognostically relevant (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}).
