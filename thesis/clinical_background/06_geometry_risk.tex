\section{Geometry as a risk factor}
\label{sec:geometry}

The shape of the coronary tree is a clinical quantity, not just a description of it.
Shape sets the drag on the wall, the drag decides how the lining behaves, and the lining is what
produces plaque (Section~\ref{sec:hemodynamics}) \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
What makes a particular measurement worth taking is evidence that the chain holds in patients, and
three measurements have it.

\subsection{Evidence from patients}

Han et al.\ looked at patients who went on to have a heart attack and asked what about the culprit
lesion predicted it, beyond how narrow the vessel was and what the plaque was made of
\cite{han2022plaque}.
Three things did: the lesion sitting closer to the ostium, the lesion sitting at a bifurcation, and
higher tortuosity.
None of the three is a property of the plaque; all three describe the tree it grew in, which is
why the shape of the tree is worth studying on its own.

Stone et al.\ then showed the shape comes first.
They measured shear inside the arteries of 506 patients and reimaged 374 of them six to ten months
later \cite{stone2012prediction}.
Low shear at baseline predicted which segments went on to lose lumen area.
Two limits matter: it did not predict the primary endpoint, growth in plaque area, which went with
how much plaque was already there; and every patient had already had a heart attack and been
stented, so this does not transfer unchanged to people who are still well.
Even so, it is the closest the literature comes to what this thesis proposes --- measure the shape
first, then see where disease appears.

\subsection{Bifurcation angle}

The left main bifurcation angle has the firmest evidence of the three.
Juan et al.\ measured it on CCTA in 313 patients, sorted by how much disease they had, and the
angle widened as the disease grew \cite{juan2017bifurcation}.
It was $75.5^\circ \pm 14.7^\circ$ in the 40 patients with clean arteries and no calcium at all,
$81.2^\circ \pm 20.1^\circ$ in the 62 whose plaque did not block the vessel, and
$87.3^\circ \pm 18.8^\circ$ in the 211 with a stenosis of at least half the vessel.
Wang et al.\ supplied the reason.
They took the centerlines of 76 left main arteries, sorted them into four shapes, and simulated the
blood flow through each \cite{wang2024lmphenotypes}.
Shear was lowest around the branch points of the LAD, and lowest of all in the wide-angled shape.
A gradient in patients and a mechanism in simulation pointing the same way is what makes this
angle worth measuring in its own right.
Studies disagree on the size of the effect and on where to put a cut-off, so this thesis takes the
direction of the association and not a threshold.

Part of that disagreement is that \emph{bifurcation angle} is not one measurement.
Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation-angle} shows two conventions in use: the angle between the side branch
and the continuation of the parent vessel, and the angle between the side branch and the parent's
onward course.
They agree on a straight vessel and diverge on a curved one \cite{murasato2022bifurcation}.
Any angle extracted here therefore has to name which convention it follows before it can be compared
with a published value.

\subsection{Tortuosity}

Tortuosity is the second.
Zebi\'c Mihi\'c et al.\ studied 131 patients being investigated for suspected coronary artery
disease, counting a vessel as severely tortuous if it had three or more consecutive bends of at
least $45^\circ$ \cite{zebicmihic2023tortuosity}.
It was the factor most strongly associated with non-obstructive disease, at roughly eight times
the odds --- an odds ratio of 7.96, 95\% confidence interval 1.79 to 35.40.
That interval is very wide on 131 patients, so the effect is clearly there but its size is not,
making this a reason to measure tortuosity rather than a number to reproduce.

\subsection{Dominance}

Dominance is the third.
Veltman et al.\ followed 1425 patients after CCTA for a median of two years, recording heart
attacks and deaths from any cause \cite{veltman2012dominance}.
Left-dominant patients reached one of those about three times as often as right-dominant ones once
other risk factors were accounted for, a hazard ratio of 3.20 with a 95\% confidence interval of
1.67 to 6.13.
The effect appeared only in patients who already had significant disease, among whom three-year
event rates were 35\% in left-dominant and 9.5\% in right-dominant circulations; without
significant disease the two groups did not differ \cite{veltman2012dominance}.
Dominance therefore changes how dangerous existing disease is rather than causing it, so this
thesis stratifies the other features by it rather than reporting it alone.

Curvature and tortuosity both change with the heartbeat, so the phase an image is taken in decides
what they measure \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
Every image here is reconstructed in mid-diastole (Section~\ref{sec:ccta}), which fixes that phase.
The values in this thesis are therefore comparable to each other, but not to values measured in
systole.

\subsection{Branching pattern}

Angle, tortuosity and dominance each carry outcome evidence of the kind quoted above.
Descriptors of branching pattern --- how many branches a tree carries, and how they are arranged
--- do not, to anything like the same degree.
That gap is part of why describing their distribution across a large cohort is worth doing at all.
