\section{Coronary hemodynamics}
\label{sec:hemodynamics}

Shape cannot injure a vessel by itself.
It acts through the forces blood exerts on the wall as it flows past, and those forces are the
middle link in the chain running from the geometry of the tree to the disease that forms in it.
Two quantities describe them, at different scales.
\emph{Wall shear stress} (WSS) is a local mechanical stimulus on the cells lining the vessel,
varying from point to point within a single artery.
\emph{Fractional flow reserve} (FFR) is a lesion-level measurement of whether one narrowing limits
flow.
The first explains where disease begins, the second how an established narrowing is treated.

\subsection{Wall shear stress}

Blood flowing along an artery drags on the wall it passes, much as wind drags on a surface it blows
across.
WSS is that drag: the tangential frictional force per unit area exerted by flowing blood on the
endothelium, measured in pascals (Pa).
For steady laminar flow of a fluid of viscosity $\mu$ at flow rate $Q$ through a tube of radius
$R$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \tau = \frac{4 \mu Q}{\pi R^{3}},
  \label{eq:wss}
\end{equation}
%
so shear falls with the cube of the radius: at a fixed flow rate, halving a vessel's radius raises
the drag on its wall eightfold.
Radius and the branching pattern that distributes $Q$ between vessels therefore fix the shear the
wall experiences, which makes the geometry of the tree a hemodynamic quantity and not a merely
descriptive one.
The endothelium senses that shear rather than passively enduring it, and responds to disturbed
shear by switching into a pro-inflammatory state \cite{rampidis2022geometry}.

Early atherosclerotic lesions develop preferentially where shear is low or oscillatory
\cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
Shear is \emph{oscillatory} where flow reverses over the cardiac cycle, dragging the wall
alternately forward and backward rather than steadily in one direction.
The oscillatory shear index is the standard way of summarizing how much reversal a given point on
the wall experiences.

Low and oscillatory shear both arise from vessel geometry and are therefore predictable from an
image: flow separates at a bifurcation, leaving a low-shear region on the lateral walls away from
the flow divider, and curvature drives secondary flows that leave one on the inner wall of a bend
\cite{rampidis2022geometry}.
Bifurcation angle and tortuosity are consequently not arbitrary descriptors of morphology: each
governs the size and position of a region in which lesions are expected to form.

Equation~\eqref{eq:wss} leaves one question open, and the answer decides what counts as a normal
tree.
Radius falls at every branching, and shear depends on radius, so does shear rise, fall or hold
level as blood works its way out toward the small vessels?

The textbook answer is that it holds level.
Murray's law, derived by minimizing the power dissipated in driving the flow plus the metabolic
cost of maintaining the blood itself, gives $Q \propto R^{3}$.
Substituted into Equation~\eqref{eq:wss}, this makes $\tau$ constant everywhere: as vessels narrow,
the flow they carry drops by exactly the amount needed to leave the drag on the wall unchanged.
On this account a healthy arterial tree holds every endothelial cell under the same mechanical load.

The coronary circulation does not follow the cubic law.
A systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 1070 coronary trees places the flow--diameter
exponent at $k = 2.39$, with a 95\% confidence interval of 2.24 to 2.54
\cite{taylor2024murray}.
Since $\tau \propto Q / R^{3} \propto R^{\,k-3}$, an exponent below three gives
$\tau \propto R^{-0.61}$: in a normal coronary tree wall shear stress \emph{rises} toward the
smaller distal vessels rather than holding level.
Uniform shear is therefore the wrong baseline for judging a coronary tree, and any feature defined
as deviation from Murray's law must be referenced to the coronary exponent, not the cubic one.

\subsection{Fractional flow reserve}
\label{sec:ffr}

How narrow a vessel appears and how much it restricts flow are different questions, which is why the
diagnostic pathway separates anatomical from functional testing \cite{knuuti2019esc}.
FFR addresses the functional one: it is the ratio of mean blood pressure just downstream of a lesion
to mean pressure upstream of it, obtained with a sensor-tipped guidewire passed across the
stenosis during catheterization.

The measurement is made under pharmacologically induced maximal \emph{hyperemia}, a state of
greatly increased blood flow, because at rest the narrowing would be masked by autoregulation.
The small vessels downstream of a stenosis dilate to compensate for it, holding resting flow nearly
constant however tight the stenosis becomes, so a pressure gradient measured at rest understates
the lesion \cite{duncker2015regulation}.
Only once that downstream resistance has been driven to its minimum and held there does the ratio
of pressures track the ratio of flows.
An FFR of 0.80, the threshold below which a lesion is treated as functionally significant
\cite{knuuti2019esc}, then means a vessel admitting 80\% of the flow it would carry were it not
narrowed.

FFR requires a pressure wire within the artery, a measurement no CT scan can supply.
Wall shear stress, which follows from vessel geometry, is therefore the hemodynamic quantity
accessible from imaging.
